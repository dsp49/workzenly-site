This page is for US small-business owners, managers and HR leads who need a clear, fair review process without buying software first. A performance review is the conversation, and the written record of it, about how an employee did against their goals and the job’s standards, and what comes next. Below are 7 templates, each for a different part of the cycle: an annual performance review form, a one-page employee review template, a goal scorecard in Excel, quarterly check-ins, a competency rubric, a 360-degree feedback summary and a performance improvement plan. How to run a performance review with these templates

A good performance review form helps, but the conversation does the work. This process fits a small business with no HR department.

  1. Set goals at the start of the period. Three to six goals, each with a number or a clear finish line. Write them on the annual review or the goal sheet so you review against them later.
  2. Check in every quarter. Use the quarterly template. Problems found in April can be fixed by December.
  3. Collect evidence a week before. Goal results, attendance and late clock-ins, customer comments, earlier check-in notes and any 360 feedback.
  4. Ask for a self-review. Our self performance review examples help employees write specific answers.
  5. Draft ratings with evidence. Every rating gets an example. Calibrate with other managers if several people rate the same role.
  6. Meet for 30 to 60 minutes. Results first, then ratings, then next year’s goals and development. Let the employee speak at least half the time.
  7. Sign, share and file. Give a copy to the employee, put the original in the personnel file, and set the first quarterly check-in.

US rules to know about performance reviews

These are general principles checked against federal and state agency sources. They are not legal advice; laws vary by state, so check with your state labor department.

  • Reviews and raises are not required by federal law. No federal law requires private employers to review employees, and the Department of Labor says the FLSA does not require pay raises above the minimum wage. Raises are a matter of agreement between employer and employee.
  • Anti-discrimination laws apply to reviews. The laws the EEOC enforces cover all terms of employment, and EEOC guidance points out that evaluations often decide pay, promotion and termination. Use the same job-related criteria for everyone in a role, and do not count legally protected leave, such as FMLA leave, against attendance. Our FMLA leave guide explains who qualifies.
  • Keep the records. EEOC regulations require personnel and employment records to be kept for one year, and one year from the date of an involuntary termination. California requires personnel records to be kept at least three years after employment ends.
  • Employees may have a right to see reviews. In California, current and former employees can inspect and copy records relating to their performance, and the employer must respond within 30 calendar days of a written request. Other states set their own rules.
  • Montana requires good cause after probation. Montana’s labor department says the state is not at-will once the probationary period ends, so documented reviews and PIPs matter more there.

Frequently asked questions

What should a performance review template include?

Employee and reviewer details and the review period; results against last period’s goals; ratings on core responsibilities and competencies with a defined scale and evidence; an overall rating; goals for the next period with measures and dates; a development plan; comment boxes for both sides; and signatures confirming the review was discussed.

How often should performance reviews happen?

No federal law sets a schedule, so it is your choice. A common pattern for small businesses is one annual review that covers pay and goals, short quarterly check-ins in between, and a 90-day review for new hires. Hourly teams with high turnover often do better with six-month reviews than with annual ones.

Are performance reviews required by law?

No. Federal law does not require private employers to hold performance reviews, and the Department of Labor notes that the FLSA does not require pay raises. If you do hold them, anti-discrimination laws enforced by the EEOC apply, so use the same job-related criteria for everyone in a role and keep the records.

What is a performance improvement plan?

A PIP is a written plan, usually 30 to 90 days long, that states a specific performance gap, measurable goals, the support the employer will give and dates for check-in meetings. It gives the employee a fair chance to improve and gives the employer a clear record if the problem continues and further action is needed.

Should performance reviews be tied to raises?

Many employers link them, because a documented rating makes pay decisions easier to explain. Some hold the pay conversation a few weeks after the review so the discussion stays on performance. Either way, use the same scale and budget rules for everyone in a role, and check that similar ratings lead to similar raises.

How long should we keep performance reviews?

EEOC rules require private employers to keep personnel records for at least one year, and one year from the date of an involuntary termination. Some states require longer; California requires personnel records to be kept at least three years after employment ends. Keep reviews in the personnel file and check your state’s rule.
