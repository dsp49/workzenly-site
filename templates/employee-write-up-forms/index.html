How to write up an employee

A good write-up reads the same to the employee, a judge and an unemployment examiner. Use any employee write up template above and follow these rules.

  • Write facts, not opinions. “Clocked in at 8:26 a.m. for an 8:00 a.m. shift” instead of “always late.”
  • Name the rule. Quote the policy or handbook section, and note when the employee received it.
  • Include the history. Earlier coaching or warnings on the same issue, with dates.
  • Say what must change and by when. One or two specific, measurable expectations.
  • State the next step. Final warning, suspension or termination if it continues.
  • Leave room for the employee. A comments box or a separate response form.
  • Write it soon. The same day or the next, while records and memories are fresh.

Leave out anything about age, health, disability, pregnancy, religion, race, national origin or family, and never mention that the employee took leave or made a complaint.

How to hold the write-up meeting

  1. Check the facts and protected leave first. Pull the time clock, schedule, POS or safety records, and remove any absence protected by law.
  2. Check consistency. Look at the log for how you handled the same issue for others.
  3. Meet privately, with a second manager for anything at the written warning level or above.
  4. Walk through the form. Facts, rule, action, what must change, next step. Then ask for the employee’s side and write it down.
  5. Ask for a signature that confirms receipt. If they decline, note it and have the second manager sign.
  6. Give a copy, file the original, log it, and put the follow-up date in your calendar.

Repeated write-ups for the same issue usually lead to a termination letter. Before that step, read our guides to no call, no show and attendance policies, and make sure your employee handbook describes the discipline steps you actually follow.

US rules to know before you use a disciplinary form

These are general principles, checked against federal agency sources. State and local laws add more, so check your state labor department or an employment lawyer.

  • No federal law requires write-ups. In most states employment is at will, and a write-up is a policy tool. If your handbook promises specific steps, follow them or say you may skip steps for serious issues.
  • Protected leave stays out. The Department of Labor says employers may not count FMLA leave under no-fault attendance policies or use it as a negative factor in discipline. Many state and city paid sick leave laws protect covered sick days too.
  • No retaliation. The EEOC lists a reprimand given because someone complained about discrimination as a possible form of retaliation. OSHA prohibits discipline for reporting an injury or a safety concern.
  • Pay and working conditions talk is protected. The NLRB says most private-sector employees, with or without a union, can discuss pay and working conditions together, and employers cannot discipline them for it. Union-represented employees can ask for a representative in an investigatory interview.
  • Personnel file access. Some states let employees see their file. California, for example, requires employers to allow inspection within 30 days of a written request and to provide copies of documents the employee signed.
  • Record retention. The EEOC requires covered employers to keep personnel records for one year, and one year after an involuntary termination.

Frequently asked questions

What is an employee write-up?

A write-up is a written record of a workplace problem and the action a manager took, such as a documented verbal warning, a written warning or a suspension. It records the facts, the rule involved, what must change and what happens next, and the employee signs to confirm they received it.

What should a write-up form include?

The employee’s name and job, the date of the incident and of the write-up, the type of issue, a factual description, the policy involved, the action taken, what must change, the consequence if it happens again, a place for the employee’s comments, and signatures confirming receipt rather than agreement.

Can an employee refuse to sign a write-up?

Yes, and the write-up is still valid. Explain that signing only confirms receipt. If they still decline, write “Employee declined to sign” with the date and time, have a second manager or witness sign, and give the employee a copy. Note any comments they make in the comments box.

Is a write-up the same as a warning?

Mostly. People use the words interchangeably. Strictly, the write-up is the document, and the warning is one possible action recorded on it. A write-up form can also record coaching, a suspension, a performance improvement plan or a termination, which is why the templates include an action section.

Can I write up an employee for reporting an injury or a safety problem?

No. OSHA says employers may not retaliate against employees for reporting a work-related injury or raising a safety concern, and its recordkeeping rule bars disciplining employees simply because they report a work-related injury or illness. You can still write up the unsafe act itself, applied the same way to everyone.

How long should I keep employee write-ups?

The EEOC requires covered employers to keep personnel records for at least one year, and for one year after an involuntary termination. Keep them longer if a charge, lawsuit or unemployment claim is pending, and check your state. Many employers keep write-ups for the length of employment plus several years.
