How to use these evaluation forms

  1. Pick one form per job type. Use the same form for everyone in the same role. Changing forms from person to person makes ratings hard to compare and hard to defend.
  2. Set the schedule. Once a year is common, with a short check-in at six months. New hires get the 90-day form. Put the dates on the calendar for the year.
  3. Gather records first. Pull attendance, late clock-ins, sales or production numbers, customer comments and any earlier write-ups for the period. If your timesheets live in a spreadsheet, our attendance tracker templates make the counts easy to find.
  4. Ask for a self-evaluation. Give the employee a week to complete it.
  5. Fill in the form before the meeting. Write an example for every rating, especially the 1s, 2s and 5s.
  6. Meet in private for 30 to 45 minutes. Start with what went well, go through each factor, agree on two or three goals, and let the employee comment.
  7. Sign, copy and file. Give the employee a copy and keep the original in the personnel file.

How to write fair, useful ratings

A form is only as good as the ratings on it. These habits make evaluations more accurate and easier to stand behind.

  • Rate the whole period. The last two weeks are easy to remember. Keep brief notes through the year, or check records, so a strong spring is not erased by one bad October shift.
  • Use examples, not adjectives. “Three clock-ins 5 to 10 minutes late in July” is useful. “Not punctual” is not.
  • Rate the job, not the person. Every factor should relate to the work. Leave out personality, family situation, age, health and anything else unrelated to performance.
  • Watch for the middle and the extremes. If every employee gets a 3, the form tells you nothing. If one manager gives all 5s, calibrate with other managers before ratings affect pay.
  • Separate protected leave from attendance. Do not count absences that are protected by law, such as FMLA leave or state paid sick leave, against an attendance rating. Our FMLA leave guide explains which absences are protected.
  • No surprises. If a 2 is coming, the employee should already have heard about the problem during the year.

US rules to know about employee evaluations

These are general principles checked against federal and state agency sources. They are not legal advice, and state law varies, so check with your state labor department.

  • Evaluations are not required by federal law. No federal law requires private employers to evaluate employees, and the Department of Labor notes that the FLSA does not require pay raises. They are a management tool, which is why consistency matters more than the format.
  • Anti-discrimination law covers evaluations. Title VII and other laws enforced by the EEOC apply to all terms of employment, and EEOC guidance notes that evaluations often drive pay, promotion and termination decisions. Use the same job-related factors for everyone in a role, and check ratings for patterns by group.
  • Keep the records. EEOC rules require employers to keep personnel and employment records for one year, and one year from the date of an involuntary termination. Some states are longer: California requires personnel records to be kept for at least three years after employment ends.
  • Some states let employees see their file. In California, current and former employees can inspect and copy records relating to their performance, and the employer must respond to a written request within 30 calendar days. Other states have their own rules, and some have none for private employers.
  • Montana is different. Montana is not an at-will state after the probationary period. Once an employee completes it, the employer generally needs good cause to discharge, so evaluations there carry extra weight.

Frequently asked questions

What should an employee evaluation form include?

The employee’s name, job title, evaluator and review period; a short list of job-related factors with a defined rating scale; space for specific examples; an overall rating; strengths, areas to improve and goals for the next period; an employee comment box; and signatures that confirm the review took place.

Are employers required to do employee evaluations?

No federal law requires private employers to evaluate employees, and the FLSA does not require pay raises. Many employers still do them because written evaluations support pay, promotion and discipline decisions. If you do evaluate, apply the same process to everyone, because evaluations are covered by federal anti-discrimination laws such as Title VII.

How long should employers keep evaluation forms?

EEOC rules require private employers to keep personnel records for at least one year, and one year from the date of an involuntary termination. Some states require longer. California, for example, requires employers to keep personnel records for at least three years after employment ends. Many employers keep evaluations for the length of employment plus several years.

Does an employee have to sign an evaluation?

Asking for a signature is a good practice, not a federal requirement. Word the signature line so it confirms receipt and discussion, not agreement, and give the employee space to comment. If an employee refuses to sign, write “employee declined to sign,” date it, and have a second manager initial it.

What is the difference between an evaluation form and a performance review?

People use the terms interchangeably. Usually the evaluation form is the document that records ratings and comments, while the performance review is the whole process: the self-evaluation, the meeting, the goals and any pay decision. A short evaluation form works well for hourly teams; longer annual reviews add goal results and a development plan.

Can employees see their evaluation forms?

It depends on the state. There is no general federal right for private-sector employees to see their personnel files, but several states grant one. In California, current and former employees can inspect records relating to their performance, and employers must respond to a written request within 30 calendar days. Check your state labor department.
