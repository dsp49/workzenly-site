California’s Labor Commissioner says overtime is due after 8 hours in a workday or 40 in a workweek, double time after 12 hours in a workday, and on the seventh consecutive day of work in a workweek the first 8 hours are overtime and the rest are double time. How to use these timesheet templates

  1. Pick the sheet that matches your pay period. Weekly, biweekly or semimonthly, plus the California version for anyone working in that state. Use the same template for everyone in a pay group.
  2. Set the inputs once. The yellow cells hold the week or period start date, the hourly rate and the overtime threshold. Green cells are formulas; leave them alone. Set the start date to the first day of your workweek, which is a fixed, recurring 168-hour period you choose and keep.
  3. Record times daily. Ask employees to write or type in and out times as they happen. A timesheet filled in on Friday from memory is how unpaid minutes and disputes start.
  4. Approve before payroll. The supervisor checks the hours against the schedule, signs, and sends it to whoever runs payroll. Fix errors with the employee, not by quietly changing the sheet.
  5. Pay and file. Enter regular and overtime hours in payroll, then keep the signed timesheet with the payroll record. Our guide on how to process payroll covers the steps after this one.

Every free timesheet template here opens in Excel, Google Sheets or LibreOffice. To use Google Sheets, upload the .xlsx to Drive and open it; the formulas keep working. Spreadsheets hold up for one location and a handful of people. Once you have more than a few people clocking in at different sites, the weak points are late submissions, retyped hours and lost sheets, which is when teams compare free time clock apps.

US timekeeping rules to know

These principles are checked against Department of Labor fact sheets and the California Labor Commissioner. State and city rules vary, so check your state labor department too.

  • Any format is fine if it is accurate. The DOL says employers may use any timekeeping method they choose, as long as it is complete and accurate. For non-exempt employees, the records must include hours worked each day and the total for each workweek, along with pay details such as the regular rate and overtime earnings.
  • Keep timesheets at least two years. Under the FLSA, time cards and the records used to calculate wages are kept for two years, and payroll records for three. Many employers keep everything for three to make it simple. California requires payroll records showing daily hours to be kept for three years.
  • Overtime is counted per workweek. Non-exempt employees earn 1.5 times their regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek. The DOL says averaging hours over two or more weeks is not permitted, which is why the biweekly and semimonthly templates total each workweek separately. Learn more about how to calculate overtime with bonuses and different rates.
  • Short breaks are paid, meal breaks may not be. The DOL says rest periods of short duration, usually 20 minutes or less, count as hours worked. A meal period is unpaid only if the employee is completely relieved from duty. Eating at the desk while answering phones is work time.
  • Rounding must even out. Federal rules accept rounding start and stop times to the nearest 5 minutes, tenth of an hour or quarter hour, but only if over time it does not shortchange employees. Rounding that always favors the employer is not allowed, and some states restrict it further.
  • Some states add daily overtime. California pays overtime after 8 hours in a day and double time after 12, as template 3 shows. Check your state’s rules before you rely on a weekly-only sheet.

Exempt employees do not need hour-by-hour timesheets for overtime, but a free timesheet template is still a simple way to track their leave. Not sure who is exempt? Read exempt vs non-exempt first, and see what is a timesheet for the basics.

Frequently asked questions

What should a timesheet include?

The employee’s name, the workweek or pay period dates, the hours worked each day and the total for each workweek. For hourly employees, record start and end times and unpaid meal breaks, then split regular and overtime hours. Add paid leave hours by type, and signatures from the employee and the supervisor who approves it.

Do employers have to use timesheets?

Employers must keep accurate records of hours worked by non-exempt employees, but the Department of Labor says they may use any timekeeping method they choose. A paper time card, a spreadsheet or a time clock app all work if the record is complete and accurate. Some states add their own requirements, such as recording meal periods.

How long should I keep employee timesheets?

Under the FLSA, keep time cards and the records behind wage calculations for at least two years, and payroll records for at least three years. Many employers simply keep timesheets for three years with payroll. Several states require longer periods, so check your state labor department before you throw anything away.

How is overtime calculated on a biweekly timesheet?

Separately for each workweek. Federal law does not allow averaging hours over two or more weeks, so 44 hours in week one and 36 in week two means 4 overtime hours, even though the total is 80. Our biweekly timesheet template has a subtotal and overtime line for each week for exactly this reason.

Can I round employee time on a timesheet?

Federal rules accept rounding start and stop times to the nearest 5 minutes, tenth of an hour or quarter hour, as long as it averages out so employees are paid for all the time they actually work. Rounding that always favors the employer is not allowed. Some states are stricter, so some employers simply pay to the minute.

Do these timesheet templates work in Google Sheets?

Yes. Upload the .xlsx file to Google Drive and open it with Google Sheets; the formulas for hours, overtime and pay keep working. The Word time card opens in Google Docs. Each template also has a printable PDF for teams that fill in timesheets by hand.
