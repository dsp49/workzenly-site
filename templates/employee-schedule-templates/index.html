Once a schedule is posted, every change has a cost in goodwill, and in some places in pay. Oregon’s law, for example, covers retail, hospitality and food service employers with 500 or more employees worldwide; when such an employer changes a posted schedule without the required notice, it can owe one extra hour of pay for adding more than 30 minutes or moving a shift, and half pay for scheduled hours it cuts. Changes an employee asks for in writing are generally not subject to the notice rule. The log records who asked and when, so the premium column is easy to fill in. How to schedule employees with these templates

  1. Collect availability first. Hand out the availability form at hiring and when anything changes. Keep the completed forms beside the schedule.
  2. Set your minimums. Decide how many people each shift, section or role needs. Write them down; they are the yellow cells in the clinic and retail templates.
  3. Enter fixed time off and limits. Approved PTO, school hours for minors, standing days off.
  4. Fill shifts and watch hours. Check each person’s weekly total against 40 hours and against what they asked for.
  5. Check coverage, then post. Every coverage row should meet your minimum. Post with the date, one or two weeks ahead, or more where local law requires it.
  6. Log changes and compare with actual hours. Record changes in the log and compare the schedule with timesheets before payroll.

US rules to know when you schedule employees

General principles, checked on federal and state agency sites. States and cities add their own rules, so confirm with your state labor department.

  • Overtime is weekly. Non-exempt employees must get at least 1.5 times their regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek. The workweek is a fixed, recurring seven-day period, and hours cannot be averaged over two weeks, even on biweekly payroll.
  • Minors have federal hour limits. 14- and 15-year-olds may work up to 3 hours on a school day and 18 in a school week, 8 on a non-school day and 40 in a non-school week, between 7 a.m. and 7 p.m. (9 p.m. from June 1 through Labor Day). States can be stricter.
  • Breaks are set by states. Federal law does not require meal or rest breaks; short breaks you give are paid time, and meal periods of 30 minutes or more can be unpaid if the employee is fully relieved of duty.
  • Predictive scheduling laws exist in some places. Oregon requires covered employers to give written schedules 14 days ahead, and Seattle requires covered employers to post 14 days ahead and pay time-and-a-half for close-to-open shifts under 10 hours apart.
  • Records. Keep work and time schedules for two years and payroll records for three, according to the Department of Labor’s recordkeeping rules.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a free employee schedule template in Excel?

Yes. Every template on this page is a free Excel file with a printable PDF, and none needs a sign-up or email. The spreadsheets include working formulas for hours, overtime and coverage counts, and they open in Google Sheets as well as Excel. Save a copy for each week or pay period.

How do I make an employee schedule?

Collect each employee’s availability and approved time off, decide how many people each shift needs, then fill shifts while watching each person’s weekly hours. Check coverage for every day, post the schedule as early as you can, and record any changes after posting along with who asked for them.

What is the best way to schedule employees for free?

For one location and a stable team, a spreadsheet template plus an availability form is enough. Use the template that matches how you staff: by section for restaurants, by open and close for retail, by role for clinics. When changes and shift swaps take hours every week, compare scheduling apps; some have free plans, so check each vendor’s site.

How much notice do employers have to give for work schedules?

There is no federal requirement. A few states and cities have predictive scheduling laws for covered employers: Oregon requires large retail, hospitality and food service employers to give schedules 14 days ahead, and Seattle requires covered retail and food service employers to post 14 days ahead. Check your state and city rules.

Do employers have to give breaks between shifts?

Federal law does not require breaks or a minimum rest between shifts. Some states and cities do: Seattle, for example, requires covered employers to pay time-and-a-half for hours worked when a closing and the next opening shift are less than 10 hours apart. Check your state’s meal and rest break rules too.

How long should I keep old employee schedules?

Keep them at least two years. The Department of Labor lists work and time schedules among the records employers keep for two years, and payroll records must be kept for three. Saving each week’s schedule file with the date in its name is an easy way to meet the rule.
