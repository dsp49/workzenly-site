The store owner's guide to retail workforce management software

A store runs on a week that peaks on Saturday, a team that is mostly part-time, a register that opens at a fixed minute, and often a second location. This guide covers what retail workforce management software has to handle, the fair workweek laws that reach retail first, the federal limits on minors' hours, the part-time line, more than one store, and how to choose.

What retail workforce management software needs to do

Five jobs, in order. Schedule each store by zone and show coverage for every open hour, not just a list of names. Work around availability that changes with the semester, and let staff trade and pick up shifts without a manager in the middle of every text. Record hours from a shared tablet or a phone, with breaks, overtime and late starts worked out. Hire and onboard seasonal staff in time for Black Friday. Then pay each store for exactly the hours that were approved. Scheduling, the time clock kiosk, hiring, onboarding and payroll in Workzenly are those five jobs on one record.

Tools built only for retail employee scheduling, like most of those in our comparison of the best retail scheduling software, stop at publishing; the hours then get retyped into a payroll service, and the first corrections appear on Friday. Still on a spreadsheet? Our weekly schedule templates show what a zone-by-zone grid looks like.

Fair workweek rules that reach retail

Federal law says nothing about how far ahead a schedule must be posted. One state and several cities do, and retail is the industry they name first. New York City's retail rule is the strictest on short notice: 72 hours' written notice of the schedule, no on-call shifts, no cancellations within 72 hours, and no added shifts on short notice without the worker's written consent, according to the city's Department of Consumer and Worker Protection. Most independent stores fall under the thresholds everywhere but New York City. Publishing two weeks ahead is still the right habit: people can only claim a Saturday they can see. Oregon and others treat changes the employee asks for differently from the employer's changes, and a swap requested from the app carries the employee's name and a time stamp; a text thread does not.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. Scheduling and wage rules change and thresholds are updated, so check your city or state labor agency or an employment lawyer for your situation.

Minors on the schedule

Under the Fair Labor Standards Act, as summarized by the U.S. Department of Labor, 14- and 15-year-olds may work in retail jobs such as cashiering, bagging and stocking shelves, but only outside school hours and within these limits:

  • No more than 3 hours on a school day, including Fridays
  • No more than 18 hours in a week when school is in session
  • No more than 8 hours on a non-school day
  • No more than 40 hours in a week when school is not in session
  • Only between 7 a.m. and 7 p.m., extended to 9 p.m. from June 1 through Labor Day

They may not operate power-driven machinery other than office machines or work from ladders. Federal law sets no hour limits for 16- and 17-year-olds, but no one under 18 may operate a paper baler or compactor, which matters in every stockroom. Many states add hour and night limits for 16- and 17-year-olds, and the stricter rule applies. Workzenly shows each person's hours as you build and lets you block out school hours; checking a minor's shifts against your state's rules is yours to do. Keep each minor's age record in HR documents so whoever builds the schedule can see who is under 18.

Part-time hours and the 30-hour line

"Part-time" means three different things. Your handbook sets one line, usually 20 to 29 hours, for benefits. The Bureau of Labor Statistics counts 35 hours as full-time. And for the Affordable Care Act's employer shared responsibility rules, the IRS counts an employee averaging at least 30 hours of service a week, or 130 hours a month, as full-time; an applicable large employer, generally 50 or more full-time employees including equivalents, must offer that person coverage. Our guide to how many hours is part time covers all three.

The retail problem is drift. A part-timer scheduled for 24 hours picks up two open Saturdays a month, covers a sick day and averages 31. Hours per person on the schedule show that in week two rather than on a year-end report. The same weekly total keeps a pickup from turning a 38-hour week into 46: every hour a non-exempt employee works past 40 costs 1.5 times the regular rate, and a few states, California among them, add daily overtime.

Setting up more than one store

A second store doubles the schedules, not the owner. The setup that works is one account with each store as its own location:

  • Each store has its own schedule, hours and zones. The board shows one store at a time or both.
  • Each store has its own geofence and its own kiosk. A phone punch is recorded with its location and compared against that store's geofence; a tablet at each stockroom door takes PIN punches. Our geofence guide covers how big to draw it when two stores share a plaza.
  • Store managers see their store; the owner sees all of them. Tessa approves Carytown's timesheets, Devon approves Short Pump's, and neither sees the other's pay.
  • Staff can float. When Elias from Short Pump picks up a Saturday at Carytown, he clocks in at Carytown and the hours count toward one weekly total of 38.5, not two totals of 32 and 6.5.
  • Payroll is one run, subtotaled by store. You see what each store cost in regular hours, overtime and PTO, and pay everyone on the same Friday.

Attendance, no-shows and records

A register that opens at 10 cannot wait for someone who is 20 minutes late, so most stores write down what "late" means and what happens after the third one. Our attendance policy templates give you four to copy, and the no-call, no-show guide covers the opener who never shows and never calls. A policy only holds up if the record behind it is clean: the scheduled start, the actual punch and the note on any fix, with the original punch kept. The Fair Labor Standards Act accepts any complete and accurate timekeeping method and requires a record of hours worked each day and each workweek; payroll records must be kept at least three years and time cards and schedules two, according to the Department of Labor.

How to choose

  1. Run last Saturday through it. Take your real schedule for the busiest day, two swaps that happened by text, one late opener and one missed clock-out, and see which tool turns them into a correct pay run with the fewest steps.
  2. Count coverage, not names. Ask to see how many people are on the floor at 2 p.m., by zone.
  3. Check the stockroom. If phones stay in lockers, the tablet kiosk is not optional. Ask how PINs, breaks and a second store's tablet work.
  4. Open the app as a 19-year-old cashier would. Staff who avoid the app produce the same bad data as paper. Pick up a shift, request a day off, read a pay stub.
  5. Count the hand-offs. Every time hours are retyped between tools, someone fixes a pay stub later. Our guide to how to process payroll lists every step; on the demo call, ask us who handles tax deposits and filings for your setup.

Does Workzenly work for more than one store?

Yes. Each store is its own location with its own schedule, geofence and tablet kiosk, and each store manager sees only their store. The owner sees every store on one board, and timesheets and payroll are grouped by store. An employee who covers a shift at another store clocks in there and the punch lands in the same timesheet.

How does retail scheduling software handle part-timers whose availability changes?

Each person’s unavailable times and approved time off sit on the grid while you build, so a student with Monday and Wednesday classes is never dropped into a Monday morning shift. When availability changes mid-week, swaps and open shifts let the team cover each other from their phones, and the manager sees everyone’s weekly hours before saying yes.

Can store staff clock in if phones stay in the break room?

Yes. A shared tablet by the stockroom door or behind the registers works as a time clock kiosk, and each person clocks in with their own PIN. Staff who prefer their phones clock in from the Workzenly app, with GPS confirming they are inside the store’s geofence. Kiosk and phone punches land in the same timesheet.

Does it help with fair workweek laws for retail?

Workzenly records when each schedule was published and keeps a history of every change, and swap and open-shift requests are stored as requests from the employee with a time stamp. That is the evidence fair workweek laws ask for. Checking your city’s notice period and premium rules is still yours to do; New York City retail, for example, requires 72 hours’ written notice and bans on-call shifts.

Can I schedule a 15-year-old on the register after school?

Under federal child labor rules, 14- and 15-year-olds may do cashiering, bagging and shelf stocking, but only outside school hours, for no more than 3 hours on a school day, 18 hours in a school week, 8 hours on a non-school day and 40 hours in a non-school week, and only between 7 a.m. and 7 p.m. (9 p.m. from June 1 through Labor Day). They may not operate power-driven machinery, and no one under 18 may operate a baler or compactor. Many states add their own limits, so check yours.
