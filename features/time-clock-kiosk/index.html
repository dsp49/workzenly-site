The buyer’s guide to a time clock kiosk

A time clock kiosk is the simplest answer for a crew that can’t or won’t clock in on personal phones: one device by the entrance, and everyone punches there. This guide covers when a shared time clock beats phones, how it compares with other clocks, buddy punching, placement and choosing an app.

When a kiosk beats phones

Phone clock-in costs nothing in hardware and suits staff who start at different sites. A tablet at the door wins when:

  • Phones aren’t allowed on the floor. Commercial kitchens, food production, warehouses with forklifts, clinics and some retail stockrooms keep phones in lockers for safety, hygiene or patient privacy.
  • Some people don’t have, or don’t want to use, a smartphone. Requiring a personal phone to get paid is a poor policy. A shared tablet gives everyone a way to clock in.
  • Everyone starts at the same door. If the whole crew comes in one entrance, one device there is easier to supervise than a dozen phones.

Most teams end up with both. Floor staff use the kiosk, while managers, drivers and anyone who starts off-site clock in from the employee time clock app with GPS and geofencing. The GPS time clock app guide covers the phone side.

A tablet time clock runs on ordinary hardware and its punches never need retyping. A biometric terminal is harder to fake but brings legal paperwork most small teams would rather skip.

Biometric time clocks and state privacy laws

Fingerprint and face-scan clocks collect biometric identifiers, and several states regulate that closely. Illinois is the best known. Under the Biometric Information Privacy Act (740 ILCS 14), a private entity must tell people in writing that it is collecting biometric data, state the purpose and how long it will keep it, and get a written release first. It must also publish a written retention schedule and destroy the data once the purpose is met or within 3 years of the person’s last interaction, whichever comes first. BIPA also lets individuals sue.

Texas and Washington have their own biometric laws, New York limits requiring fingerprints as a condition of employment, and more states and cities add privacy rules most years. If you are considering a biometric terminal, read the current law in each state you operate in before you buy. Workzenly’s kiosk works with each person’s own PIN, so no fingerprint scanner is needed.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. Biometric and privacy laws change often, so check your state’s current statute or an employment lawyer for your situation.

Preventing buddy punching without biometrics

Buddy punching, one employee clocking in for another, is the main worry with any shared time clock. You can make it rare without scanning anyone’s finger:

  1. One PIN per person, never shared. Treat a PIN like a key to the building. Ask people to cover the pad and to tell a manager if theirs has been seen.
  2. Write it into policy. Your handbook should say that clocking in for someone else is not allowed and what happens if it does. Our attendance policy guide has wording to start from.
  3. Put the tablet where a manager can see it. Near the manager’s station or the line, not in a back hallway nobody walks through.
  4. Compare punches with the floor. Workzenly shows who is in, late or out against the schedule. If the list says five people are working and you can count four, you know where to look.
  5. Review the exceptions every week. Missed clock-outs, late starts and people close to 40 hours are flagged, and the edit history keeps the original punch next to any change.

Placement and setup

Where the tablet sits matters as much as the app on it:

  • At the entrance staff actually use. Usually the back or staff door, so people clock in before they start work and clock out after they finish. Time spent working before a punch still counts as hours worked.
  • Mounted and plugged in. A wall mount or locking stand with a charger keeps the kiosk where it belongs and stops it running flat mid-shift.
  • On reliable Wi-Fi. Test the signal at the exact spot before you drill holes.
  • Out of the way of steam, grease and forklifts. A case helps in kitchens and on loading docks.

Any current iPad or Android tablet that stays at the door is the usual choice for an iPad time clock or Android kiosk; confirm the device you plan to use on your demo call. Then send us your employee list as a spreadsheet. We import it, help you set up the location and the kiosk, and most small teams are clocking in within a week.

Records the law expects

The Fair Labor Standards Act does not require a time clock. The U.S. Department of Labor says employers may use any timekeeping method they choose, as long as it is complete and accurate. For non-exempt employees you need hours worked each day and each week, and you must keep payroll records for at least 3 years and time cards for at least 2. A kiosk that writes every punch, break and edit into each person’s record covers that without a filing cabinet.

If you still work from paper, the attendance tracker templates are a free stopgap, and the time card calculator shows how punches, breaks and overtime add up for a week.

Choosing a kiosk time clock app

Run a real week through each app on your shortlist, on the tablet you would actually mount. Check these:

  • Does each person clock in under their own account, and can they see their shift before they punch?
  • Do kiosk punches and phone punches land in the same timesheet, or in two reports you reconcile?
  • Are breaks, overtime and late arrivals worked out from the punches?
  • Can an employee request a fix for a missed punch, and does the history keep the original?
  • Do approved hours flow into payroll without an export and re-import?
  • What does it collect? If biometrics, what notice, consent and retention does your state require?

What is a time clock kiosk?

A time clock kiosk is a shared device, usually a tablet mounted by the entrance, where employees clock in and out instead of using their own phones or a punch card. In Workzenly each person clocks in at the kiosk under their own account, and the punch goes into the same timesheet as any phone punch.

Can some employees use the kiosk and others use their phones?

Yes. Many teams mix the two: the kitchen or warehouse floor clocks in at the tablet by the door, while managers and staff who work off-site clock in from the Workzenly app on their phones, with GPS and geofencing. Every punch lands in the same timesheet, so the manager approves one week, not two.

How do you stop buddy punching on a shared tablet?

Give every employee their own PIN and a written rule that punching for someone else is not allowed, put the tablet where a manager can see it, and review punches against the schedule. Workzenly shows who is in, late or out and flags missed clock-outs and late starts, so a punch that does not match the floor stands out.

Are biometric time clock kiosks legal?

In most states, yes, but some regulate them closely. Illinois, under the Biometric Information Privacy Act, requires written notice, a written release and a public retention and destruction policy before an employer collects fingerprints or face scans, and Texas and Washington have their own biometric laws. Check your state before buying one. Workzenly's kiosk does not use biometrics.

Does federal law require a time clock?

No. The U.S. Department of Labor says employers may use any timekeeping method they choose, as long as it is complete and accurate. Employers must keep payroll records for at least 3 years and time cards for at least 2 years. A kiosk is one way to keep those records without paper cards.
