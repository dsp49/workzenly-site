Buyer’s guide: how to pick an employee time clock app

A time clock app is the part of your workforce software that employees touch most, often four times a shift. If it is slow or confusing, people avoid it and you are back to guessing at hours. This guide covers what to look for, phone versus kiosk versus punch clock, the federal rules on rounding and breaks, location notice and rollout.

What to look for in a time clock app

Start with the person holding the phone. Hand the app to your least technical employee and time how long it takes them to clock in. Then check:

  1. The shift is on screen. The clock-in screen should show today’s start time, role and site, pulled from the schedule.
  2. One tap to punch. Clock in, start a break, end a break and clock out should each be one obvious button.
  3. A location check you can explain. If you use GPS, look for a geofence check at the punch, a clear inside or outside result, and a review step for punches outside the line. Our explainer on what a geofence is covers how the radius works.
  4. A way to fix mistakes. Employees should be able to request a fix for a missed punch, and the edit history should keep the original next to the change and show who approved it.
  5. Hours people can see. A week-so-far view in hours and decimals cuts “was I paid right?” questions. The hours to decimal calculator shows why 26:24 is 26.4 hours, not 26.24.
  6. A path to payroll. If approved hours are retyped into payroll, you have kept half the old problem.

If you are comparing vendors, our roundup of employee time clock apps sets the main options side by side, and the list of free time clock apps covers no-cost plans and their limits. Use them to build a shortlist, then test a real week.

What the record must hold

Federal law does not require a time clock; 29 CFR 785.48 says so directly. What the Fair Labor Standards Act does require is accurate records for non-exempt employees, including the hours worked each workday, the total hours each workweek and the day and time the workweek begins. According to the U.S. Department of Labor, payroll records must be kept for at least 3 years and time cards for at least 2.

An online time clock meets that rule more easily than paper because each punch is stored with its date, time and any later edit.

Rounding rules under 29 CFR 785.48

Under 29 CFR 785.48(b), recording start and stop times to the nearest 5 minutes, tenth of an hour or quarter hour is accepted only if the practice does not, over time, fail to pay employees for all the time they actually worked. Rounding that always favors the employer is not allowed.

Quarter-hour rounding moves a punch by up to 7 minutes either way. The same section also says employees who clock in early or stay late without working do not have to be paid for that time, but large gaps between punches and real hours raise doubts about the records. Some states are stricter. When your app records the exact minute, the simplest choice is to pay the exact minute and write that in your handbook.

Paid and unpaid breaks

Federal law does not require lunch or coffee breaks. When you do offer them, the U.S. Department of Labor sets out how they count:

  • Short breaks, usually 5 to 20 minutes, are hours worked. They are paid and they count toward the 40 hours that trigger overtime.
  • Bona fide meal periods, typically 30 minutes or more, are not work time, as long as the employee is completely relieved of duty. A lunch spent answering the phone at the counter is paid time.
  • Unauthorized extensions of a short break need not be paid if you clearly told employees the time limit and that going over breaks the rules.

Several states, including California, Washington and Oregon, go further and require meal or rest breaks at set points in a shift. Your time clock app should keep short rest breaks paid and deduct only real meal breaks. Our guide to the attendance policy shows how to write break and punch rules down.

Telling employees about location

GPS at the punch is common. Problems start when people find out about it later. Before you turn it on:

  • Tell employees in writing what the app records and when. With Workzenly, that is the location when they tap clock in or out.
  • Decide what happens to a punch outside the geofence. A manager review is fairer than a blocked clock-in that leaves someone working off the clock.
  • Check your state’s rules. Some states require written notice before employers monitor employees electronically, and the details differ, especially when staff use personal phones.

Our guide to the GPS time clock app covers this in more depth.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. Rules change and states differ, so check your state labor department or an employment lawyer for your situation.

Rolling the clock in clock out app out to staff

  1. Pick a start date at the top of a workweek. For a Sunday to Saturday week, start on a Sunday so no week is split across two systems.
  2. Import your people. Send us your employee list and PTO balances as a spreadsheet and we import them during setup.
  3. Set up each site. Add the location, draw the geofence and decide whether staff punch on phones, a tablet at the door or both.
  4. Write the rules down. Breaks, rounding (or the lack of it), missed-punch fixes and location notice go in your employee handbook.
  5. Show people once, in person. Five minutes at a shift meeting: find today’s shift, clock in, start a break.
  6. Run one week next to the old method. Compare totals, settle any differences, then switch fully for the next pay period.

What is an employee time clock app?

An employee time clock app lets staff clock in and out, start and end breaks and check their hours from a phone instead of a punch clock or paper card. Each punch records the time and, if you turn on GPS and geofencing, where it happened. In Workzenly those punches build the timesheet a manager approves and the hours that reach payroll.

Can employees clock in from their own phones?

Yes. Each employee has their own Workzenly account and can clock in and out from their phone, with today's shift on screen and a geofence check at the punch. Where phones stay in lockers, a shared tablet at the door works as a time clock kiosk instead, and many teams use both.

Is a GPS time clock legal?

Recording location when an employee clocks in is common in the US, but state privacy and notice rules vary. Tell employees in writing what the app records and when, put it in your handbook, and check your state's rules before you roll it out, especially if staff use personal phones.

Can I round time clock punches?

Federal rules in 29 CFR 785.48(b) accept rounding to the nearest 5 minutes, tenth of an hour or quarter hour only if, over time, employees are paid for all the time they actually worked. Rounding that always favors the employer is not allowed, and some states are stricter. When your clock records the exact minute, paying the exact minute avoids the question.

Are breaks paid when employees clock out for them?

Under federal law, short breaks of about 5 to 20 minutes count as paid hours worked, and bona fide meal periods, usually 30 minutes or more with the employee relieved of duty, do not. Federal law does not require breaks at all, but several states do. Workzenly deducts unpaid meal breaks and keeps short rest breaks paid.

What happens if an employee forgets to clock out?

The missing clock-out is flagged for the manager, and the employee can send a fix request from the app with the time they left and a short note. When the manager approves it, the timesheet updates and the edit history keeps the original punch next to the correction, with who made it.
