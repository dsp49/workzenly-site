The buyer’s guide to time and attendance software

Most small employers start with paper time cards or a shared spreadsheet, and it works until the team grows past a dozen people or a second location. This guide covers what time and attendance software does, what US law requires you to record, the types of systems on the market and how to pick one for an hourly team.

What time and attendance software does

A time and attendance system records when employees start and stop work, tracks breaks and absences, totals hours by day and workweek, and produces timesheets for approval. Good systems also flag the things that cost money: missed punches, late arrivals, overtime and punches from the wrong place. The approved hours then go to payroll, either by export or, in a connected setup like Workzenly, directly into the pay run.

It sits between two other tools. Employee scheduling software decides who should work. Time and attendance records who did. Payroll pays for it. The fewer hand-offs between the three, the fewer errors.

What US law requires you to record

The Fair Labor Standards Act does not require a time clock or any particular method. It does require employers to keep accurate records for non-exempt employees, including:

  • Hours worked each workday and total hours each workweek
  • The day and time the workweek begins
  • Regular hourly rate, overtime earnings and total wages for each pay period

According to the U.S. Department of Labor, payroll records must be kept for at least 3 years and time cards and wage calculation records for at least 2. Federal rules allow rounding punches to the nearest 5 minutes, tenth of an hour or quarter hour only when it averages out so employees are paid for all time actually worked. Our guide to how to calculate overtime walks through the regular rate, and the time card calculator shows how breaks and minutes add up.

State rules to plan for

Federal law sets the floor. Several states add rules your system needs to handle:

  • Daily overtime. California pays overtime after 8 hours in a day and double time after 12, on top of the weekly rule. See our guide to California overtime law.
  • Meal and rest breaks. States such as California, Washington and Oregon require breaks at set points in a shift, and some charge a premium when one is missed.
  • Scheduling notice. Oregon and cities including New York City, San Francisco, Seattle, Chicago and Philadelphia have fair workweek laws for some employers that tie attendance to the posted schedule.
  • Paid sick leave. Many states and cities bar counting protected sick time against attendance. Our attendance policy guide explains how to write a policy that respects it.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. Rules change, so check your state labor department or an employment lawyer for your situation.

Switching from paper or spreadsheets

  1. Pick a start date at the beginning of a workweek, so no week is split across two systems.
  2. Import your employee list and PTO balances. Workzenly does this during setup from a spreadsheet.
  3. Set up locations and geofences, and decide whether each site uses phones, a tablet or both.
  4. Write down your break, rounding and overtime rules in your employee handbook and tell employees what location data is recorded.
  5. Run the first week alongside your old method, compare totals, then switch fully for the next pay period.

How to choose

Run one real week through every system you are considering. Take last week’s schedule and punches, including a missed clock-out, a late start and someone who went over 40 hours, and see which tool turns them into a correct pay stub with the fewest manual steps. Ask each vendor how they handle location notice, edit history, overtime rules for your state and the hand-off to payroll. Then check what employees see on their phones, because a tool people avoid using produces the same bad data as paper.

What is time and attendance software?

Time and attendance software records when employees start and stop work, tracks breaks, absences, lateness and overtime, and turns those records into timesheets a manager can approve. Most systems then send approved hours to payroll. Workzenly does all of that and gives each employee an app to clock in, see their hours and request time off.

Does federal law require a time clock?

No. The Fair Labor Standards Act does not require any particular timekeeping method. It does require employers to keep accurate records of hours worked each day and each week for non-exempt employees, and to keep payroll records for 3 years and time cards for 2 years. Software is simply the easiest way to meet that rule.

Can employees clock in from their own phones?

Yes. Each person has their own Workzenly account and can clock in from their phone, with GPS and geofencing confirming they are at the right site. For workplaces where phones stay in lockers, a shared tablet at the door works as a time clock kiosk instead.

How does time and attendance software handle overtime?

It totals hours by workweek and flags anything over 40 for non-exempt staff, which is the federal overtime rule. Workzenly works out overtime and breaks from the actual punches, so approved overtime reaches payroll at the right rate instead of being added by hand. States such as California also have daily overtime rules to check.

Is it legal to round employee punches?

Federal rules allow rounding to the nearest 5 minutes, tenth of an hour or quarter hour, but only if it averages out so employees are paid for all the time they actually work. Rounding that always favors the employer is not allowed. Many employers simply pay the exact punched minutes to avoid the question.
