The buyer’s guide to performance review software

Most small employers run reviews with a Word form and a spreadsheet, and that works until there are two managers, a second location or a raise decision someone questions. This guide covers what performance review software does, the kinds of reviews small businesses run, the legal points to get right and how to pick a tool for a team that works in shifts.

What performance review software does

At its simplest, it holds four things for each employee: their goals, notes from one-on-ones, self-reviews and the manager’s written review. On top of that it tracks the cycle itself, so you can see who has finished their part without a side spreadsheet. The finished review is stored in the employee’s record, where it can be found when you plan pay, promotions or answer a question later.

Larger performance management suites add 360 surveys, engagement surveys and analytics. Many small businesses do not need those. What they need is a fair, repeatable review that managers actually finish. If you are not ready for software, our performance review templates and employee evaluation forms are free to download and follow the same structure.

Types of performance reviews

No federal law sets a schedule. Most small businesses mix three kinds of review:

Review typeWhenWhat it coversGood fit
90-day reviewAbout three months after hireWhether the new hire has learned the job, first goals, questions both waysEvery new hire, hourly or salaried
Quarterly check-inEvery three months, 15 to 20 minutesProgress on goals, schedule and workload, no ratingsKeeping the annual review free of surprises
Annual reviewOnce a year, often on a set month or the hire anniversaryRatings against the job’s standards, goals for next year, payRaise decisions and formal records
Six-month reviewTwice a yearA lighter version of the annual reviewHourly teams with high turnover

One-on-ones sit under all of them. Short, regular talks with notes are what make the formal reviews quick to write and hard to dispute.

Performance management software for small business shift teams

Most review tools are designed for office staff who sit at a laptop. A café, store or clinic has different needs. Employees need to write a self-review on a phone. Managers need attendance history nearby, because showing up on time is part of the job. And the categories should fit the work: customer service, food safety, cash handling and teamwork matter more on a coffee bar than “strategic thinking.”

Keep self-reviews short. Three or four plain questions get honest answers from a barista between shifts. If employees ask what to write, point them to our self performance review examples, which include phrases for hourly roles.

Legal cautions for reviews

The U.S. Department of Labor says the Fair Labor Standards Act does not require performance evaluations, and does not require raises above the federal minimum wage. Once you do hold reviews, though, they become evidence of how you treated people.

  • Anti-discrimination laws apply. The EEOC enforces federal laws that bar discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex (including pregnancy, sexual orientation and gender identity), national origin, age (40 or older), disability and genetic information, in pay, promotion, discipline and other terms of employment. Title VII and the ADA cover employers with 15 or more employees, the age law covers 20 or more, and the Equal Pay Act covers virtually all employers. State and city laws often reach smaller employers.
  • Do not count protected leave against attendance. FMLA regulations at 29 CFR 825.220(c) say employers cannot use the taking of FMLA leave as a negative factor in employment actions, and cannot count it under no-fault attendance policies. Many state and city sick leave laws protect sick time the same way. Our attendance policy guide lists the absences that should never become points, and the FMLA leave guide explains who is covered.
  • Watch for retaliation. The EEOC lists a performance evaluation that is lower than it should be as a possible form of retaliation against someone who complained of discrimination or took part in an investigation. If an employee recently complained, have a second manager check the ratings against the evidence.
  • Keep the records. EEOC rules require private employers to keep personnel records for at least one year, and one year from the date of an involuntary termination. Some states require longer.

In practice: use the same categories for everyone in a job, write an example behind every high and low rating, and leave protected characteristics, leave and accommodations out of the comments.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. Rules change, so check with your state labor department or an employment lawyer for your situation.

Linking performance reviews to raises

If ratings drive raises, write the link down before the cycle starts, so managers rate the work and not the budget. Here is an illustrative merit table using Jordan Lee, a barista at $18.25 an hour. The percentages are examples only; set your own from your budget and local pay rates.

Moving reviews off spreadsheets

  1. Pick your rating scale and categories. A 5-point scale with a written anchor for each number is a solid default.
  2. Decide your calendar: 90-day reviews for new hires, quarterly check-ins and one annual or six-month review.
  3. Bring your team in. Workzenly imports your employee list from a spreadsheet during setup.
  4. Set two to four goals with each person at the start of the cycle, and keep notes from each one-on-one.
  5. Run one cycle, then ask managers and employees what to change before the next.

How to choose performance review software

Run one real review through each tool you are considering. Take an employee with a mixed year, such as a strong performer with a few late starts and a week of approved leave, and see which tool lets the manager write a fair review with the evidence in view. Ask each vendor what employees see on a phone, who can read a finished review, where reviews are stored and how pay changes reach payroll. Then check whether the tool covers the rest of the job, or whether you would be adding one more login next to scheduling, time clocks and payroll. What is performance review software?

Performance review software replaces the review spreadsheet and the paper forms in a filing cabinet. It holds each person's goals, the notes from one-on-ones and the reviews themselves, and it shows who has finished their part of a review cycle. In Workzenly, every review lives in the employee's record next to their schedule, time off and documents.

Are performance reviews required by law?

No. The U.S. Department of Labor says the FLSA does not require performance evaluations or pay raises above the minimum wage. If you do hold reviews, the anti-discrimination laws enforced by the EEOC apply, so use the same job-related criteria for everyone in a role and keep the records.

How often should a small business run performance reviews?

No federal law sets a schedule. A common pattern is a 90-day review for new hires, short quarterly check-ins and one annual review that covers pay and next year's goals. Hourly teams with high turnover often do better with six-month reviews than with annual ones.

Can attendance be part of a performance review?

Yes, but only absences and late arrivals that are not protected by law. FMLA regulations at 29 CFR 825.220(c) say employers cannot use the taking of FMLA leave as a negative factor in employment actions or count it under no-fault attendance policies. Many state and city sick leave laws protect sick time the same way, so take protected leave out before you rate reliability.

Can employees write a self-review on their phone?

Yes. The employee side of Workzenly is built for phones, so a barista or cashier can write a self-review in the same app they use to clock in and request time off. Managers can work from a phone too, though most prefer a laptop for writing longer reviews.

Who can see an employee's performance review?

Employees see only their own record, including their own goals and reviews, never a coworker's. Managers work with their own team, and pay is visible only to the people you give payroll access to, so a review can be shared without exposing anyone's pay rate.
