Quick answer

  • Pew Research Center found 50% of US workers extremely or very satisfied with their job overall in October 2024, its latest survey on the topic.
  • Only 30% were highly satisfied with their pay and 26% with promotion chances, while 64% were highly satisfied with coworkers.
  • The Conference Board's broader index hit a record 68.9% in 2026, but it ranged from 45.3% in the lowest-income households to 76% in the highest.

The most cited job satisfaction statistics come from Pew Research Center, whose survey of 5,273 employed US adults, conducted October 7 to 13, 2024, found that 50% are extremely or very satisfied with their job overall. The Conference Board, using a different scale, reported on June 2, 2026 that overall satisfaction reached 68.9%, the highest in its survey's history. Both agree on the weak spots: pay, promotion and lower-income workers. This page collects the latest job satisfaction survey results, with the source, sample and period beside each number.

Overall job satisfaction in the US

Three well-known surveys measure how satisfied US workers are, and each asks a different question, so their numbers differ.

  • 50% of workers were extremely or very satisfied with their job overall, 38% somewhat satisfied and 12% not too or not at all satisfied, per Pew Research Center's December 10, 2024 report (survey fielded October 7 to 13, 2024).
  • Self-employed workers were more satisfied: 60% highly satisfied, versus 49% of those who work for someone else.
  • In Pew's earlier survey, fielded February 6 to 12, 2023, 51% of workers who are not self-employed were extremely or very satisfied (Pew, March 30, 2023). In that survey, 50% said their job was enjoyable all or most of the time, 47% fulfilling, 29% stressful and 19% overwhelming.
  • 68.9% of workers were satisfied with their jobs in 2026, up from 68.3% in 2025, according to The Conference Board's June 2, 2026 release. Satisfaction with the 27 individual job elements it asks about averaged about 59%.
  • 18% of US employees were extremely satisfied with their company as a place to work as of August 2026, per Gallup's engagement indicator.

Why the spread? Pew and Gallup count only the top boxes ("extremely or very" and "extremely"), while The Conference Board counts workers who are satisfied at all. When you quote employee satisfaction statistics, give each figure with its source and wording rather than comparing them directly. For how many workers are actively engaged, a related but different measure, see our employee engagement statistics.

What this means for employers: Most of your staff probably like their job well enough, but only about half like it a lot. The other half is where turnover risk sits, and asking a short, direct satisfaction question once or twice a year tells you which half each person is in.

Satisfaction with pay, benefits, managers and coworkers

Pew asked workers who are not self-employed about 11 parts of their job. People are happiest with the people they work with and least happy with money and advancement.

  • 29% were not too or not at all satisfied with their pay, and 38% felt that way about promotion opportunities.
  • 40% were dissatisfied with how much flexibility they have to work remotely. Our remote work statistics show how few jobs allow it.
  • Workers feel respected day to day: 86% say coworkers and 82% say supervisors treat them with respect all or most of the time.

Job satisfaction by age, income and race

In both Pew and Conference Board data, satisfaction rises with age and income.

  • Age. The Conference Board's June 11, 2025 release, based on 1,700 employed US workers, found 57.4% of workers under 25 satisfied, compared with 72.4% of workers 55 and older.
  • Income. In The Conference Board's 2026 survey, satisfaction ranged from 45.3% for households earning under $25,000 to 76% for households earning $150,000 or more. It called income the strongest demographic factor.
  • Income gaps on benefits and pay. Pew found 30% of lower-income workers highly satisfied with their benefits, versus 48% of middle-income and 56% of upper-income workers. For pay it was 21%, 28% and 44%.
  • Race and ethnicity. 55% of White workers were extremely or very satisfied with their job, compared with 44% of Hispanic, 43% of Black and 42% of Asian workers (Pew, October 2024).
  • Gender. The Conference Board found men more satisfied than women on 26 of its 27 job elements in 2026. Work-life balance was the exception. The largest gaps were wages (7.2 points), health plans (7.0), pension and retirement benefits (6.3) and promotion policy (6.2).

What this means for employers: If your team is young and paid hourly, expect satisfaction below the national average before you change anything. Younger workers rate growth and feedback highly, so regular reviews with a clear next step do more for them than for a 60-year-old who has settled into a role.

Job satisfaction by industry and type of work

No major public survey reports job satisfaction by detailed industry each year. The closest current data is Pew's look at blue-collar workers, defined as those who do manual or physical labor in manufacturing, mining and construction; agriculture; retail and trade; hospitality or service; or transportation. They make up about 27% of the US workforce (Pew, March 31, 2025, from the October 2024 survey, including 1,004 blue-collar workers).

  • Among blue-collar women, 40% were not too or not at all satisfied with their pay, compared with 30% of blue-collar men.
  • Pew also found blue-collar workers less satisfied with health insurance and other benefits and with schedule flexibility, without publishing the exact shares.
  • On respect for their line of work, 56% of health care and social assistance workers said most Americans have a great deal or fair amount of respect for people who do their job. About four in ten workers in hospitality, service, arts, entertainment and recreation, and in retail or trade, said most people have little or no respect for it (Pew, October 2024).
  • By employer type, government workers were the most likely to report a great deal of job security (47%), followed by nonprofit workers (39%), private company workers (29%) and the self-employed (28%).

What this means for employers: Blue-collar jobs, which include much of the work in restaurants, stores, warehouses and job sites, start about 10 points behind on overall satisfaction. Predictable schedules, accurate pay and a manager who treats people with respect are the levers within reach. Worker satisfaction trend, 2010 to 2026

The long-run trend in job satisfaction statistics depends on which survey you follow.

202568.3%Up 5.7 points, the largest one-year jump in the survey's history
202668.9%Highest since the survey began in 1987, after 16 consecutive years of increases from the 2010 low
  • The Conference Board fields its survey each January using data from its Consumer Confidence Survey. In 2025, satisfaction rose on 26 of 27 job elements; quality of equipment was the only decline.
  • Its principal researcher, Allan Schweyer, said the 2026 high "may reflect that workers are grateful to be employed amid widespread uncertainty."
  • Pew's measure moved the other way between February 2023 and October 2024: overall high satisfaction among workers who are not self-employed went from 51% to 49%, and seven of nine job aspects fell 3 to 7 points.
  • 52% of workers said in October 2024 it would be difficult to get the kind of job they want if they looked, up from 37% in 2022 (Pew).

The two series measure different things: The Conference Board asks about 27 job elements and reports a share satisfied, while Pew reports only the most satisfied. A weaker job market can raise contentment with having a job while lowering enthusiasm for it, which fits both results.

What this means for employers: High national satisfaction during a slow hiring market can hide people who are staying because they feel stuck. When hiring picks up, those workers leave first.

Why workers are unhappy with pay

Pay is the clearest pain point in job satisfaction statistics from both Pew and The Conference Board. Among workers who are not self-employed and not too or not at all satisfied with their pay, Pew asked for the reasons in October 2024:

  • 80% said a major reason is that their pay has not kept up with increases in the cost of living.
  • 71% said it is too low for the quality of work they do, and 70% too low for the amount of work.
  • 54% said they don't earn enough to pay their bills, including 69% of lower-income, 51% of middle-income and 30% of upper-income workers.
  • 28% said they are paid less than coworkers who do similar work. Women were more likely than men to say this (32% vs. 24%) and to say they cannot pay their bills (61% vs. 47%).

What this means for employers: Most pay complaints are about keeping pace and fairness, not about being the top payer. Review rates at least once a year against local wages (our minimum wage by state and average hourly wage guides help), explain how pay is set, and get every paycheck right. A shift differential for nights and weekends is one targeted option.

Satisfaction, job searching and quits

  • 64% of workers who are not too or not at all satisfied with their job said they were likely to look for a new job in the next six months, compared with 31% of somewhat satisfied and 11% of extremely or very satisfied workers (Pew, October 2024).
  • Overall, 25% of workers said they were at least somewhat likely to look for a new job in the next six months, and 63% said they were unlikely to.
  • 37% of workers ages 18 to 29 and 37% of lower-income workers said they were likely to look.
  • 50% of workers see their current job as a career, 15% as a stepping stone to one and 35% as just a job to get them by.
  • Employers recorded 3.4 million quits in September 2026, a quits rate of 2.1%, per the BLS Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey released November 3, 2026.
  • Gallup's American Job Quality Study found only 41% of US workers hold "quality jobs" in 2026, based on more than 17,000 workers, for the second year in a row at about two in five.

For quit and separation rates by industry, see our employee turnover statistics. For the stress side of dissatisfaction, see burnout statistics.

What this means for employers: A dissatisfied worker is about six times as likely as a highly satisfied one to plan a job search in Pew's data (Workzenly calculation: 64% vs. 11%). Finding out who is unhappy early is cheaper than replacing them.

What the data means for employers

  1. Hold regular check-ins. Only 44% of workers are highly satisfied with the feedback they receive. Use a performance review template and ask people to prepare with self-performance review examples.
  2. Show a path up. Promotion is the lowest-rated job aspect at 26%. Write down what the next role or raise requires.
  3. Make pay predictable and correct. Most pay complaints are about cost of living and fairness, and errors make both worse.
  4. Give hourly staff the same information as office staff. Schedules, pay stubs and time-off balances in an employee app remove guesswork for people who are rarely at a desk.
  5. Measure your own team. National averages are context. A two-question survey each quarter tells you more.

About this data

Every one of these job satisfaction statistics comes from a source page or report opened on November 13, 2026. Pew Research Center publishes worker surveys every year or two; its latest job satisfaction data is from October 2024. The Conference Board releases its job satisfaction survey each spring, with fieldwork in January; its full report is for members, so we use its public press releases, which do not state the 2026 sample size. Gallup's indicator pages update as new data arrive, and BLS JOLTS is published monthly. We review this page when new releases come out. Journalists and bloggers are welcome to cite this page with a link, and we recommend linking the original source as well.

Frequently asked questions

What percentage of US workers are satisfied with their jobs?

In Pew Research Center's October 2024 survey of 5,273 employed adults, 50% said they were extremely or very satisfied with their job overall, 38% somewhat satisfied and 12% not too or not at all satisfied. The Conference Board's broader measure put overall satisfaction at 68.9% in 2026.

Is job satisfaction going up or down in 2026?

It depends on the measure. The Conference Board reported on June 2, 2026 that satisfaction reached 68.9%, a record and up from 68.3% in 2025. Pew found that between February 2023 and October 2024, high satisfaction fell 3 to 7 points on seven of nine job aspects it tracks.

What part of their job are workers least satisfied with?

Pay and promotion. In Pew's October 2024 survey, only 30% of workers who are not self-employed were extremely or very satisfied with their pay and 26% with their opportunities for promotion. By contrast, 64% were highly satisfied with their relationship with coworkers and 59% with their manager.

Are younger workers less satisfied with their jobs?

Yes. Pew found 43% of workers ages 18 to 29 were extremely or very satisfied with their job in October 2024, versus 67% of workers 65 and older. The Conference Board's 2025 survey found 57.4% of workers under 25 satisfied, compared with 72.4% of workers 55 and older.

Does income affect job satisfaction?

Strongly. The Conference Board's 2026 survey found satisfaction of 45.3% in households earning under $25,000 and 76% at $150,000 or more. In Pew's 2024 survey, 42% of lower-income workers were highly satisfied with their job, compared with 53% of middle-income and 54% of upper-income workers.

Are dissatisfied workers more likely to quit?

Pew found that 64% of workers who were not too or not at all satisfied with their job said they were likely to look for a new job in the next six months, versus 11% of highly satisfied workers. Nationally, 3.4 million people quit in September 2026, a 2.1% rate, per BLS JOLTS.
