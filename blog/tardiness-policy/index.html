Quick answer

  • Tardiness means starting work after the scheduled start time, beyond any grace period your written policy allows.
  • You can discipline for it, but hourly staff must still be paid for every minute they work, and exempt salaries generally can’t be cut for a late start.
  • Lateness tied to FMLA leave, a disability accommodation or protected sick time can’t be counted against the employee.

Tardiness is arriving at work, or starting work, after your scheduled start time. One late morning is a nuisance. A pattern of it leaves a coffee bar short at the 7 a.m. rush, pushes work onto coworkers and, when it is handled badly, turns into a wage claim or a discrimination complaint. This guide explains how US employers can handle tardy employees fairly and legally: how to set a grace period, what federal wage law says about pay for late arrivals, which reasons for being late are protected, and how to build a late to work policy with clear tiers. What tardiness means at work

No federal law defines tardiness. Your written policy does. Most policies cover four situations, and it helps to name each one so managers apply them the same way.

  • Late arrival: clocking in after the scheduled shift start, past the grace period.
  • Late return from a meal break: coming back after the end of an unpaid meal period.
  • Leaving early: clocking out before the shift ends without approval. Many policies treat this like a tardy.
  • Very late arrival: showing up an hour or more late, which many employers handle as a partial absence rather than a tardy.

Not showing up at all and not calling is a different problem with its own rules. Our guide to no call no show covers that, including when it can be treated as job abandonment.

Two definitions matter most: what “start” means and what “on time” means. Write down whether the start is the moment the employee clocks in, or the moment they are at their station ready to work. If staff must put on a uniform or pass a security check first, and that work is required, it may be paid time, so the clock-in point should come before it.

Grace periods: how late is late?

A grace period is a few minutes after the scheduled start during which a late arrival is not counted as tardy for discipline. Many employers use 5 to 7 minutes. It absorbs a slow bus or a crowded parking lot without turning every minute into a write-up.

Three rules keep a grace period out of trouble:

  1. A grace period is about discipline, not pay. An employee who clocks in at 7:04 and starts working is still owed pay from 7:04 (or from the rounded time, if you round neutrally). The grace period only decides whether the arrival counts as a tardy.
  2. Apply it to everyone in the same job. Giving one employee 10 minutes and another none, without a business reason, is the kind of uneven treatment that shows up in discrimination claims.
  3. Don’t confuse it with rounding. A 7-minute grace period and 15-minute rounding are separate rules. Rounding changes pay; the grace period doesn’t.

Tardiness and pay: what the law allows

Federal wage rules come from the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), enforced by the US Department of Labor’s Wage and Hour Division. States can add stricter rules, and where they do, you follow the stricter one.

Hourly (non-exempt) employees

The FLSA does not require you to pay non-exempt employees for time they did not work. If someone starts 14 minutes late, you can leave those 14 minutes unpaid. What you cannot do is leave worked time unpaid, or let any deduction push pay for the week below the minimum wage or the overtime owed.

Here is an illustrative example. Sam Rivera works in the kitchen at Northfield Coffee in Austin for $18.00 an hour. His shift starts at 9:00 a.m. and he clocks in at 9:14 a.m.

How the late arrival is handledPaid fromUnpaid time workedWages shorted
Pay from the actual punch9:14 a.m.None$0.00
Neutral rounding to the nearest quarter hour9:15 a.m.1 minute (evens out over time)$0.30
“Late penalty”: dock to 9:30 a.m.9:30 a.m.16 minutes$4.80

The first two are lawful under federal rules. The third is not: Sam worked from 9:14 to 9:30, and 16 minutes at $18.00 an hour is $4.80 of wages he earned and didn’t get. Done every week to a team of 20, that adds up to a real back-pay bill.

Rounding. The Department of Labor’s rule at 29 CFR 785.48(b) accepts rounding punches to the nearest 5 minutes, tenth of an hour or quarter hour, as long as over time it does not fail to pay employees for all the time they actually worked. With quarter-hour rounding, a punch 1 to 7 minutes after the quarter rounds back and 8 to 14 minutes rounds forward. Rounding that only ever goes the employer’s way, such as rounding late punches forward but never rounding early starts back, is not neutral. To see how minutes turn into payroll hours, use our hours to decimal calculator, and to total a week of punches with breaks, the time card calculator.

Early punches. Under 29 CFR 785.48(a), if employees clock in early but do no work, you don’t have to pay for that idle time. If they start working, you do.

State rules. Some states restrict wage deductions more tightly than federal law or limit rounding. Check your state labor department before using any deduction or rounding rule.

Salaried exempt employees

Exempt employees must be paid on a salary basis. Under 29 CFR 541.602, you can make deductions for one or more full days missed for personal reasons, but not for a partial day. So if an exempt manager arrives two hours late, you generally cannot cut those two hours from the salary. The main exception is unpaid FMLA leave, which may be deducted in partial days. The Department of Labor’s Fact Sheet 17G explains that an actual practice of improper deductions can cost you the exemption for that job group, which can mean back overtime.

You can still address lateness with exempt staff: coaching, warnings, a performance review note, or termination. A separate rule allows unpaid full-day suspensions for violating workplace conduct rules, but employment lawyers generally read it as covering serious misconduct such as harassment or violence, not attendance, so don’t rely on it for tardiness. When lateness is legally protected

Some late arrivals cannot be counted against an employee, even under a strict no-fault policy. Before you add a point or write a warning, ask why the employee was late.

  • FMLA leave. Eligible employees can take FMLA leave intermittently, in blocks as short as an hour, for their own or a family member’s serious health condition. A late start caused by a flare-up or a treatment appointment can be FMLA time. The Department of Labor’s rule at 29 CFR 825.220(c) says FMLA leave cannot be counted under no-fault attendance policies. Our FMLA leave guide covers eligibility and certification.
  • Disability accommodation (ADA). The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) says an employer may have to adjust attendance rules, such as moving a start time, as a reasonable accommodation unless it causes undue hardship. Its guidance gives the example of a manager whose medication made mornings hard and who could do the job fully starting an hour later. The EEOC also says employers generally do not have to excuse repeated, unpredictable lateness, and a request made after a warning does not erase the warning, though it must still be considered for the future.
  • State and local paid sick leave. Many states and cities protect sick time from discipline. California’s Labor Commissioner, for example, says in its paid sick leave FAQ that an employer may not give an attendance “occurrence” when an employee uses accrued, available paid sick leave for a covered reason, including a partial day for a doctor’s appointment. Check the rules where your employees work. Texas has no statewide paid sick leave law.
  • Predictive scheduling laws. Oregon and several cities require some employers, mostly larger retail, food service and hospitality businesses, to post schedules in advance and pay a premium for late changes. Those laws don’t excuse lateness, but an employee who arrives at the time on the posted schedule is not late because you changed the shift without proper notice. Check your city and state rules.
  • Religious practice and pregnancy. Requests to adjust a start time for religious observance or a pregnancy-related limitation may also need to be considered as accommodations.

Paid time off can help here too. A policy that lets employees use a block of PTO for a late start turns a tardy into planned time off.

Sample tardiness policy you can copy

This tardiness policy is written for an hourly team and fits inside a larger attendance policy or the attendance section of our employee handbook template. Replace the text in brackets, and have an employment attorney review it for your state.

Template 1: Tardiness policy with discipline tiers
TARDINESS POLICY [Company name] Effective date: [Month day, year] 1. PURPOSE Our customers and coworkers count on every shift starting on time. This policy explains what counts as late, how lateness is recorded and what happens when it repeats. 2. DEFINITIONS Tardy: Clocking in more than [5] minutes after your scheduled start time, returning more than [5] minutes late from an unpaid meal break, or clocking out more than [5] minutes before your scheduled end time without manager approval. Grace period: The first [5] minutes after your scheduled start time. Arriving within the grace period is not counted as tardy. You are always paid for the time you actually work, including time inside the grace period. Late arrival of [60] minutes or more: Treated as a partial absence under our attendance policy, not as a tardy. 3. HOW TO TELL US YOU WILL BE LATE If you expect to be late, message or call your manager through [the employee app / phone number] as soon as you know, and before your shift starts whenever possible. Telling us early helps us cover your work. A late arrival with notice is still recorded as tardy unless your manager approves a schedule change in advance. 4. PAY Hourly employees are paid from the time they clock in to the time they clock out. [Company name] [pays from actual punch times / rounds punches to the nearest [5] minutes in both directions]. We do not deduct extra time from pay as a penalty for being late. Salaried exempt employees do not have their salary reduced for arriving late. 5. DISCIPLINE STEPS Tardies are counted over a rolling [90]-day period. Each tardy drops off [90] days after it happened. - 3 tardies: Documented coaching conversation with your manager. - 5 tardies: Written warning. - 7 tardies: Final written warning. - 8 tardies: Review for possible termination. Management may skip or repeat steps depending on the circumstances, including how late you were, whether you gave notice and your overall record. 6. TIME THAT IS NOT COUNTED A late arrival is not counted as tardy when it is: - Approved in advance by your manager, including an approved shift swap - Covered by leave protected by law, including FMLA leave and [state / city] paid sick leave - Part of a reasonable accommodation for a disability, pregnancy or religious practice - Caused by a schedule change we made without the notice required by law - Caused by a documented emergency, a severe weather closure or our own systems (for example, a time clock outage) If you believe a late arrival falls into one of these groups, tell your manager or [HR contact name] at [email or phone]. You will not be retaliated against for asking. 7. NO CALL NO SHOW Not arriving for a shift and not contacting us is handled under our no call no show policy, not this policy. 8. RECORDS Your clock-in and clock-out times are recorded by [the time clock / employee app]. You can review your punches in [the employee app]. If a punch is wrong, tell your manager within [2] days so it can be corrected. Questions about this policy go to [HR contact name] at [email or phone]. This policy does not change the at-will nature of employment and does not limit any right you have under federal, state or local law.

A few choices to make before you paste it in. Pick a grace period you can live with; 5 minutes is common. Keep the tiers short enough that they fit on a manager’s cheat sheet. And keep “management may skip or repeat steps” so the policy reads as a guide, not a contract.

Progressive discipline and point systems

There are two common ways to handle tardy employees over time. Most small businesses do best picking one and sticking to it.

Progressive discipline uses steps: coaching, a written warning, a final warning, then termination review. It is flexible, so a manager can treat a 6-minute tardy differently from a 45-minute one. The trade-off is that it depends on each manager’s judgment, so uneven treatment is a risk.

A point system gives each event a value, for example half a point for a tardy, one point for a partial absence and more for a no call no show, with a discipline step at each point total. It is predictable and easy to explain. The trade-off is rigidity: a no-fault point system still has to leave out protected time, as the EEOC and Department of Labor rules above make clear. The attendance policy guide includes a full attendance point system covering absences and tardies together, so you don’t need a second one for lateness.

Whichever you use, a few habits keep it defensible:

  • Write down every tardy the day it happens, with the punch time and any reason the employee gave.
  • Use the same count and the same steps for everyone in the same role.
  • Ask about the reason before each formal step, so protected time is spotted early.
  • Never hold back wages as the discipline. Warnings and schedule changes are fine; unpaid worked time is not.
  • Remember that employment is at will in every state except Montana, but a written policy you ignore can be used against you.

How to talk to an employee who keeps coming in late

Most tardiness is fixed with one honest, private conversation. The goal is to learn the cause, restate the expectation and agree on a plan. Here is a script you can adapt. Theo Park is a fictional opening barista at Northfield Coffee who has been late four times in five weeks.

Template 2: Coaching conversation script for repeated lateness
OPEN "Theo, thanks for making time. I want to talk about start times. The time clock shows you clocked in late on [dates], between [8] and [20] minutes after your 6:00 a.m. start. Does that match what you remember?" ASK WHY, THEN LISTEN "Is something making the 6:00 a.m. start hard right now?" [Listen without interrupting. If the employee mentions a health condition, a family member's medical care, pregnancy, a religious practice or sick time, stop the discipline discussion and say:] "Thanks for telling me. Let's look at whether leave or a schedule change can help. I'll follow up with [HR contact name] and get back to you by [day]." STATE THE IMPACT "When the opener is late, the line backs up and [coworker] runs the bar alone during our busiest half hour. I need the bar staffed at 6:00." RESTATE THE EXPECTATION "Our policy counts a tardy after a [5]-minute grace period. You're at [4] tardies in the last [90] days. At [5], the next step is a written warning." AGREE ON A PLAN "What would help you be here on time? Some options: a later opening shift if one is open, swapping with someone who prefers early starts, or setting a reminder in the app." [Write down what you agree on.] CLOSE "So the plan is [plan]. I'll check in with you on [date]. If something comes up and you'll be late, message me before 6:00 so I can cover the bar. Thanks, Theo." AFTER THE MEETING Write a short note with the date, the dates and times of each tardy, what the employee said, the plan and the follow-up date. Keep it in the employee's file.

Keep the tone factual. Use punch times, not impressions like “you’re always late.” And if a schedule change is the answer, a shift scheduling app with approved swaps makes it easy to give an employee a later start without leaving the morning short.

Frequently asked questions

Can an employer dock pay for being late?

For hourly employees, you can leave the minutes they did not work unpaid, but you must pay every minute they did work. Docking extra time as a penalty, such as 30 minutes for being 10 minutes late, leaves worked time unpaid. Salaried exempt employees generally cannot have their pay cut for arriving late part of a day.

Can you fire an employee for being late?

In most states, yes. Employment is at will everywhere except Montana, so repeated tardiness can be a lawful reason to let someone go. The exceptions are lateness tied to a protected reason, such as FMLA leave, a disability accommodation or protected sick time, and firing that singles out one employee for lateness others get away with.

How many minutes late counts as tardy?

No federal or state law defines it, so your written policy decides. Many employers count an employee as tardy after a grace period of 5 to 7 minutes past the scheduled start. Whatever you pick, apply it the same way to everyone and still pay for every minute worked, including minutes inside the grace period.

Can a salaried employee be docked for coming in late?

Generally no, if the employee is exempt. Under 29 CFR 541.602, an exempt salary cannot be reduced for partial-day absences such as a late start, except for unpaid FMLA leave. Deductions are allowed for full days missed for personal reasons. Regular improper deductions can cost you the exemption and create overtime liability.

Does FMLA leave count against an employee as tardiness?

No. FMLA leave, including intermittent leave taken an hour or two at a time, cannot be counted under a no-fault attendance or point policy. If an employee arrives late because of a certified serious health condition, record that time as FMLA leave rather than a tardy, and do not add points or discipline for it.
