Quick answer

  • A strong self-review lists 3 to 5 specific results with evidence you can point to: dates, counts from your own records, and feedback you received.
  • Name one real area to improve, what you are already doing about it, and the support you want.
  • End with 2 or 3 goals for the next cycle that your manager can check, then edit the phrases below into your own words.

The best self performance review examples have one thing in common: they are specific. “I am a team player” tells your manager nothing. “I covered four extra closing shifts in August when we were short-staffed, and trained Dana on the espresso machine in her first week” tells them exactly what you did. This guide is written for employees. It walks you through gathering evidence, gives you 36 phrases grouped by area, shows three complete self-reviews for real jobs, and ends with notes for the managers who read them.

How to write a self performance review in five steps

Block out 45 minutes. Most people write a weak self-review because they start typing before they remember what they did. Do the steps in this order.

1. Gather your evidence first

Go back through the whole review period, not just the last month. Useful places to look:

  • Your schedule and time records. Shifts you picked up, swaps you covered, how often you were on time. If your employer uses an app, this history is usually there.
  • Messages and emails. Thank-you notes from customers, patients or coworkers, and messages where your manager asked you to handle something new.
  • Last year’s review and goals. Your manager will compare against them, so you should too.
  • Training records. Certifications, courses, new equipment or systems you learned, people you trained.
  • Your own notes. If you do not keep any, start a running list today. Two lines a month is enough to make next year easy.

2. Use numbers you actually have

Numbers make a self-review believable, but only real ones. Use counts you can back up: shifts covered, new hires trained, tickets closed, days the schedule went out on time. If you do not have a number, describe the before and after instead (“the supply closet used to run out of gloves about once a month; since I set up the reorder sheet in March it has not”). Never guess a percentage. If your manager checks one figure and it is wrong, they will doubt the rest.

3. Be specific: situation, action, result

For each accomplishment, write one or two sentences: what was going on, what you did, and what happened. “When the online order tablet went down during the Saturday rush, I switched us to paper tickets and called each order back. We did not lose any orders.” That one sentence does more than a paragraph of adjectives.

4. Own a weakness

Pick one or two real skills you want to improve. Managers trust a self-review more when it is honest about a gap, and naming it yourself lets you frame it as a plan rather than a problem. There are 6 areas of improvement examples below.

5. Set goals you can check

Two or three goals is plenty. Each one should say what you will do and how you and your manager will know it happened. “Get better at inventory” is a wish. “Complete the weekly inventory count on my own by the end of January, with the manager checking the first two” is a goal.

If your company uses a form, answer its questions in its order. If you get a blank page, the structure in the full examples below works for almost any job. For the manager side of the form, see our performance review templates.

Self evaluation examples: 30 phrases by area

Copy a phrase, then replace the brackets with your own details. If a phrase does not match something you really did, skip it. Every block copies as plain text.

Reliability and attendance
1. I worked every scheduled shift this period and was on time for [number] of [number] shifts, based on my time clock record. 2. I picked up [number] open shifts when we were short, including [example, such as two closing shifts during the August staff shortage]. 3. When I needed a shift covered, I arranged the swap through [the app / my manager] at least [number] days ahead, so the schedule was never left with a gap. 4. I completed the [opening / closing] checklist on every shift I worked, and [manager name] did not have to follow up on any missed tasks. 5. I met every deadline for [weekly reports / supply orders / schedule requests] this period, including during [busy season / staff changes]. 6. When I knew I would be late on [date], I called before my shift started and offered to stay late to make up the time.
Customer and patient service
1. I received [number] written compliments from [customers / patients] this period, including one from [brief description] that [manager name] shared with the team. 2. When a [customer / patient] was upset about [situation], I listened, apologized, fixed [what you fixed] and followed up the next day. 3. I learned the names and usual orders of many of our regulars, and I use them to keep the line moving during the morning rush. 4. I handled [number] [phone calls / walk-ins / tickets] a day on average during [period] while keeping [wait times / response times] within our target. 5. I noticed [problem customers kept running into, such as confusion about the new loyalty app] and suggested [change], which [manager name] adopted in [month]. 6. I stay calm with difficult [customers / patients] and know when to bring in a manager, such as [brief example].
Teamwork and communication
1. I trained [number] new team members on [task or station], including [name], who was working [station] on [his / her / their] own within [time]. 2. I covered for [coworker or team] during [situation] so they could [reason], and I made sure the handoff notes were complete. 3. I write clear shift notes, so the next person knows what is low, what broke and what is still open. 4. When [team] disagreed about [issue], I suggested [solution] and we agreed to try it for two weeks. We kept it. 5. I share what I learn: after the [training / vendor visit] I put together a one-page summary for the rest of the team. 6. I ask for help early when I am stuck, rather than letting a task slip past its deadline.
Leadership and initiative
1. I ran [number] shifts as the lead this period, including [situation, such as the Saturday when the manager was out sick]. 2. I created [checklist / guide / process] for [task], and [number] team members now use it. 3. I took ownership of [responsibility, such as weekly supply ordering] in [month] and have not missed an order since. 4. I gave direct, kind feedback to [new team member] when [situation], and followed up a week later to see how it was going. 5. I spotted [risk or problem] before it became a bigger issue and brought it to [manager name] with a suggested fix. 6. I volunteered for [project or task outside my role] and finished it by [date].
Problem-solving and quality
1. When [equipment / system] failed on [date], I [what you did] so we could keep [serving customers / seeing patients / processing orders]. 2. I noticed we were [wasting / running out of / repeating] [item or task] and proposed [change]. Since [month], [observable result]. 3. I double-check [orders / forms / charts] before they go out, and I caught [number or example] errors this period before they reached a [customer / patient / vendor]. 4. I set up [tool, template or folder system] for [task], which cut the time it takes from about [before] to about [after]. 5. When I do not know the answer, I find out who does, and I write the answer down for next time. 6. I keep my [station / work area / files] organized so anyone can step in if I am out.

That is 30 phrases across five areas. The sixth group, growth areas, needs a bit more care, so it gets its own section.

Areas of improvement examples (and how to own a weakness)

This is the part most people dread. Treat it as a chance to steer next year, not a confession. A good growth-area answer has three parts: the gap, one example of it, and what you are doing about it. Ask for something specific in return, such as training, a shadow shift or more feedback.

Pick something real and relevant to your job. If you are in a customer-facing role, “public speaking” probably is not it. Avoid humble brags like “I work too hard” or “I care too much”; managers have heard them many times and they read as dodging the question.

Growth areas: 6 areas of improvement examples
1. Delegating: I tend to do tasks myself during a rush instead of assigning them, which leaves me behind on [lead duties]. I have started assigning stations at the start of each shift. I would like [manager name] to watch one shift and give me feedback. 2. Time management: I missed the [deadline] on [date] because I underestimated how long [task] would take. I now block time on my calendar for it [number] days ahead. 3. Difficult conversations: I avoid telling coworkers when something is not done right. I want to get better at giving feedback in the moment and would like to role-play a few examples with [manager name]. 4. Technical skill: I am still slower than I want to be at [system, equipment or procedure]. I plan to complete [training] by [date] and ask to shadow [coworker] for two shifts. 5. Asking for help: I sometimes wait too long to say I am stuck. Next cycle I will flag any task that is at risk at least [number] days before it is due. 6. Staying organized: My [notes / inbox / files] got behind during [busy period]. I have set a fifteen-minute end-of-shift routine to keep them current.

One rule on time off: you do not need to justify approved PTO, sick days or medical leave in a self-review. Use your time off as you are entitled to under your company’s paid time off policy and leave it out unless it changed a goal, in which case note simply that the goal was adjusted.

Full self assessment examples for three jobs

These three self assessment examples are the most complete self performance review examples in this guide. They use invented people and illustrative numbers. They show how the pieces fit together on one page. Use the structure, not the words: your manager knows how you talk.

Example 1: Shift lead at a coffee shop

Nina Alvarez has worked at Northfield Coffee in Austin, Texas for two years and became a shift lead this spring. Her review covers January through October.

Self-review: shift lead, Northfield Coffee
Name: Nina Alvarez Role: Shift lead (promoted from barista in April) Review period: January 1 to October 31, 2026 WHAT WENT WELL 1. Leading shifts. Since April I have led about three shifts a week, mostly weekend opens. On the two Saturdays the manager was out, I ran the shift on my own, handled the cash count and closed out the drawer with no discrepancies. 2. Training. I trained two new baristas on the espresso bar this year, and I am now training Dana Brooks, who started November 2. I built a one-page drink build sheet that new hires now get on day one. 3. Reliability. I picked up five open shifts in July and August when we were short, and I was on time for every scheduled shift except one in May, when I called ahead. 4. Waste. I noticed we were throwing out oat milk most Mondays. I suggested ordering on Tuesday and Friday instead of Monday only, and since June we have rarely tossed a carton. WHAT I WANT TO IMPROVE Delegating during a rush. When the line gets long, I jump on the bar myself instead of assigning stations, and then I fall behind on breaks and restocking. I have started assigning stations at the start of each shift. I would like feedback on one Saturday shift in November. GOALS FOR NEXT PERIOD 1. Complete the weekly inventory count on my own by the end of January, with the manager checking my first two counts. 2. Run the new-hire training for every barista hired in the first half of 2027, using the drink build sheet and a check-off list. 3. Take the food handler course offered by the company by March 31. SUPPORT I WOULD LIKE Time on the schedule to train new hires without being counted on the bar, and a monthly 15-minute check-in on how I am doing as a lead.

Example 2: Medical assistant at a family clinic

Marcus Hale is a medical assistant at a four-provider family practice. Notice that he describes his work without any patient names or details. Keep patient information out of your self-review entirely; describe the situation in general terms.

Self-review: medical assistant, family clinic
Name: Marcus Hale Role: Medical assistant Review period: November 2025 to October 2026 WHAT WENT WELL 1. Patient flow. I room patients for two providers on most days. Since I started prepping charts the afternoon before, the providers I support have told me the morning schedule starts on time more often. 2. Accuracy. I double-check vitals entries and medication lists before the provider walks in. Twice this year I caught an allergy that was missing from the chart and flagged it before the visit. 3. Supplies. I took over the exam room restocking list in February. I set up a simple reorder sheet by the supply closet, and we have not run out of gloves or exam table paper since. 4. Teamwork. I covered the front desk during lunch on the weeks we were short a receptionist in the spring, and I trained our newest MA on the vaccine logging process. 5. Feedback. Two patients mentioned me by name in comment cards this year, which the office manager shared at a staff meeting. WHAT I WANT TO IMPROVE Phlebotomy confidence on difficult draws. I still hand off hard sticks more often than I would like. I would like to shadow our senior MA on lab mornings for two weeks and complete a refresher course. GOALS FOR NEXT PERIOD 1. Complete a phlebotomy refresher course and the two weeks of shadowing by March. 2. Keep my CPR/BLS certification current and renew it before it expires in May. 3. Write a one-page rooming checklist for new MAs and have it reviewed by the office manager by the end of Q1. SUPPORT I WOULD LIKE Scheduled lab mornings for shadowing, and help from the clinic with the course cost if that is available.

Example 3: Office coordinator

Leah Kim is the office coordinator for a 25-person property management office. Her work is less visible than a front-line role, so she points to processes and deadlines.

Self-review: office coordinator
Name: Leah Kim Role: Office coordinator Review period: January 1 to December 31, 2026 WHAT WENT WELL 1. New-hire setup. I prepared the desk, equipment, building access and first-day schedule for seven new hires this year. Every one had a working laptop and login on day one, which was one of my goals from last year. 2. Vendors. I moved our office supply ordering to one vendor and a monthly order, which cut down rush orders. I keep a shared list so anyone can add requests. 3. Office move. I planned the move from the second to the fourth floor in June: I built the seating plan, coordinated with the movers and IT, and the office reopened on the Monday as planned. 4. Calendar and meetings. I manage the calendars for two directors and book the monthly owner meetings. Neither director missed a meeting due to a scheduling error this year. WHAT I WANT TO IMPROVE Saying no and setting priorities. I take on every request as it comes in, and in the busy months that meant a couple of smaller tasks slipped. I have started a request list sorted by due date and will review it with my manager every Monday. GOALS FOR NEXT PERIOD 1. Write a written onboarding checklist for office setup and share it with hiring managers by February 28. 2. Complete a spreadsheet skills course and use it to build a simple budget tracker for office spending by June 30. 3. Hold a 30-minute monthly check-in with my manager to agree on priorities. SUPPORT I WOULD LIKE Clear guidance on which requests come first when the directors' needs overlap, and time for the spreadsheet course during work hours.

Leah’s first accomplishment ties directly to the company’s employee onboarding process. Linking your work to something the business already cares about is one of the easiest ways to make a self-review land.

What not to write in a self-review

  • Empty adjectives. “Hardworking, dedicated, passionate.” Replace each with an example or delete it.
  • Numbers you cannot back up. “I increased sales 30%” invites the question “compared to what?” If you do not have the data, describe what changed instead.
  • Complaints about coworkers. Your self-review is about your work. Bring conflicts to your manager or HR directly.
  • Blame without ownership. “The schedule was always wrong” reads as an excuse. “I missed two shifts because I misread the schedule; I now check it in the app every Sunday” reads as responsibility.
  • Surprises. If you want a raise, a promotion or a schedule change, say so, but do not let the self-review be the first time your manager hears about it. Mention it in a one-on-one too.
  • Private information. Leave out medical details, patient or customer personal information, and anything a coworker told you in confidence.
  • Copied phrases you did not edit. Managers who read several self-reviews notice when two people use the same sentence. Make every phrase yours.

How managers should use self-reviews

A self-review is the employee’s side of the record. Used well, it makes the review meeting shorter and fairer. A few habits help:

  • Send the form early. Give employees at least a week and the review period dates. Point them to the records they can check, such as their schedule and time history.
  • Read it before you write yours. Employees often remember work their manager never saw, like a closing shift that went wrong and was fixed before morning. Add it to your view.
  • Compare against the same facts. When the schedule, swaps and attendance live in one system, you and the employee are looking at the same shift history, and disagreements become a quick check rather than an argument.
  • Talk about the gaps. Where your rating differs from theirs, ask about it in the meeting. A big gap usually means expectations were never clear, which is a manager problem to fix.
  • Treat protected leave carefully. The Department of Labor’s FMLA regulations at 29 CFR 825.220(c) say employers cannot use the taking of FMLA leave as a negative factor in employment actions, and many state and city sick leave laws protect sick time as well. Our attendance policy guide covers this in more detail. Rate the work, not approved time away.
  • Turn goals into the next review. Copy the employee’s goals into their record and check them at your next one-on-one, not 12 months later.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a self performance review be?

For most hourly and office roles, one page is enough: three to five accomplishments with evidence, one or two areas to improve, and two or three goals. Answer every question on your company's form, but keep each answer to a short paragraph. Your manager will read it closely, so specific beats long.

Should I rate myself high or low on a self-evaluation?

Rate yourself where your evidence puts you. If you met every expectation and exceeded one, say exactly that and point to the proof. Rating yourself low to look modest can anchor the conversation downward, and rating yourself top marks everywhere without examples makes the rest of your review easier to dismiss.

What weakness should I put in a self-review?

Pick a real skill gap that matters for your job and that you can improve within a review cycle, such as delegating, handling upset customers or keeping notes up to date. Describe one example, what you have already started doing about it, and the help you would like. Avoid fake weaknesses like working too hard.

Can I mention a coworker in my self-review?

Yes, when you are giving credit or describing shared work. Saying that you and a teammate rebuilt the opening checklist together is fair and shows teamwork. Do not use your self-review to complain about a coworker. Raise conflicts directly with your manager or HR, where they can be handled properly.

Should time off or medical leave appear in my self-review?

You do not need to explain approved time off or medical leave. Under the federal FMLA regulations, employers cannot use FMLA leave as a negative factor in employment actions, and many state sick leave laws protect sick time too. If leave affected a goal, you can simply note the goal was adjusted.
