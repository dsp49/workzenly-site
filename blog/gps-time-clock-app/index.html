Quick answer

  • A GPS time clock app records where each clock-in and clock-out happens and checks it against a geofence around the job site, so you know punches came from work.
  • When you compare apps, look at geofence radius control, how flagged punches are handled, a shared tablet option for no-phone sites, manager alerts, payroll handoff, edit history and when location is read.
  • Before rollout, give written notice (required in New York, Connecticut and Delaware), check whether you owe phone reimbursement (California and Illinois), and pay for time spent on app problems.

A GPS time clock app turns each employee’s phone, or a shared tablet at the site, into a time clock that also notes where the punch happened. For a crew that starts at a different address every week, that replaces paper cards and “I got there at 7, I promise” texts with a record a manager can check. This guide covers what a GPS time clock does, who gets the most out of one, a buying checklist, a comparison of the main approaches, the honest downsides, and the US legal points to settle before you switch.

What a GPS time clock app does

Strip away the marketing and a GPS clock in app does three jobs.

  • Stamps location on each punch. When someone taps clock in or clock out, the app reads the phone’s location and saves it with the time. A manager reviewing the timesheet can see where each punch happened.
  • Checks the punch against a geofence. A geofence is a boundary drawn on a map around the site. If the phone is inside, the punch goes through. If it is outside, the app blocks it, flags it, or asks for a note, depending on your settings. Our guide what is a geofence explains how the boundary and the phone’s location work together.
  • Keeps a list of job sites. Multi-site teams need one geofence per location: each store, each client’s house, each active construction job. Good apps let a manager add a site in a minute and retire it when the job ends.

From there, the punches feed the same things any time clock feeds: hours, breaks, overtime and payroll. A mobile time clock does not change the pay math. It changes how much you can trust the start and end times going into it.

Who needs a GPS time clock

A wall-mounted clock works when everyone starts in the same building. A GPS time clock earns its keep when they do not.

  • Construction and trades crews. Workers report straight to the job site, which moves. Crews often clock in at the truck or the gate, so the fence has to cover the parking area, not just the building pad.
  • Field service. HVAC, plumbing, pest control, landscaping and cleaning teams visit several addresses a day or start from home. The punch location shows the first stop of the day.
  • Home care and home health. Caregivers clock in at a client’s home. A geofence around each client address supports visit records. Note that agencies billing Medicaid for personal care or home health services also face federal electronic visit verification (EVV) rules run through each state, which can have their own requirements for how visits are recorded, so check your state Medicaid agency before choosing a tool for that purpose.
  • Multi-location retail and restaurants. Staff who float between stores need to clock in at whichever one they are working today, and managers need to see which store the hours belong to.

If your whole team works in one shop with a manager at the door, a shared tablet clock may be all you need. GPS matters most where nobody from management is there when the shift starts.

GPS time clock buying checklist

Use this list on every demo call. Ask the vendor to show each item on screen, not just say yes.

  1. Geofence radius control. Can you set the radius per site, and change it? Android’s developer documentation recommends a minimum geofence radius of 100 to 150 meters (about 330 to 490 feet) for reliable results. A store in a strip mall, a downtown office and a rural job site need different settings.
  2. What happens outside the fence. Can you choose between blocking the punch, allowing it with a flag, or asking for a note? For new sites, flag first and block later.
  3. Accuracy handling. Does the app record the phone’s accuracy estimate with the punch, so a manager can tell “a block away with poor signal” from “across town”?
  4. Offline punches. Basements, steel buildings and rural sites lose signal. Ask whether a punch made with no connection is saved with the original time and synced later, and how it is marked.
  5. Shared kiosk option. Some sites ban phones on the floor and some employees do not want the app on a personal phone. A shared tablet at the entrance covers both.
  6. Manager alerts. Does a manager see who is late, who has not clocked in, and which punches were flagged, without opening every timesheet?
  7. Breaks and overtime. Are meal breaks and overtime worked out from the punches, using your state’s rules? Check a few results against our time card calculator during the trial.
  8. Payroll handoff. Do approved hours go straight into payroll, or do you export a file and re-key it? Every re-key is a chance for error.
  9. Edit history. When a manager fixes a missed punch, is the original kept with who changed it, when and why? You need that if a pay dispute or a Department of Labor audit comes up.
  10. Privacy controls. Is location read only at the punch, or during the shift as well? Who can see punch locations, and how long are they kept? The answer belongs in your employee notice.

Honest trade-offs of GPS clock-ins

A GPS time clock solves real problems, but it brings its own. Plan for these before employees find them for you.

  • Accuracy indoors. GPS.gov, the US government’s GPS site, says GPS-enabled smartphones are typically accurate to within a 4.9 meter (16 foot) radius under open sky, and that accuracy worsens near buildings, bridges and trees. Indoors, phones fall back on Wi-Fi, which Android’s documentation puts at roughly 20 to 50 meters, and in rural areas without Wi-Fi, cell location can be off by several hundred meters or more. An honest employee at the back door can land outside a tight fence.
  • Battery and data. Reading location once per punch costs very little. Apps that read location throughout the shift use more battery and data, which employees notice on their own phones.
  • Phones and permissions. If location services are off or the app lacks permission, it cannot check the fence. Older phones and full storage cause their own problems. Decide in advance what an employee should do: clock in at the tablet, or text a manager.
  • Spoofing. Apps that fake a phone’s location exist. A geofence makes casual time theft harder, but schedules, job records and a manager’s knowledge still matter.
  • Employee trust. Many workers accept a location check at the punch and object strongly to being followed after hours. Telling people exactly what the app records, and sticking to it, does more for adoption than any feature.

Here is an illustrative example. A 9-person roofing crew in Austin sets a 150-meter fence around each house it works on and allows outside punches with a flag. In the first week, two punches are flagged: one from a worker parked down the street because the driveway was full, and one from a coffee shop a mile away. The manager approves the first, talks to the second employee, and widens the fence on the next job to cover street parking. Nobody’s pay was held up and the fence got better.

Legal points before you roll out a GPS time clock

Location at clock-in is common and generally lawful in the US, but several rules apply to how you do it. This is general information, not legal advice.

Notice and consent laws

  • New York. Civil Rights Law section 52-c, in effect since May 7, 2022, requires employers with a place of business in the state that monitor employee phone, email or internet use to give written or electronic notice at hiring, get an acknowledgment, and post the notice. The Attorney General enforces it, with civil penalties of up to $500 for a first offense, $1,000 for a second and $3,000 for each later one. Cover location at punch in the same notice.
  • Connecticut. General Statutes section 31-48d requires prior written notice of electronic monitoring of employees, which can be given by posting a notice. Public Act 26-73, effective October 1, 2026, revised the statute; check the Connecticut Department of Labor for the current requirements.
  • Delaware. Title 19, section 705 of the Delaware Code requires notice before monitoring employee phone, email or internet use, either daily or as a one-time notice the employee acknowledges. The civil penalty is $100 per violation.
  • California. The California Consumer Privacy Act covers employee data at businesses that meet its thresholds, and treats precise geolocation as sensitive personal information. Covered employers owe a notice at collection.

Even where no statute requires it, a written notice with a signed acknowledgment is the best protection you have. Say what the app records, when, who sees it and how long you keep it. Our employee handbook template has a timekeeping section you can adapt.

Personal phones and expense reimbursement

Asking employees to clock in on their own phones (bring your own device, or BYOD) can create a reimbursement duty.

  • California. Labor Code section 2802 requires employers to indemnify employees for all necessary expenditures or losses incurred in doing their jobs. In Cochran v. Schwan’s Home Service (2014), a California Court of Appeal held that when employees are required to use personal cell phones for work, the employer must reimburse a reasonable percentage of the bill, even if the employee has an unlimited plan.
  • Illinois. Since January 1, 2019, section 9.5 of the Illinois Wage Payment and Collection Act requires employers to reimburse necessary expenditures that primarily benefit the employer. Employment lawyers commonly read it to cover required phone and data use; a written reimbursement policy limits what you owe.
  • Federal minimum wage. Under 29 CFR 531.35, wages must be paid “free and clear.” If an employer requires workers to supply tools of the trade and the cost cuts into minimum or overtime wages in a workweek, that violates the Fair Labor Standards Act.

Other states have reimbursement rules too. The simplest fixes are a modest monthly phone stipend, paid through payroll, or a shared tablet so the personal phone is optional.

Paying for time spent on app problems

The Department of Labor’s rule is that work not requested but suffered or permitted is work time, and 29 CFR 785.11 says time counts when the employer knows or has reason to believe the employee is working. A worker who spends 15 minutes on site fighting a frozen app before the punch goes through has been working. So has one who downloads the app and sets it up at your request. Pay for that time, and give managers an easy way to add a missed punch. A failed geofence check is a reason to review a punch, never a reason to skip paying for hours someone actually worked. A rollout plan for your GPS time clock

  1. Week 0: write the policy. Decide when location is read (we suggest punches only), who sees it, how long you keep it, what happens to a flagged punch, and the alternative for people without a phone. Add phone reimbursement if your state requires it.
  2. Set up sites. Add every location with the fence centered on the entrance staff actually use, covering the parking area. Put a shared tablet at any site where phones are banned or signal is poor.
  3. Give notice and collect acknowledgments. Hand out the written notice, post it where your state requires, and get each employee’s signature or electronic acknowledgment before the first punch.
  4. Run a pilot. Start with one crew or one store for two pay periods with outside punches flagged, not blocked. Keep the old method running in parallel and compare totals.
  5. Tune and expand. Widen fences that flag honest punches, fix permission problems, and only then switch hard blocks on where they make sense. Roll out site by site.
  6. Review monthly. Look at flagged punches, manual edits and complaints. A rising edit count usually means a fence or a device problem, not a people problem.

Frequently asked questions

What is a GPS time clock app?

A GPS time clock app is a mobile time clock that records where an employee is when they clock in and out. Most compare that location with a geofence drawn around each work site, then allow the punch, block it or flag it for a manager. The punches become the timesheet used for payroll.

Does a GPS time clock app track employees all day?

Not necessarily. Many apps read location only at the moment of clock-in and clock-out. Some products also offer tracking during the shift as a separate option. Ask each vendor exactly when location is read, choose the narrowest setting that meets your needs, and put that answer in your written employee notice.

Do I have to pay employees for using their own phone to clock in?

It depends on your state. California Labor Code section 2802 requires employers to reimburse necessary work expenses, and a 2014 California appeals court decision applied it to a reasonable share of personal cell phone bills. Illinois has a similar reimbursement law. Federal law also bars costs that push pay below minimum wage.

What if an employee has no smartphone?

Offer another way to clock in. A shared tablet at the site entrance lets employees punch without a personal phone, and it also works where phones are not allowed on the floor. Do not make owning a smartphone a condition of being paid correctly, and write the alternative into your timekeeping policy.

How accurate is a GPS time clock?

Under open sky, GPS.gov says GPS-enabled smartphones are typically accurate to within about 16 feet. Indoors, underground or between tall buildings, phones rely on Wi-Fi and cell signals and the error grows. Set a geofence that allows for that error and review flagged punches instead of rejecting them automatically.
