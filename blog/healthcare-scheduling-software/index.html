Quick answer

  • This guide covers staff scheduling (who works which shift), not patient appointment booking, which lives in your practice management or EHR system.
  • Good healthcare scheduling software covers nights and weekends, checks that licenses and certifications are current before anyone is scheduled, and ties shifts to the time clock and payroll.
  • The pay rules are where schedules get expensive: on-call pay in the regular rate, the 8/80 overtime option, state limits on mandatory nurse overtime and paid travel time between home care clients.

Healthcare scheduling software, in this guide, means the tool that decides which nurses, aides, medical assistants, techs and front-desk staff work which shifts. It is for clinics, home care agencies, assisted living and other small healthcare employers. It is not patient appointment scheduling, which books visits and usually sits inside a practice management or EHR system. Clinics often need both, and they are separate purchases. Here is what the staff side has to do, the US pay and labor rules a healthcare schedule sets off, and how to compare the options.

Healthcare staff scheduling, not patient booking

A healthcare staff scheduling tool answers four questions every day: who is on, are they allowed to be on, what will it cost, and who covers the gap. The first is the schedule itself. The second is credentials: a nurse with a lapsed license or an aide with an expired CPR card should not be on the floor. The third is pay: nights, on-call days and long weeks all change what a shift costs. The fourth is what makes or breaks a clinic at 6 a.m. when someone calls out.

A generic shift app handles the first and part of the fourth. The healthcare-specific work is the rest. It is also why the most useful tools for small employers connect the schedule to the time clock and payroll, so the hours people actually worked, not the hours they were scheduled for, drive overtime and pay.

24/7 coverage, 12-hour shifts and float pools

Clinics with set hours can schedule like an office. Residential care, urgent care and home care agencies with overnight clients cannot. Covering every hour of every day usually means one of three patterns:

  • Three 8-hour shifts (for example 7 a.m. to 3 p.m., 3 p.m. to 11 p.m. and 11 p.m. to 7 a.m.). Easy to staff part time and the natural fit for the 8/80 overtime system covered below.
  • Two 12-hour shifts on a fixed rotation. The Pitman schedule repeats every two weeks (two on, two off, three on, then the reverse), and the DuPont schedule rotates day and night 12-hour shifts over four weeks. Both average 42 hours a week, so some weeks run over 40.
  • Three 12-hour shifts a week per nurse, a common pattern in nursing, with the days chosen each cycle rather than fixed.

Nurse scheduling software should let you build a repeating pattern once and roll it forward, instead of rebuilding every week. It should also show which upcoming weeks put someone over 40 hours, because a rotation that averages 42 still has 48-hour weeks in it.

Then there is cover. Many employers keep a float pool: staff who are qualified for more than one unit or location and fill gaps where they appear. In software terms, that means each person can hold several roles or locations, open shifts can be posted to the people qualified for them, and a manager can see who has the hours to spare before calling. For a small home care agency, the float pool may be three caregivers who will take a short-notice visit. The principle is the same: post the open shift to the right people and keep a record of who took it.

Credentials before the schedule

In healthcare, being available is not enough. Before anyone is scheduled, the license, certification or training the role requires must be current. Typical items include RN and LPN licenses, CNA or home health aide certification, BLS or CPR cards, TB tests and any state-required training for the role. The rules come from your state licensing boards, your payers and your own policies, so the list differs by employer.

Clinic scheduling gets this wrong in two ways. Expiration dates live in a binder or an HR folder the scheduler never sees, or they live in the scheduler's head. Either way, the problem is found after the shift, not before. When you compare tools, ask three things:

  1. Where are certifications stored, and are they attached to each person's record?
  2. Does the tool show expiration dates, and does it warn anyone before a date passes?
  3. Can it stop or flag a shift assignment when a required credential is missing or expired?

Note the difference between storing a credential and verifying it. Primary-source verification with a state board is a separate process, and larger organizations often use dedicated credentialing software for it. A small clinic usually needs the simpler version: the right documents on file and someone told before they expire.

On-call pay, 8/80 overtime and other pay rules

A healthcare schedule is also a payroll decision. These are the federal and state rules that come up most.

On-call time and on-call pay

Under the FLSA regulation at 29 CFR 785.17, an employee who must stay on call at the facility, or so close to it that they cannot use the time for their own purposes, is working. An employee who only has to be reachable by phone is generally not working while waiting, though any calls handled and trips in are hours worked. Your state may be stricter.

Flat on-call payments are a separate question. 29 CFR 778.223 says they must be included in the regular rate, which raises the overtime rate. An illustrative example: a CNA earns $20.00 an hour, works 44 hours and has two on-call days at $25.00 each.

  • Straight-time pay: 44 × $20.00 = $880.00, plus $50.00 of on-call pay.
  • Regular rate: $930.00 ÷ 44 = about $21.136 an hour.
  • Overtime premium: 4 hours × $21.136 × 0.5 = $42.27.
  • Total: $880.00 + $50.00 + $42.27 = $972.27.

Leaving the on-call pay out of the rate would pay $40.00 of premium instead of $42.27. Small per week, but it is a classic audit finding. Our shift differential guide covers night, weekend and on-call premiums in more detail, and how to calculate overtime walks through the regular rate step by step.

The 8/80 system for hospitals and residential care

Under 29 CFR 778.601, an employer operating a hospital or a residential care establishment may use a fixed 14-day period instead of the 7-day workweek, if the employer and employee agree before the work is done. Overtime at time and a half is then owed for hours over 8 in any workday and over 80 in the 14-day period. The agreement does not have to be written, but if it is not, the employer must keep a record of it.

8/80 helps uneven 8-hour schedules and hurts 12-hour ones. Two illustrative examples for a nurse at $30.00 an hour over two weeks:

8-hour shifts: 6 days in week one (48 hours), 4 in week two (32 hours)8 overtime hours: $2,400.00 + $120.00 = $2,520.00No day over 8, total of 80: $2,400.00

So a facility running 12-hour shifts usually stays on the standard workweek. California adds its own version of 8/80 under Wage Order 5 and a separate 12-hour alternative workweek for healthcare; see our California overtime law guide.

Sleep time on 24-hour shifts

For duty of 24 hours or more, 29 CFR 785.22 lets the employer and employee agree to exclude a bona fide sleep period of up to 8 hours, if the employer provides adequate sleeping facilities and the employee can usually get an uninterrupted night's sleep. Interruptions count as hours worked, and if the employee cannot get at least 5 hours of sleep, the whole period counts. Shifts under 24 hours are all hours worked, even if the employee is allowed to sleep.

California's health care minimum wage

California sets a separate minimum wage for covered health care facilities under SB 525, and it applies to support staff such as clerks and food service workers at those facilities, not only clinical staff. From July 1, 2026, the Department of Industrial Relations lists rates from $19.28 to $25.00 an hour depending on the facility category, against a state minimum of $16.90. Our California minimum wage guide has the full table. If your tool builds schedules with cost estimates, make sure each role's rate reflects it.

State limits on mandatory overtime for nurses

Federal law does not cap how many hours an adult can work. Many states, though, restrict mandatory overtime for nurses or other health care staff, which changes how you fill a gap. Three examples, as described by each state:

  • New York: Labor Law Section 167 bars covered health care employers, such as hospitals, nursing homes and clinics licensed under Article 28 of the Public Health Law, from requiring RNs and LPNs to work beyond their regularly scheduled hours, except in a health care disaster, a declared emergency, an unforeseen emergency or an ongoing procedure. The New York State Department of Labor says employers must first make a good-faith effort to cover the hours voluntarily, follow their nurse coverage plan and report use of the exceptions.
  • Washington: RCW 49.28.140 says no employee of a health care facility may be required to work overtime, with exceptions for unforeseeable emergencies, prescheduled on-call time, documented reasonable staffing efforts and finishing a patient care procedure already underway. Prescheduled on-call time cannot be used in place of regularly scheduled shifts, and filling vacancies caused by chronic short staffing does not count as a reasonable effort.
  • Pennsylvania: Act 102, the Prohibition of Excessive Overtime in Health Care Act, generally bars covered facilities from requiring employees to work beyond their agreed, predetermined and regularly scheduled shifts, with limited exceptions. The Department of Labor and Industry says on-call time cannot be used as a substitute for mandatory overtime.

Other states have their own rules, and coverage, exceptions and the treatment of on-call time differ. Check your state labor department or nursing board. For software, the practical point is the record: you want to see that an extra shift was offered, who accepted it, and when, so voluntary overtime can be shown to be voluntary. Chicago's fair workweek ordinance also covers healthcare employers above its size and pay thresholds; our scheduling page summarizes those laws.

Home care scheduling: travel time and the companionship exemption

Home care agencies schedule visits rather than shifts, and two federal rules shape the cost.

Travel between clients is hours worked. The Department of Labor's Fact Sheet #79D says travel from one client to another during the workday is compensable, and the agency must make sure it is paid. Ordinary travel from the aide's home to the first client and back from the last is not. A schedule that packs five visits across town into a day also packs in paid drive time, and it counts toward the 40 hours for overtime. Good home care scheduling groups visits by area and shows travel time between them.

The companionship exemption is in flux. Since a 2013 Department of Labor rule took effect on January 1, 2015, third-party employers such as home care agencies could not claim the FLSA companionship services or live-in exemptions, so their aides were owed minimum wage and overtime. In July 2025 the Department proposed returning to the earlier 1975 rules, and its Field Assistance Bulletin 2025-4 told investigators to stop enforcing the 2013 rule against agencies while that proposal is pending. As of this update, the 2013 text is still in the regulation at 29 CFR 552.109, and the bulletin says it does not change any legal requirement. Many states also require overtime for home care workers on their own. Talk to an employment attorney before you change how you pay overtime, and check dol.gov for a final rule.

Medicaid visits need EVV. Under the 21st Century Cures Act, states must use electronic visit verification for Medicaid personal care and home health services that involve an in-home visit. EVV records the service, the client, the caregiver, the date, the location and the start and end times. Most states run or approve specific EVV systems, so check your state Medicaid agency before assuming any scheduling or time clock app will satisfy it.

Keep patient health information out of the staff schedule

A staff schedule needs shifts, roles, locations and credentials. It does not need diagnoses, room-by-room patient assignments with names, or visit notes. Keeping protected health information out of the scheduling tool keeps that tool out of most of your HIPAA work.

If a tool will hold patient information, HIPAA requires a covered entity to have a business associate agreement with the vendor before sharing it, according to the Department of Health and Human Services. For home care, where the schedule is a list of visits, use client codes or first names and a general area, as your privacy officer allows, and keep addresses and care plans in your clinical or agency system. Ask every vendor directly whether their product is designed to hold patient data and whether they sign business associate agreements, and get the answer in writing.

Buying checklist for healthcare staff scheduling

Use this list on any tool you trial, including ours. Ask the vendor to build one of your real two-week schedules in the demo.

  1. Your pattern, rolled forward. Can it build 8-hour, 12-hour and rotating schedules such as Pitman or DuPont once and repeat them?
  2. Credentials attached to people. Are licenses and certifications stored with each person, with expiration dates someone will see before a shift is assigned?
  3. Overtime seen before it happens. Does it show who will pass 40 hours (or 8 and 80, if you use that system) while you build, not on payday?
  4. Open shifts to qualified staff. Can you post a gap to the float pool or the people qualified for that role, and see who accepted?
  5. Swaps with a record. Is every swap and extra shift logged with who asked, who agreed and when? That record matters where mandatory overtime is restricted.
  6. Time off in view. Is approved PTO visible where you build shifts, so nobody is scheduled on a day off?
  7. Connected to the clock. Do staff clock in against the shift, by phone with GPS or at a shared tablet, so late arrivals and no-shows show up straight away?
  8. Pay rules handled. Do on-call pay and differentials feed the regular rate, and do worked hours flow into payroll without retyping?
  9. No patient data required. Does the tool work without patient information, and if not, will the vendor sign a business associate agreement?
  10. Home care needs. If you run visits, does it account for travel time between clients, and does it meet your state's EVV requirements or work alongside the system that does?

Frequently asked questions

Is healthcare scheduling software the same as patient scheduling software?

No. Patient scheduling software books appointments and is usually part of a practice management or EHR system. Healthcare staff scheduling software decides which nurses, aides, techs and front-desk staff work which shifts, and ties those shifts to time records, credentials, overtime and payroll. Many clinics need both, and they are usually bought separately.

Do nurses get paid for on-call time?

It depends on the restrictions. Under the FLSA, on-call time is hours worked if the employee must stay on the premises or so close that they cannot use the time for themselves. Being reachable by phone from home is usually not hours worked, but any calls answered or trips in are. Flat on-call pay must be included in the regular rate.

What is the 8/80 overtime system?

It is an FLSA option for hospitals and residential care establishments. By agreement made before the work, the employer uses a fixed 14-day period instead of a 7-day workweek and pays time and a half for hours over 8 in a day and over 80 in the period. It suits 8-hour shifts, not 12-hour shifts.

Do home care aides get paid for travel between clients?

Yes. The Department of Labor says travel from one client's home to another during the workday is hours worked, and the agency must pay for it. The commute from the aide's own home to the first client and back from the last is ordinary commuting and generally not paid, unless state law or your policy says otherwise.

Can a hospital or clinic require nurses to work overtime?

Federal law does not limit overtime hours for adults, but many states restrict mandatory overtime for nurses. New York, Washington and Pennsylvania, for example, generally bar covered facilities from requiring work beyond scheduled shifts except in emergencies or similar exceptions. Rules on coverage and on-call differ, so check your state agency before you rely on mandatory overtime.

Should a staff schedule include patient names?

Keep them out where you can. A staff schedule needs shifts, roles, locations and credentials, not diagnoses or treatment details. If any tool will hold protected health information, HIPAA requires a business associate agreement with the vendor first. For home care, use client codes or first names only, as your privacy officer allows.
