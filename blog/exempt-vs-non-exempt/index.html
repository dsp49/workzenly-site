Exempt vs non-exempt employees: tests, salary thresholds and examples

Who gets overtime and who does not comes down to three tests. Here is how each one works, the salary figures in force today, and how common jobs usually come out.

Quick answer

  • Non-exempt employees get overtime at 1.5× their regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek. Exempt employees do not.
  • To be exempt, an employee must be paid a fixed salary, at or above the salary threshold, and do work that fits an FLSA exemption. Job title does not count.
  • The federal threshold in force on November 4, 2026 is $684 a week ($35,568 a year). California, New York, Washington, Colorado, Alaska and Maine require more.

Exempt vs non exempt is the line between employees who are owed overtime and those who are not. Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), every employee is non-exempt by default. An employer can treat someone as exempt only if the job passes three tests: the salary basis test, the salary level test and a duties test. Getting it wrong is one of the most common and expensive payroll mistakes a small business can make, because the unpaid overtime builds up every week until someone notices.

Non exempt vs exempt: what the terms mean

A non-exempt employee is covered by the FLSA’s minimum wage and overtime rules. They must earn at least the federal minimum wage of $7.25 an hour (or the higher state or local rate) and be paid time and a half for every hour over 40 in a workweek. Non-exempt employees can be paid by the hour, by salary, by the piece or by commission. What makes them non-exempt is that their hours are counted and overtime is owed.

An exempt employee is excluded from those minimum wage and overtime rules because the job meets one of the FLSA exemptions. They receive the same salary whether they work 35 hours or 55 in a week. Exempt status is a legal conclusion about a specific job, not a perk, a title or a pay grade.

Two points trip up many employers. First, “salaried” and “exempt” are not the same thing; a salaried assistant manager who spends most of the day on the register is usually non-exempt. Second, exemption also differs from contractor status, which is a separate question about whether someone is an employee at all. Our guide to 1099 vs W-2 worker classification covers that one.

The three tests for an exempt employee

The U.S. Department of Labor’s Wage and Hour Division sets out the tests in 29 CFR Part 541 and explains them in its Fact Sheet #17 series. An employee must pass all three.

  1. Salary basis test. The employee receives a predetermined amount each pay period, weekly or less often, that is not reduced because of the quality or quantity of the work. In general, an exempt employee must get the full salary for any week in which they do any work, no matter how many days or hours they worked.
  2. Salary level test. The salary must be at least the minimum set by federal law, and by state law where that is higher. Up to 10% of the federal standard salary level can come from non-discretionary bonuses and commissions paid at least once a year.
  3. Duties test. The employee’s primary duty, meaning the main or most important work, must fit one of the exemptions below. The Department of Labor looks at the actual work, not the job description.

Fact Sheet #17G lists the only deductions an employer may make from an exempt salary: full-day absences for personal reasons, full-day absences for sickness under a bona fide sick-pay plan, unpaid disciplinary suspensions of one or more full days for workplace conduct violations, penalties for major safety violations, and offsets for jury fees, witness fees or military pay. You also do not have to pay a full week’s salary in the first or last week of employment, or for unpaid FMLA leave. Docking half a day because someone left early for an appointment is not allowed.

An employer with an “actual practice” of improper deductions can lose the exemption for every employee in the same job. The rule includes a safe harbor: if you have a clearly communicated policy against improper deductions with a way to complain, reimburse any improper deduction, and commit to complying, you keep the exemption unless you willfully keep making the deductions.

The federal salary threshold in 2026

On November 4, 2026, the Department of Labor enforces a standard salary threshold of $684 a week, or $35,568 a year, for the executive, administrative and professional exemptions. The highly compensated employee threshold is $107,432 a year, including at least $684 a week paid on a salary or fee basis. These are the figures from the 2019 rule.

Here is how the rules got back there. In 2024 the Department of Labor issued a rule raising the threshold to $844 a week from July 1, 2024 and to $1,128 a week from January 1, 2025, with automatic updates every three years. In November 2024, a federal court in Texas vacated the entire rule, and employers returned to the 2019 levels. In May 2026, the Department published a technical amendment in the Federal Register that removed the vacated 2024 text from 29 CFR Part 541 and restored the 2019 regulations. The amendment set no new threshold and did not change how the Wage and Hour Division enforces the rules.

We found no proposed rule on a new federal salary level published as of November 4, 2026. The Department has not ruled out future rulemaking, so check the Wage and Hour Division’s salary levels page before you set salaries for 2027.

FLSA exemptions by type

These are the main FLSA exemptions that small businesses use. Each one has its own duties test, described in Fact Sheet #17A.

  • Executive. The primary duty is managing the business or a recognized department or subdivision. The employee customarily and regularly directs the work of at least two full-time employees or the equivalent, and has authority to hire or fire, or their recommendations on hiring, firing and promotion carry particular weight.
  • Administrative. The primary duty is office or non-manual work directly related to the management or general business operations of the employer or its customers, and includes exercising discretion and independent judgment on matters of significance. Think HR managers, accountants who make real decisions, and purchasing managers, not clerks who follow set procedures.
  • Learned professional. The primary duty requires advanced knowledge in a field of science or learning, usually acquired through a prolonged course of specialized study. Examples include registered nurses, pharmacists, engineers and CPAs. Licensed doctors, lawyers and teachers are exempt without any salary test.
  • Creative professional. The primary duty requires invention, imagination, originality or talent in a recognized artistic or creative field, such as music, writing, acting or graphic arts.
  • Computer employee. Systems analysts, programmers, software engineers and similarly skilled workers whose primary duty is systems analysis, design, development or testing of programs or systems. Federally, they can be paid a salary of at least $684 a week or an hourly rate of at least $27.63. The help desk technician who resets passwords usually does not qualify.
  • Outside sales. The primary duty is making sales or obtaining orders or contracts, and the employee is customarily and regularly away from the employer’s place of business. There is no federal salary test. Inside sales staff who work from the office or by phone do not qualify.
  • Highly compensated employee (HCE). An office or non-manual worker earning at least $107,432 a year in total pay is exempt if they customarily and regularly perform at least one exempt executive, administrative or professional duty. Some states, including California, do not follow this exemption.

The Department of Labor is clear that these exemptions do not cover manual or blue-collar workers, such as carpenters, electricians, mechanics and laborers, however much they earn. Police officers, firefighters, paramedics and similar first responders are also not exempt, regardless of rank or pay.

Misclassification risks

Classifying a non-exempt employee as exempt means you have not paid overtime that was owed. Under the FLSA, an employee can recover back wages for two years, or three years if the violation was willful, plus an equal amount as liquidated damages and their attorney fees. State laws can add penalties, interest and longer lookback periods.

The cost grows quickly. A misclassified assistant manager earning $40,000 a year who works 50 hours a week has a regular rate of about $15.38 if the salary is treated as covering all 50 hours ($40,000 ÷ 52 ÷ 50). Each of the 10 overtime hours then earns an extra half-time premium, about $76.92 a week. If the salary was meant to cover only 40 hours, the bill is higher. Over two years that is roughly $8,000 in back wages, and about $16,000 with liquidated damages, for one person. If the same title covers five people, so does the claim.

The most common causes are paying “salaried” staff no overtime because they are salaried, promoting a crew member to a manager title without changing their duties, letting a salary fall below a state threshold after the state minimum wage rises in January, and docking exempt pay for partial days.

What employers should do

An exempt vs non exempt review does not need a consultant to get started. Work through these steps once a year and whenever a job changes.

  1. List every salaried role. For each one, write down the salary and the primary duty in plain words, based on what the person actually does.
  2. Check the salary level for every state you employ in. Use the higher of the federal and state figure. Put a reminder on your calendar for each January and, in Alaska, each July, when thresholds tied to minimum wage change.
  3. Match the duties to a specific exemption. If you cannot name the exemption and say why it fits, treat the job as non-exempt.
  4. Write a no-improper-deductions policy. Put it in the handbook with a way to raise complaints. This keeps the safe harbor available.
  5. Track hours for every non-exempt employee. Use a time clock app so overtime is paid on real punches. Our guide on how to calculate overtime covers salaried non-exempt pay and bonuses.
  6. When you reclassify, do it cleanly. Tell the employee in writing, start tracking time from the effective date, and get advice from an employment attorney on whether back pay is owed.

Frequently asked questions

What is the federal salary threshold for exempt employees in 2026?

The U.S. Department of Labor enforces a standard salary level of $684 a week, or $35,568 a year, for the executive, administrative and professional exemptions. The highly compensated employee threshold is $107,432 a year. A court vacated the 2024 increase, and a May 2026 technical amendment formally restored the 2019 figures.

Can an hourly employee be exempt?

Usually not. Most exemptions require a fixed salary. The main exception is computer professionals, who can be exempt at $27.63 an hour or more under federal law, with much higher hourly floors in states such as California and Washington. Doctors, lawyers and teachers are also exempt without a salary test.

Do exempt employees have to track their hours?

Federal law does not require hour-by-hour records for exempt employees, but many employers still track attendance, PTO and project time. Tracking hours does not destroy an exemption. What can destroy it is docking salary for partial-day absences or paying a different amount based on hours worked.

Can I make a salaried employee non-exempt?

Yes. Any employee can be paid a salary and still be non-exempt. You then owe overtime at 1.5 times the regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek, and state daily overtime where it applies. You must track their hours accurately, just as you would for an hourly employee.

What happens if I misclassify an employee as exempt?

You can owe the unpaid overtime for up to two years, or three if the violation was willful, plus an equal amount in liquidated damages and the employee’s attorney fees under the FLSA. State laws can add penalties, interest and longer lookback periods, and one misclassified role often affects everyone in it.
