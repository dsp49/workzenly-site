Employer payroll taxes in 2026: what you pay on top of wages

Every paycheck costs you more than the gross pay on it. Here are the 2026 rates, a worked example for one hourly employee, a calculator, and the deposit and filing deadlines that come with them.

Quick answer

  • Federal employer payroll taxes in 2026: 6.2% Social Security on wages up to $184,500, 1.45% Medicare on all wages, and 0.6% FUTA on the first $7,000 per employee ($42 a year).
  • State unemployment tax (SUTA) is on top, at a rate your state assigns on a state wage base. Texas, for example, taxes the first $9,000 and starts new employers at 2.7%.
  • For an $18.25-an-hour employee working 80 hours, the biweekly check is $1,460 and the employer’s taxes add $159.87, about 11% of wages until the FUTA and SUTA wage bases are reached.

Employer payroll taxes are the taxes a business pays out of its own pocket because it has employees, separate from the income tax and FICA it withholds from each paycheck. In 2026 that means matching the employee’s Social Security and Medicare taxes, paying federal unemployment tax (FUTA), paying state unemployment tax (SUTA), and in a growing list of states contributing to disability or paid family leave insurance. The table below gives the rate and wage base for each, using the IRS figures in Publication 15 for 2026. 2026 employer payroll tax rates and wage bases

These are the payroll tax rates for 2026 that fall on the employer. The “wage base” is the amount of each employee’s wages the tax applies to in a calendar year; above it, the tax stops for that employee until January.

The Social Security Administration set the 2026 wage base at $184,500, and the IRS confirms the 6.2% and 1.45% employer rates in Publication 15 (Circular E) for 2026. The 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax on wages above $200,000 has no employer match; you withhold it from the employee only. FUTA stays at 6.0% on the first $7,000 with a credit of up to 5.4% for paying state unemployment tax in full and on time, which is why nearly every employer’s real FUTA rate is 0.6%.

How much are payroll taxes in total? Add the three federal pieces and the employer’s federal share is 7.65% of wages plus $42 a year per employee. Add a typical SUTA rate and most small employers land between 8% and 12% of wages, with the percentage falling for higher earners because FUTA and SUTA stop early in the year.

Worked example: one barista, one paycheck

Dana Brooks is a barista at Northfield Coffee in Austin, Texas, hired November 2, 2026, at $18.25 an hour and paid biweekly. For the November 15–28 pay period, paid Friday, December 4, she works 80 regular hours with no overtime.

Gross pay: 80 × $18.25 = $1,460.00.

Employer taxCalculationAmount
Social Security match6.2% × $1,460.00$90.52
Medicare match1.45% × $1,460.00$21.17
FUTA0.6% × $1,460.00 (Dana is under $7,000 for the year)$8.76
Texas SUTA (illustrative 2.7%)2.7% × $1,460.00 (under $9,000 for the year)$39.42
Total employer payroll taxes10.95% of gross$159.87
Total cost of this paycheck$1,460.00 + $159.87$1,619.87

The 2.7% SUTA rate is the Texas entry rate for new employers and is used here as an illustration; the Texas Workforce Commission assigns each employer its own rate, and other states use different rates and wage bases. Texas has no state income tax and no state disability program, so there is nothing else in the employer column.

Annualized. If Dana worked this schedule for a full year, her wages would be 26 × $1,460 = $37,960. The employer’s Social Security match would be $2,353.52 and Medicare $550.42, both on the full amount because she is far below the $184,500 base. FUTA stops at $7,000 of wages, so it tops out at $42.00, and Texas SUTA stops at $9,000, so it tops out at $243.00. Total employer payroll taxes for the year: $3,188.94, which makes her real cost $41,148.94, or 8.4% above her wages. Notice how the percentage dropped from 10.95% on the first check to 8.4% for the year: the unemployment taxes are front-loaded into the first few months.

The employee side, for contrast. Dana’s own stub for the same period shows $90.52 Social Security and $21.17 Medicare withheld, the mirror image of the employer match, plus federal income tax based on her Form W-4. Between the two sides, $223.38 of Social Security and Medicare goes to the IRS from this one paycheck before income tax is counted. Overtime raises every employer tax above with it; our overtime calculator shows how a 45-hour week changes the gross.

FUTA, SUTA and credit reduction states

Unemployment tax is the part of employer payroll taxes with the most moving parts, because it has a federal layer and a state layer that interact.

FUTA. The federal rate is 6.0% on the first $7,000 you pay each employee in a calendar year. You owe FUTA if you paid $1,500 or more in wages in any calendar quarter of this year or last, or had at least one employee for some part of a day in 20 or more different weeks. If you pay your state unemployment tax in full and on time, the IRS gives you a credit of up to 5.4%, leaving 0.6%, or $42 per employee. Pay the state late and you can lose part of the credit, which turns a $42 bill into one as high as $420 per person.

Credit reduction states. When a state borrows from the federal government to pay unemployment benefits and still owes the money on two consecutive January 1 dates, employers in that state lose 0.3% of the FUTA credit, and another 0.3% each year the loan stays unpaid. For 2025, California employers paid a 1.2% reduction, so their FUTA rate was 1.8%. For 2026 the Department of Labor’s potential list, published on its FUTA credit reduction page, has California at 1.5% (up to 5.3% if an additional add-on is not waived) and the U.S. Virgin Islands at 4.8%. The final determination is made after November 10, 2026, and shows up on Schedule A (Form 940) for 2026, so confirm the rate there before you file. A 1.5% reduction adds $105 per employee to the usual $42.

SUTA. Every state runs its own unemployment insurance tax with its own wage base and rate schedule. New employers get an entry rate for their first years; after that the state assigns an experience rate each year based on how many former employees drew benefits on your account. Texas is a useful example: the Texas Workforce Commission taxes the first $9,000 of each employee’s wages, new employers pay the greater of 2.7% or the average rate for their industry, and the total rate also includes small components such as the obligation assessment, set at 0.01% for 2026. Other states differ a lot. Several have wage bases above $50,000, and a few (Alaska, New Jersey and Pennsylvania) also take a small employee contribution. Your state’s rate notice, usually mailed in December, is the only source for your own rate.

Two things keep SUTA costs down: respond to every unemployment claim on time so benefits are not charged to your account by default, and keep terminations documented with a clear attendance policy and write-ups, because the state decides charges based on why the person left.

State disability and paid family leave contributions

A growing number of states fund disability insurance or paid family and medical leave through payroll contributions. In most of them the employee pays all or most of the premium through withholding, but several require an employer share, often only once you pass a headcount:

  • Washington Paid Family and Medical Leave: a 1.13% premium in 2026, rising to 1.2% on January 1, 2027, with employers of 50 or more paying an employer share (29.3% of the premium in 2027) and smaller employers paying none, per the Employment Security Department.
  • Colorado FAMLI, Oregon Paid Leave and Massachusetts PFML all require an employer share above a headcount (10, 25 and 25 employees respectively); Minnesota’s program began collecting in 2026; Maryland’s contributions are scheduled to start in 2027.
  • New Jersey temporary disability, New York disability benefits and Hawaii temporary disability insurance put part or all of the cost on the employer, while California, Rhode Island and New York paid family leave are funded by employees.

Rates change every January, and the splits are set by each state agency, so take the percentage from your state program’s current notice rather than a table on the internet. If you have employees in more than one state, the contribution follows where the work is performed, which is one more reason to keep time and attendance records by location.

Deposits and filings: Form 941, Form 940 and the schedules

Calculating employer payroll taxes is the easy part. Getting the money to the IRS on the right day is where penalties start. Federal deposits must be made electronically, through EFTPS or another IRS electronic payment option, and the schedule depends on how much tax you reported in a lookback period.

Penalties for late payroll tax deposits

The IRS failure-to-deposit penalty is a percentage of the deposit that is late or short, based on how many calendar days late it is, and the tiers do not stack:

Days latePenaltyOn a $2,500 deposit
1 to 5 days2%$50
6 to 15 days5%$125
More than 15 days10%$250
Still unpaid more than 10 days after the first IRS notice, or on the day a notice demands immediate payment15%$375

Interest runs on top, and a deposit made by check or any method other than electronic transfer can also be treated as a 10% penalty case. Late Form 941 and Form 940 filings carry separate failure-to-file penalties, and states add their own interest and penalties on late unemployment reports. Withheld taxes are also trust fund taxes: if a business collects them and does not pay, the IRS can assess a trust fund recovery penalty equal to the unpaid amount against the owner or whoever controlled the money personally.

What changes in 2027

The Social Security Administration announced in its October 2026 cost-of-living notice that the Social Security wage base rises from $184,500 to $192,300 for wages paid in 2027. The 6.2% and 1.45% rates do not change, so the only effect is on employees earning above $184,500, whose employer match continues for an extra $7,800 of wages (up to $483.60 more per employee). The FUTA rate and $7,000 wage base are set in statute and are not scheduled to change. States announce their 2027 unemployment rates and wage bases in late 2026, and Washington’s paid leave premium moves to 1.2%. Your 2027 federal deposit schedule is based on a new lookback period, July 1, 2025 to June 30, 2026, so check whether a busy year moved you from monthly to semiweekly. Independent contractors don’t trigger employer payroll taxes

None of these taxes apply to payments made to a properly classified independent contractor. You do not match Social Security or Medicare, you owe no FUTA or SUTA, and you withhold nothing; the contractor pays 15.3% self-employment tax on their own return. That gap, roughly 8% to 12% of pay, is exactly why the IRS and state agencies audit classification. The test is control: who sets the hours, provides the tools, and decides how the work is done. If a worker is reclassified as an employee, you owe both halves of FICA, FUTA and SUTA for the back period plus penalties and interest, and the state may bill unemployment contributions too. What employers should do

  1. Budget 8% to 12% above wages for payroll taxes alone, more in a credit reduction state or where you pay a disability or leave share. Add benefits and paid time off separately.
  2. Register before the first payday. You need an EIN, EFTPS enrollment, a state unemployment account and, outside the nine states without income tax, a state withholding account. New hires also need a complete paperwork file so the W-4 is in place before you run pay.
  3. Know your deposit schedule and the exact due dates for the year. Most new employers are monthly depositors.
  4. Read your state rate notice each December and update the rate in your payroll system on January 1. A wrong SUTA rate means every quarterly report is wrong.
  5. Watch the wage bases. FUTA stops at $7,000 and your state SUTA at its base, so most of the year’s unemployment tax lands in the first and second quarters. If your numbers do not drop mid-year, something is off.
  6. Respond to unemployment claims and keep separation records, because your experience rate is the one payroll tax you can lower.
  7. Keep records at least four years. The IRS requires employment tax records for four years after the tax is due or paid, and the FLSA requires payroll records for three years.
  8. Decide who owns the deposits and filings, you, your accountant or your payroll provider, and get it in writing. Ask every provider which forms they file and which stay with you. When you compare options, our roundup of the best payroll services for small business lists the questions to ask.

Frequently asked questions

How much are payroll taxes for employers in 2026?

Federal employer payroll taxes in 2026 are 6.2% Social Security on wages up to $184,500, 1.45% Medicare on all wages, and 0.6% FUTA on the first $7,000 per employee after the state credit, about 8.25% on a typical wage. State unemployment tax adds a state-assigned rate on a state wage base, so most employers land between 8% and 12% of wages.

What payroll taxes does the employer pay and what does the employee pay?

Social Security and Medicare are split evenly: the employee has 6.2% and 1.45% withheld and the employer pays a matching 6.2% and 1.45%. Federal and state income tax and the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax come only from the employee’s pay. FUTA and, in most states, state unemployment tax are paid only by the employer.

Do employers pay payroll taxes on independent contractors?

No. Payments to a properly classified independent contractor are not wages, so there is no employer Social Security or Medicare match, no FUTA and no state unemployment tax. The contractor pays self-employment tax instead. If the IRS or a state later decides the worker was an employee, the employer owes the back taxes plus penalties.

What is the FUTA tax rate for 2026?

The 2026 FUTA rate is 6.0% on the first $7,000 of each employee’s wages. Employers that pay state unemployment tax on time get a credit of up to 5.4%, which leaves 0.6%, or $42 per employee per year. Employers in a credit reduction state get a smaller credit and pay more; the Department of Labor sets that list after November 10.

When are employer payroll taxes due?

Social Security, Medicare and withheld income tax are deposited monthly (by the 15th of the next month) or semiweekly, depending on your IRS deposit schedule, and reported on Form 941 each quarter. FUTA is deposited by the end of the month after any quarter in which your undeposited liability passes $500 and reported once a year on Form 940.

What happens if I deposit payroll taxes late?

The IRS failure-to-deposit penalty is 2% of the unpaid deposit when it is 1 to 5 days late, 5% at 6 to 15 days, and 10% when more than 15 days late. It rises to 15% if the tax is still unpaid more than 10 days after the first IRS notice. Interest is added, and states charge their own penalties on late unemployment tax.
