Quick answer

  • Good retail scheduling software matches staff to busy hours, puts the schedule on every phone, handles swaps and time off with a record, and shows labor hours against your budget before the week starts.
  • Retail has rules other industries mostly do not: federal hours limits for 14- and 15-year-olds, and fair workweek laws in cities like New York, San Francisco, Seattle, Chicago, Philadelphia and Los Angeles.
  • Choose between a standalone scheduling app and a connected system by asking where the hours go next: if someone still retypes them into payroll, the spreadsheet has only moved.

Retail scheduling software is the tool a store manager uses to build the weekly schedule, publish it to staff, handle swaps and time-off requests, and keep labor hours inside budget. Stores need more from it than most businesses do. Traffic swings hard between a Tuesday morning and a Saturday afternoon, most of the team is part-time with changing availability, the holiday season doubles the headcount for a few weeks, some staff are teenagers with legal hours limits, and several big cities regulate how far ahead a retail schedule must be posted. This guide covers what the software must handle, a buying checklist you can take into demos, and an honest comparison of the main types of tools.

What retail scheduling software has to do

Strip away the marketing and every tool does five jobs. If one of them is weak, the manager ends up back in a group text.

  1. Build the week fast. Copy last week, adjust for a promotion or a short-staffed weekend, and fill each shift with someone available and qualified for the register, the stockroom or the fitting rooms.
  2. Publish to every phone. Part-timers who work three shifts a week will not check a printout in the back room. The current schedule has to be on their phones, with a notice when it changes.
  3. Let staff fix small problems themselves. Shift swaps, open shifts and time-off requests should go through the app, with the manager approving where needed and a record of who asked for what.
  4. Keep labor inside budget. The manager should see scheduled hours and cost for the week, and who is close to 40 hours, before publishing, not on payday.
  5. Hand the hours on. On the day, the time clock compares punches with the schedule. Afterward, worked hours go to payroll. Each handoff done by hand is a chance for an error.

Retail employee scheduling problems the tool must solve

Retail employee scheduling differs from restaurant or clinic scheduling in a few specific ways. Ask any vendor how their tool handles each one.

Staffing to foot traffic

A store does not need the same five people from open to close. It needs two at 10 a.m. on a Tuesday and seven at 2 p.m. on a Saturday. Good store scheduling starts from a picture of when customers come in, usually from your point-of-sale sales by hour or a door counter, and builds shifts around those peaks. Some tools import this data and suggest staffing levels; others leave the forecast to you and make it easy to stagger shifts. Both can work. What matters is that you can build split coverage, such as an 11 a.m. to 3 p.m. mid shift, without fighting the tool.

A labor budget you can see

Many retailers set labor as a share of forecast sales. Here is an illustrative example, not a benchmark: a store forecasts $40,000 in sales next week and targets labor at 12%, which gives a budget of $4,800. At an average wage of $16.50 an hour, that buys about 290 hours. For a holiday week forecast at $60,000, the same 12% gives $7,200, or about 436 hours. The software should show scheduled hours and estimated cost as you build, so you can see you are 20 hours over before you publish, not after.

Weekends, holidays and seasonal hires

Black Friday through the end of December is when retail schedules break. You add seasonal hires who need to be in the system quickly, the regular team asks for time off at the same time, and everyone wants to avoid Christmas Eve. Look for fast employee setup, a time-off view right where you build shifts, and a way to post open shifts that existing part-timers can claim. Federal law does not require extra pay for working on a holiday; our guide to holiday pay rules explains what you can offer and how a premium affects overtime. If you pay more for nights, weekends or holidays, the shift differential guide shows how that changes the cost of a schedule and the overtime rate.

Part-timers and changing availability

A typical store team mixes students, parents working school hours and people with second jobs. Availability changes every semester. The tool should store each person's availability and warn you when you schedule outside it, keep track of weekly hours across stores, and make it easy for someone who wants more hours to pick up an open shift.

Several stores

Once you run two or more locations, people cover for each other. You need each store's schedule on its own, one view of a person's total hours across stores, and a clear record of which store's labor budget a borrowed shift came from.

Scheduling minors in a store

Retail hires a lot of teenagers, and the federal hours limits for 14- and 15-year-olds are strict. The US Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division publishes these limits under the Fair Labor Standards Act for non-agricultural jobs:

Federal limit for 14- and 15-year-oldsRule
School day (including Fridays)Up to 3 hours, outside school hours
School weekUp to 18 hours
Non-school dayUp to 8 hours
Non-school weekUp to 40 hours
Time of dayNot before 7 a.m. or after 7 p.m.; until 9 p.m. from June 1 through Labor Day

An illustrative school-week schedule for a 15-year-old: Tuesday and Thursday 4 p.m. to 7 p.m. (3 hours each) and Saturday 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. (8 hours) is 14 hours, inside the 18-hour cap. Moving Thursday to a 4:30 p.m. to 7:30 p.m. close in November breaks the 7 p.m. rule, even though the hours are the same.

Federal law sets no hours limits for 16- and 17-year-olds, though hazardous-job rules still apply. Many states set their own limits for minors, and the Department of Labor says employers must follow both federal and state rules. Check your state labor department, and ask each vendor whether the tool can flag minors or at least store birth dates so you can check these shifts before you publish.

Fair workweek laws that hit retail

There is no federal rule on how early you must post a schedule. But retail is the industry most often named in predictive scheduling, or fair workweek, laws. Most of them cover larger employers, often counted by employees or locations worldwide, and franchises can count toward the totals in some cities. In short:

  • New York City retail: schedules at least 72 hours before the first shift, no on-call shifts, no canceling a shift with less than 72 hours' notice, and no adding hours inside 72 hours unless the worker agrees, according to the city's Department of Consumer and Worker Protection.
  • San Francisco formula retail, Seattle, Oregon, Chicago, Philadelphia and Los Angeles: roughly two weeks' notice for covered employers, with extra pay for late changes the employer makes, and in several places rest rules between a closing and an opening shift. Los Angeles County added its own retail ordinance for unincorporated areas from July 1, 2025.

Coverage thresholds, premiums and exceptions differ in each place. Our scheduling page lists each law's coverage and notice rules as published by each agency; confirm with your city or state before you rely on it. For software, the practical point is the record: you want to show when a schedule was published, what changed after, and whether the employee or the employer asked for the change, because many of these laws treat employee-requested swaps differently.

Buying checklist for a retail staff scheduling app

Take this list into every demo, including ours. Ask the vendor to build one of your real weeks, ideally a December week, while you watch.

  • Staff use it. Ask two of your part-timers to try the employee app during the trial. If they still text you "am I on Saturday?", it is not working.
  • Building a week is fast. Copying last week, staggering shifts around a midday peak and filling an open Saturday should take minutes.
  • Availability and time off show while you build. You should not be able to schedule someone on an approved day off without noticing.
  • Swaps and open shifts leave a record. Who offered, who took it, who approved it and when. This matters most in fair workweek cities.
  • Hours and cost are visible before you publish. Weekly hours per person, anyone close to 40, and total labor for the store.
  • Multi-store works. Each store on its own, one person's hours across all of them.
  • Minors can be identified. At minimum, you can see who is under 16 when building a school-week evening shift.
  • The time clock knows the schedule. Late arrivals and no-shows show up against the shift. Our no call, no show guide covers what to do when someone does not turn up.
  • Hours go to payroll without retyping. Ask exactly what happens to worked hours at the end of the pay period: export, integration, or the same system.
  • Setup fits the season. Ask how long it takes to add 15 seasonal hires and get them on the schedule.

Frequently asked questions

How far in advance do retail schedules have to be posted?

Federal law sets no notice period. Covered retail employers in New York City must post schedules 72 hours ahead, and Oregon, San Francisco, Seattle, Chicago, Philadelphia and Los Angeles generally require about two weeks for covered employers. If no law applies to you, posting two weeks ahead is still a good habit.

How many hours can a 15-year-old work in a store?

Under federal law, 14- and 15-year-olds can work up to 3 hours on a school day, 18 hours in a school week, 8 hours on a non-school day and 40 hours in a non-school week, between 7 a.m. and 7 p.m. (9 p.m. from June 1 through Labor Day). Your state may be stricter.

Are on-call shifts allowed in retail?

Federal law does not ban them, but some cities do. New York City prohibits on-call shifts for retail workers, and other fair workweek cities require predictability pay when covered employers make late changes. Check your city and state rules before you schedule anyone to wait by the phone.

Do retail employees get paid extra for working holidays?

Not under federal law. The FLSA does not require extra pay for working on a holiday, so holiday premiums are a policy choice in most places. If you promise a premium, put it in writing and apply it the same way every time, because it can affect overtime calculations.

Can one scheduling app handle several store locations?

Most modern tools can, but check how. You want to see each store's week on its own, move a person between stores without double-booking them, and see total weekly hours across every location, so a part-timer working two stores does not drift into overtime unnoticed.
