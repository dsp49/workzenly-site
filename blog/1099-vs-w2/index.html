1099 vs W2: employee or independent contractor in 2026

How the IRS, the Department of Labor and states like California decide who is an employee, what each side pays in tax, and what a wrong call costs.

Quick answer

  • A W-2 employee works under the business’s control; the employer withholds taxes and pays half of Social Security and Medicare. A 1099 contractor runs an independent business and pays their own taxes.
  • Employees pay 7.65% FICA and the employer matches it. Contractors pay 15.3% self-employment tax on 92.35% of net earnings, with the Social Security part capped at $184,500 for 2026.
  • The label in a contract does not decide it. The IRS, the Department of Labor and state agencies each apply their own test, and stricter state ABC tests often win.

The 1099 vs W2 question comes down to one thing: is the worker running their own business, or working in yours? A W-2 employee gets a paycheck with taxes withheld, minimum wage and overtime protection, and usually unemployment insurance and workers’ compensation. A 1099 independent contractor sends an invoice, chooses how to do the job, and handles their own taxes. Getting this worker classification right matters for every small business that hires a bookkeeper, a delivery driver, a cleaner or an extra pair of hands for a busy season, because the tax agencies and labor departments decide based on the facts, not the paperwork.

The names come from the tax forms. Employees receive a Form W-2 each January showing wages and taxes withheld. Contractors paid $2,000 or more in 2026 receive a Form 1099-NEC showing nonemployee compensation. Here is the independent contractor vs employee comparison side by side.

The IRS common-law test: behavioral, financial and relationship

For federal income tax withholding, Social Security, Medicare and federal unemployment tax, the IRS uses the common-law rules. The IRS says all evidence of control and independence must be weighed, and no single factor decides the case. It groups the evidence into three categories.

  • Behavioral control. Does the business control, or have the right to control, what the worker does and how they do it? Detailed instructions about when and where to work, which tools to use, the order of tasks, and training in the company’s methods all point to an employee.
  • Financial control. Does the business control the money side of the job? Signs of a contractor include a significant investment in their own equipment, unreimbursed expenses, the chance to make a profit or a loss, offering services to the public, and being paid a flat fee per job rather than by the hour or week.
  • Relationship of the parties. Is there a written contract, are there employee-type benefits such as insurance, a retirement plan or vacation pay, is the relationship expected to continue indefinitely, and is the work a key part of the regular business? A contract that says “independent contractor” helps only if the rest of the facts match it.

The IRS also points out that remote work does not change the answer. Someone working from home on a schedule and process you set can still be your employee.

Form SS-8. If the answer is unclear, either the business or the worker can file Form SS-8, Determination of Worker Status for Purposes of Federal Employment Taxes and Income Tax Withholding. The IRS reviews the facts and issues a determination, which the IRS says can take at least six months. An SS-8 ruling covers federal tax only, not wage and hour law or state law.

The DOL test under the FLSA, as of November 2026

Minimum wage and overtime under the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) use a different, broader standard called the economic reality test. The question is whether the worker is economically dependent on the business or is in business for themselves. This is where the rules have moved most in the last few years.

  • 2024 rule. The Department of Labor’s Wage and Hour Division published a final rule on January 10, 2024, effective March 11, 2024, that replaced the 2021 rule with a six-factor “totality of the circumstances” analysis: opportunity for profit or loss, investments, permanence, nature and degree of control, whether the work is integral to the business, and skill and initiative.
  • May 2025 enforcement change. On May 1, 2025, the Wage and Hour Division issued Field Assistance Bulletin 2025-1, telling investigators to stop applying the 2024 rule in enforcement and to use Fact Sheet #13 (July 2008) as informed by a 2019 opinion letter instead. As widely reported, the bulletin said the 2024 rule still applies in private lawsuits, and the department announced it would reconsider and likely rescind the rule.
  • February 2026 proposed rule. On February 26, 2026, the Department of Labor proposed rescinding the 2024 rule and returning to an analysis similar to the 2021 rule (published at 91 FR 9932). It gives the most weight to two core factors, the business’s control over the work and the worker’s opportunity for profit or loss, with other factors such as skill, permanence and whether the work is part of an integrated unit as guideposts. Comments closed April 28, 2026.

As of November 4, 2026, we could not find a published final rule replacing the 2024 regulation. That means the 2024 rule is still the regulation on the books and still matters in private FLSA lawsuits, while federal investigators follow the 2008-era enforcement guidance. Check the Wage and Hour Division’s misclassification rulemaking page on dol.gov before you rely on either standard, because a final rule could take effect at any time. Remember that the FLSA test and the IRS test can reach different answers for the same worker, and state law may be stricter than both.

State ABC tests: California, Massachusetts and New Jersey

Many states use their own test for wage laws, unemployment insurance or both. The strictest is the ABC test, which presumes a worker is an employee unless the business proves all three parts.

  1. A: the worker is free from the business’s control and direction in performing the work, both under the contract and in fact.
  2. B: the work is outside the usual course of the hiring business.
  3. C: the worker is customarily engaged in an independently established trade, occupation or business of the same kind.
  • California. Assembly Bill 5, in effect since January 1, 2020 and now in Labor Code section 2775, applies the ABC test to the Labor Code, the Unemployment Insurance Code and wage orders. The California Labor and Workforce Development Agency says a worker is considered an employee unless the hiring entity meets all three conditions. Later legislation, including AB 2257, carved out exemptions for certain occupations and business-to-business arrangements, which then fall back on the older Borello multi-factor test. Daily overtime makes California mistakes expensive; see our California overtime law guide.
  • Massachusetts. The state’s independent contractor law (M.G.L. c. 149, § 148B) treats anyone performing services as an employee unless all three prongs are met, and the Attorney General’s office enforces it for state wage and hour purposes. Prong B is the one that usually decides: a cleaning company cannot treat its cleaners as contractors, because cleaning is its usual course of business.
  • New Jersey. New Jersey applies the ABC test under its unemployment compensation law and its wage and hour and wage payment laws. The New Jersey Department of Labor and Workforce Development proposed regulations in 2025 explaining how it reads each prong, and trade reports say they were adopted in 2026. The state can also assess its own misclassification penalties on top of back taxes.

Several other states use an ABC-style test for unemployment insurance even when their wage laws use a looser standard. The safe working rule: if a worker would pass the IRS test but fails your state’s ABC test, treat them as an employee in that state.

Taxes on each side, with 2026 math

This is the money part of the w2 vs 1099 decision. Both employees and contractors pay Social Security (12.4% in total) and Medicare (2.9% in total). The difference is who pays it.

  • W-2 employee: 6.2% Social Security plus 1.45% Medicare, 7.65% in total, withheld from pay. The employer pays a matching 7.65%. For 2026, Social Security applies to the first $184,500 of wages, according to the Social Security Administration and the IRS, so the most an employee pays is $11,439. Medicare has no cap, and an extra 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax applies to wages over $200,000.
  • 1099 contractor: 15.3% self-employment tax, both halves, on net earnings from self-employment (profit after business expenses). The IRS says the amount subject to the tax is generally 92.35% of net earnings, which mirrors the fact that employees never pay FICA on the employer’s half. The tax applies once net earnings reach $400, and the contractor can deduct one half of it when figuring adjusted gross income.

Worked example, $60,000 either way (2026 figures, estimates).

On the other side, the business saves the $4,590 employer match, plus unemployment taxes and workers’ compensation. That saving is exactly why misclassification gets so much attention from regulators.

The 2026 cap. A contractor with $200,000 of net profit has $184,700 of net earnings (200,000 × 92.35%). The 12.4% Social Security part stops at $184,500, so it is $22,878.00. Medicare at 2.9% applies to all $184,700, which is $5,356.30. Total self-employment tax: $28,234.30. Income tax is separate on both sides and depends on filing status, deductions and the rest of the household’s income.

A legitimate contractor usually charges a higher rate to cover their own self-employment tax, insurance, equipment and unpaid gaps between jobs. What a business gets for the higher rate is flexibility and no payroll administration. What it gives up is control over how and when the work is done. If you need to set the schedule, train the person in your methods and keep them on indefinitely, you are describing an employee.

Misclassification penalties and the VCSP

Treating an employee as a contractor without a reasonable basis can create liability on several fronts at once.

  • Federal employment tax. The IRS says that a business that misclassifies without a reasonable basis may be held liable for the employment taxes for that worker. When the error was not intentional, Internal Revenue Code section 3509 sets the liability at 1.5% of wages for income tax that should have been withheld and 20% of the employee’s share of FICA, plus the employer’s own full share. If the business also failed to file the required 1099s, those rates rise to 3% and 40%. Penalties and interest can come on top.
  • Back wages under the FLSA. Misclassified workers can claim unpaid minimum wage and overtime, and the FLSA allows liquidated damages in an equal amount in many cases.
  • State liability. Unemployment insurance contributions, workers’ compensation premiums, state wage claims and, in states such as New Jersey and Massachusetts, separate misclassification penalties.
  • Relief for the worker. A worker who believes they were misclassified can file Form SS-8, and can use Form 8919 to report only the employee share of Social Security and Medicare instead of the full self-employment tax.

Worked example (illustrative). A business paid a worker $50,000 on a 1099 in 2026, and the IRS finds the worker was an employee. Under section 3509(a): income tax 1.5% × $50,000 = $750.00; employee FICA 20% × $3,825.00 = $765.00; employer FICA $3,825.00. Total $5,340.00 for that one year, before penalties, interest, unemployment taxes and any back overtime. If no 1099 was filed, the 3% and 40% rates apply: $1,500.00 + $1,530.00 + $3,825.00 = $6,855.00.

Voluntary Classification Settlement Program. The IRS VCSP lets an eligible business reclassify workers as employees for future tax periods with partial relief. According to the IRS, the business pays 10% of the employment tax liability that would have been due on the workers’ pay for the most recent tax year, figured under the reduced section 3509(a) rates, with no interest or penalties on that amount and no employment tax audit of those workers’ classification for prior years. In the example above, that is 10% × $5,340.00 = $534.00. To qualify, the business must have consistently treated the workers as nonemployees, filed all required 1099s for them for the previous three years, and not be under an IRS employment tax audit or a Department of Labor or state classification audit. It applies with Form 8952 at least 120 days before it wants to start treating the workers as employees. The VCSP covers federal employment tax only; it does not resolve state or FLSA exposure.

Some businesses with a reasonable basis for contractor treatment may qualify for Section 530 relief from federal employment taxes. The IRS explains the requirements in Publication 1976. It does not change the worker’s status under other laws.

What employers and workers should do

Most 1099 vs W2 mistakes happen at the start of a working relationship, when nobody checks the facts against the tests. A short review before the first payment prevents most of them.

For employers:

  1. Test each role, not each person. Write down who sets the hours, who supplies the tools, how the person is paid, whether they work for other clients, and whether the work is what your business sells. Run the facts through the IRS test, the FLSA test and your state’s test.
  2. Use the strictest test that applies. In California, Massachusetts or New Jersey, assume the ABC test governs wage claims.
  3. Collect a W-9 before paying any contractor and file 1099-NECs for anyone paid $2,000 or more for 2026.
  4. Keep the contractor relationship real. Pay per project or invoice, let them set their own methods and hours, and do not add them to your shift schedule, handbook or performance reviews.
  5. Fix mistakes early. If a role fails the tests, move it to payroll going forward, record hours worked on a time clock so overtime is paid correctly, and talk to a tax advisor about the VCSP or other relief.

For workers:

  • If you are paid on a 1099, set aside money for self-employment tax and make quarterly estimated payments.
  • If you work set shifts under a manager’s direction with the employer’s tools, ask how they decided you are a contractor. You can file Form SS-8 with the IRS, a wage complaint with the Department of Labor’s Wage and Hour Division, or a claim with your state labor department.
  • As an employee, check that each pay stub shows Social Security and Medicare withheld at 6.2% and 1.45%.

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to be 1099 or W2?

It depends on what you value. A W-2 employee gets minimum wage and overtime protection, an employer that pays half of Social Security and Medicare, and usually unemployment and workers’ compensation coverage. A 1099 contractor controls how and when the work is done and can deduct business expenses, but pays 15.3% self-employment tax and makes estimated tax payments.

Can a worker choose to be paid on a 1099?

No. Classification depends on how the work is actually done, not on what the worker prefers or what a contract says. If the business controls how, when and where the work happens and the work is part of its regular operations, the worker is likely an employee, even if both sides signed an independent contractor agreement.

What is the 1099-NEC threshold for 2026?

For payments made in 2026, a business generally files Form 1099-NEC when it pays a nonemployee $2,000 or more for services in its trade or business. The threshold was $600 for 2025 payments and is set to be adjusted for inflation in later years. Contractors must report all of their income, whether or not they receive a form.

Can someone be both a W-2 employee and a 1099 contractor for the same company?

It is rarely safe. The IRS and the Department of Labor look at the whole relationship, and an employee who does similar extra work for the same employer is usually still an employee for that work. Pay extra work as additional W-2 wages unless it is genuinely separate work done through the worker’s own independent business.

How long does an IRS Form SS-8 determination take?

The IRS says a determination can take at least six months. Either the business or the worker can file Form SS-8 to ask the IRS to decide a worker’s status for federal employment tax and income tax withholding. The decision covers federal tax only, so it does not settle status under the FLSA or state law.

What happens if I misclassify an employee as a contractor?

You can owe the employment taxes you should have paid, part of the employee’s share, penalties and interest, plus back overtime under the FLSA and state assessments for unemployment insurance and workers’ compensation. If you treated the workers consistently and filed every 1099, the IRS Voluntary Classification Settlement Program may let you fix it for a reduced payment.
