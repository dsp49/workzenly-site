Comp time: what employers can and cannot do in 2026

Comp time sounds like a friendly swap for overtime pay. For most private employers it is not allowed. Here is where the line sits, who can use it, and what to do instead.

Quick answer

  • Private-sector employers cannot give non-exempt employees comp time instead of overtime pay. Hours over 40 in a workweek must be paid at 1.5 times the regular rate, and time off in a later week does not fix it.
  • State and local government agencies can offer comp time at 1.5 hours per overtime hour, up to 240 hours (480 for public safety, emergency response and seasonal work).
  • Time off inside the same workweek is legal, and so is comp time for exempt salaried staff as a policy choice. Watch state rules, especially daily overtime.

Comp time, short for compensatory time, is paid time off given in place of cash for extra hours worked. It is popular because it saves payroll dollars in a busy stretch and gives employees a break later. The catch is the Fair Labor Standards Act. Under federal law, comp time is a tool for government employers, not for private ones, and a private business that hands it to hourly staff instead of overtime pay owes back wages. This guide covers who may use comp time, the rules and caps, the legal workarounds, and a policy outline for exempt employees. It is general information, not legal advice.

What comp time is

It means an employee works extra hours and later takes paid time off instead of being paid for those hours in cash. The key question is whether the extra hours were overtime. For a non-exempt (hourly) employee, overtime is every hour worked over 40 in a workweek, paid at 1.5 times the regular rate. The U.S. Department of Labor (DOL) enforces this under the FLSA, and the statute that permits comp time, 29 U.S.C. section 207(o), applies only to public agencies.

People use the phrase loosely, so separate three things. Comp time usually means time off banked in exchange for overtime hours. Flex time means moving hours around within the same workweek. PTO is an earned leave benefit with no link to hours worked beyond the schedule. Mixing them up is how well-meaning managers end up with wage claims. If you need a refresher on who qualifies for overtime in the first place, read our guide to exempt vs non-exempt employees.

Private employers: comp time instead of overtime is not allowed

If you run a restaurant, shop, clinic, warehouse or office, the rule is simple. A non-exempt employee who works more than 40 hours in a workweek must receive overtime pay for those hours, at no less than 1.5 times the regular rate, on the regular payday for the period. Comp time as a substitute for cash overtime is an option only for public agencies. A private employer cannot offer 1.5 hours off later in place of paying the premium now.

Three common mistakes follow from this:

  • Banking hours across weeks. "You worked 46 hours this week, take 6 hours off next week" does not cure the overtime. The FLSA looks at each workweek on its own, and you cannot average weeks together.
  • Getting the employee to agree. The employee's written consent, a signed policy or an offer of "time and a half in time off" does not matter. FLSA overtime rights cannot be waived by agreement.
  • Offering a generous time-off rate. Even two hours off for each overtime hour is still not allowed for private employers. The employee is owed money for the overtime hours.

The penalty is back wages for the unpaid overtime, plus an equal amount in liquidated damages in many cases, with a two-year look-back that stretches to three for willful violations, and your state may add its own penalties. For the rate math, see how time and a half works and how to calculate overtime.

The legal exception: time off within the same workweek

There is a safe version of the idea. If an employee works extra hours early in a workweek and you send them home early later in that same workweek, so the total stays at 40 or fewer, no overtime was ever earned. There is nothing to pay at a premium, because the hours worked came to 40. The workweek is a fixed, recurring period of 168 hours (seven consecutive 24-hour periods), and for Northfield Coffee it runs Sunday through Saturday.

Two cautions. First, the adjustment must happen inside the same fixed workweek, and the employee must be paid for the hours actually worked. Second, state law can bite. In states with daily overtime, an 11-hour Tuesday triggers premium pay no matter how short Friday is. Always check the state first.

Public agencies: how comp time works

State governments, cities, counties, school districts and similar public agencies may use comp time under section 7(o) of the FLSA. The rules, set out in section 207(o) and in the DOL regulations at 29 CFR part 553, include:

  • Rate: at least 1.5 hours of comp time for each overtime hour worked.
  • Agreement first: a collective bargaining agreement or similar agreement, or, for employees outside one, an understanding reached between the agency and the employee before the work is performed.
  • Caps: 240 hours of accrued comp time for most employees, and 480 hours for public safety, emergency response and seasonal employees. After the cap, further overtime must be paid in cash.
  • Use on request: an employee who asks to use banked time must be allowed to take it within a reasonable period, unless it would unduly disrupt the agency's operations.
  • Cash-out during employment: paid at the regular rate the employee earns at the time of payment.
  • Payout at separation: unused comp time is paid at the higher of the employee's average regular rate over the last three years or the final regular rate.

Compensatory time that a public agency provides outside section 7(o) is called "other" comp time. Under 29 CFR 553.28, it can come from a collective bargaining agreement, a state or local law with a higher overtime threshold than the FLSA, or the agency's own policy, and the accrual rate and caps can differ. Private employers should not read that as a loophole. It sits in the public-sector regulations and applies to those agencies only.

Worked examples

Example 1: Northfield Coffee, the illegal swap

Emily Carter works in the kitchen at Northfield Coffee in Austin, Texas, at $19.00 an hour. Her pay period is November 1 to 14, 2026, paid Friday, November 20. She works 46 hours in the week of November 1 to 7 and 32 hours in the week of November 8 to 14, so 6 of her hours in week one are overtime. Her overtime pay is:

  • Overtime rate: $19.00 x 1.5 = $28.50 an hour
  • Overtime pay: 6 hours x $28.50 = $171.00

Suppose the manager says, "Take 9 hours off next week instead." Nine hours is 6 x 1.5, the public-sector formula, and 9 x $19.00 is also $171.00. It looks fair. For a private employer it is still a violation: the $171.00 was due on the November 20 payday, and the week-two time off does not erase week-one overtime. If Northfield Coffee paid Emily only for the straight-time value of her hours, the unpaid overtime premium would be owed in back wages, with possible additional damages.

Example 2: The legal fix inside the workweek

Now say Emily is scheduled 46 hours for November 1 to 7. By Wednesday her manager Marcus knows the week will run long, and he sends her home 6 hours early across Thursday and Friday. She works 40 hours in the workweek, so she earned no overtime. She is paid 40 x $19.00 = $760.00 for the week. Nothing is owed at a premium, as long as the 40-hour total holds and no daily overtime rule applies. Texas has no daily overtime requirement for private employers.

Example 3: A city department (illustrative)

An illustrative city parks employee earns $24.00 an hour and, under a written agreement, works 10 overtime hours in a week. Comp time earned is 10 x 1.5 = 15 hours, equal in value to the cash overtime of 10 x $24.00 x 1.5 = $360.00. If the employee leaves with a 40-hour balance, the final regular rate is $26.00 and the three-year average is $24.50. The agency pays the higher rate: 40 x $26.00 = $1,040.00.

Comp time vs overtime vs flex time vs PTO

The four terms get used as if they were interchangeable. They are not, and the differences are what keep a policy legal. This table compares them for a non-exempt private employee unless noted.

Comp time for salaried exempt employees

Exempt employees do not earn overtime under the FLSA, so extra hours do not create a legal debt. Giving a salaried manager an afternoon off after a product launch is a perk, not an overtime issue, and the law does not regulate it. This is what people mean when they search for "comp time for salaried employees". It is a policy choice, and you can write it as generously or as tightly as you like.

Be careful about two things. First, an employee only counts as exempt if they pass both the salary and duties tests, and the federal salary level is $684 a week. A misclassified employee is owed overtime, and time off will not be a defense. Your state may set a higher bar. Second, an exempt salary generally cannot be docked in ways that break the salary basis rule, so do not "charge" this time off against partial-day absences without checking the rules. Here is a short policy outline to adapt.

Comp time policy outline for exempt staff
COMP TIME POLICY (EXEMPT EMPLOYEES ONLY) 1. Who it covers: Salaried employees classified as exempt under the Fair Labor Standards Act and [State] law. It does not apply to hourly or non-exempt employees, who are paid overtime in cash for all hours over [40] in a workweek [and over any state daily threshold]. 2. Nature of the benefit: This benefit is a discretionary perk, not wages. It does not change the employee's salary and has no cash value unless [Company name] chooses otherwise in writing. 3. How it is earned: A manager may award time off after unusually long projects, weekend or on-call work. Typical award: [one] hour off for each [two] hours of approved extra work, up to [16] hours per quarter. 4. Approval and use: The employee's manager approves the award in writing, and the employee schedules the time off with [X] days' notice. Time off is taken in whole or half days and is subject to business needs. 5. Expiration: Unused time off expires [90] days after it is awarded. It is not paid out at separation, unless required by state law. 6. Salary protection: Using this time off never reduces the employee's salary for the week. 7. Records: The manager records the award and each use in [Workzenly / HR system]. The employee confirms each entry. 8. Review: HR reviews classification and this policy each [year]. This outline is a starting point; have an attorney review it before use.

State rules and daily overtime

State law can only add protection for employees. Where a state sets overtime rules that are tougher than the FLSA, you follow the tougher rule. Some states, including California, Alaska, Nevada and Colorado, require overtime based on hours in a single day as well as the workweek, so shortening a later day does not undo overtime already earned. Check your state labor department's site for the exact thresholds.

California deserves a special note. California Labor Code section 204.3 allows limited compensating time off in lieu of overtime pay. As we understand it, it is tied to an employee's written request or an agreement, a cap of 240 hours and a rate of at least 1.5 hours off per overtime hour. The statute has narrow conditions and sits alongside daily overtime and wage order rules, so confirm the current details with the California Department of Industrial Relations or an attorney before you rely on it. Read our California overtime law guide for the wider picture.

Public employers in every state also face their own statutes, civil service rules and union contracts, which can make comp time rules more generous than the FLSA minimum.

The Working Families Flexibility Act

Lawmakers have proposed letting private employers offer compensatory time for years. The best-known proposal is the Working Families Flexibility Act, which would let a private employer offer non-exempt employees a choice between cash overtime and comp time. No version has become law, so the rules in this article still apply. In the current Congress, H.R. 2870 and its Senate companion S. 1158 are titled the Working Families Flexibility Act of 2025. According to Congress.gov and the House committee report, the House Education and Workforce Committee reported H.R. 2870, and we found no floor vote or Senate committee action as of this writing. The proposals would cap private-sector accrual at 160 hours a year and require unused time to be paid out at year-end, but a bill that has not passed changes nothing for you today.

Treat any claim that private-sector compensatory time is "now legal" as wrong unless it comes from the DOL. Check dol.gov/agencies/whd and Congress.gov for the current status before changing a policy.

Records to keep

Whatever you do, the FLSA requires employers to keep accurate records of the hours each non-exempt employee works each workday and each workweek. Payroll records must be kept for three years, and time cards and wage computation records for two. For public agencies using comp time, also record the hours earned, the hours used, the running balance, and the signed agreement that allowed it. For exempt staff awarded time off as a perk, log each award and each use so the policy is applied evenly across managers. Overtime and balances are easier to defend when they come from time clock data, not memory. Frequently asked questions

Is comp time legal for private employers?

Not as a substitute for overtime pay. Under the FLSA, a private employer must pay a non-exempt employee 1.5 times the regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek, and time off in a later week does not satisfy that duty. Time off inside the same workweek is legal because no overtime was earned. Exempt staff are a policy matter.

How much comp time do public employees earn?

Public agencies that have a proper agreement can give at least 1.5 hours of comp time for each overtime hour worked. Most employees can bank up to 240 hours. Public safety, emergency response and seasonal employees can bank up to 480. Past the cap, further overtime must be paid in cash.

Can I give comp time to salaried exempt employees?

Yes. Exempt employees are not owed overtime, so time off for extra hours is a policy choice, not an FLSA requirement. Put it in writing, set who approves it, and never reduce the salary for it. Check that the employee is truly exempt, because a misclassified employee is owed overtime.

What happens to unused comp time when a public employee leaves?

The agency must pay out unused comp time at the higher of two rates: the employee's average regular rate over the last three years of employment, or the final regular rate. That means a long-serving employee with a big balance can be owed a meaningful payout.

Does California allow comp time?

California Labor Code section 204.3 allows limited compensating time off in lieu of overtime, tied to a written request or agreement, a 240-hour cap and a 1.5 hour rate. Rules are narrow and daily overtime still applies, so confirm the details with the California Department of Industrial Relations or an attorney first.
