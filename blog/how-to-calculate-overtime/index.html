How to calculate overtime, step by step

The method US employers use under federal law: set the workweek, find the regular rate of pay, then add the overtime premium. Five worked examples show the exact math.

Quick answer

  • Add up hours worked in one fixed seven-day workweek. Every hour over 40 is an overtime hour for a non-exempt employee.
  • Find the regular rate of pay: total straight-time pay plus bonuses, differentials and commissions, divided by total hours worked.
  • Overtime is owed at 1.5 times that regular rate. At $18 an hour and 47 hours, weekly pay is $909.00.

Here is how to calculate overtime under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA): count the hours a non-exempt employee worked in a single workweek, work out their regular rate of pay for that week, and pay at least time and a half, 1.5 times the regular rate, for every hour over 40. For a worker paid only by the hour with no extras, the regular rate is just the hourly wage. The math gets harder once bonuses, shift differentials, commissions, a second pay rate or a salary enter the week, and that is where most payroll mistakes happen. How is overtime calculated? Five steps

The U.S. Department of Labor’s Wage and Hour Division describes the same process in its Fact Sheet #23 on overtime pay. In practice it comes down to five steps.

  1. Confirm the employee is non-exempt. Most hourly workers are. Salaried workers are non-exempt unless they pass both a salary test and a duties test. Our exempt vs non-exempt guide covers the tests.
  2. Total the hours worked in the workweek. Count only time worked. Paid holidays, vacation and sick hours do not count toward 40 under federal law. If you are adding up paper cards, convert minutes first with the hours to decimal calculator.
  3. Add up all pay for the week that belongs in the regular rate. Hourly wages, differentials, commissions and nondiscretionary bonuses go in. Excluded payments stay out.
  4. Divide that pay by total hours worked. The result is the regular rate of pay.
  5. Pay the overtime premium. Because step 3 already paid every hour at straight time, the employer owes an extra 0.5 × regular rate for each hour over 40.

Put as one formula: weekly pay = all includable pay + (hours over 40 × regular rate × 0.5). For a simple hourly worker this gives the same answer as 40 × rate + overtime hours × rate × 1.5. Both routes are correct, but the half-time premium method is the one that still works when the regular rate changes from week to week.

Start with the workweek

The Department of Labor defines a workweek as “a fixed and regularly recurring period of 168 hours,” meaning seven consecutive 24-hour periods. It does not have to match the calendar week. A restaurant can run its workweek from Wednesday at 4:00 a.m. to the next Wednesday at 3:59 a.m. if that fits its late shifts.

  • Each week stands alone. Federal law does not allow averaging hours over two or more weeks. A biweekly pay period of 50 hours and 30 hours still contains 10 overtime hours.
  • Pay period is not the workweek. You can pay every two weeks or twice a month, but you calculate overtime week by week inside that period.
  • Change it only for good reason. An employer may change the workweek if the change is meant to be permanent, not to dodge overtime in a particular week.
  • Days do not matter federally. The FLSA has no daily overtime rule and no special rate for weekends or holidays as such. Our holiday pay guide explains when holiday premiums come from policy rather than law.

What goes into the regular rate of pay

The FLSA says the regular rate includes “all remuneration for employment,” except payments the law itself excludes. This is the part of the overtime rate that employers most often get wrong. The Department of Labor’s Fact Sheets #56A (regular rate), #56B (scheduling law penalties) and #56C (bonuses) set out the details.

Nondiscretionary vs discretionary bonuses. According to Fact Sheet #56C, a bonus can be left out only if the employer alone decides both whether to pay it and how much, at or near the end of the period, and it was not promised in advance. A bonus announced to encourage attendance or output is nondiscretionary, even if you call it a “gift.” A surprise year-end thank-you that nobody was told to expect can be discretionary.

Commissions. Federal regulations at 29 CFR 778.117 treat commissions as pay for hours worked, so they always go into the regular rate. When a commission is paid monthly or quarterly, it is spread back over the workweeks in which it was earned, and extra overtime premium is owed for any of those weeks with more than 40 hours.

Scheduling penalties. Fact Sheet #56B explains that some city and state predictive-scheduling payments, such as pay for a last-minute schedule change or a short rest between shifts, can be excluded if the trigger was not prearranged. On-call pay cannot. Whatever is excluded also cannot be credited toward overtime owed.

Worked examples with exact math

Example 1: hourly only. An employee earns $18 an hour and works 47 hours. Straight time: 47 × $18 = $846.00. Overtime premium: 7 hours × $9.00 = $63.00. Total: $909.00. Check it the other way: 40 × $18 = $720.00 plus 7 × $27.00 = $189.00, also $909.00.

Example 2: bonus and shift differential. An employee earns $16 an hour, works 44 hours including 12 night hours with a $2 differential, and earns a $75 attendance bonus.

  1. Straight-time pay: 44 × $16 = $704.00.
  2. Differential: 12 × $2 = $24.00. Bonus: $75.00.
  3. Regular rate: ($704 + $24 + $75) ÷ 44 = $803 ÷ 44 = $18.25 an hour.
  4. Overtime premium: 4 hours × ($18.25 × 0.5) = 4 × $9.125 = $36.50.
  5. Total pay: $803.00 + $36.50 = $839.50.

If the same employee also got a $50 holiday gift that was never promised, it is paid on top but left out of the regular rate, so the week totals $889.50. Night premiums are covered in more depth in our shift differential guide.

Example 3: two pay rates (weighted average). Under 29 CFR 778.115, when someone works two jobs at different rates in one workweek, the regular rate is the weighted average. A worker spends 30 hours as a line cook at $20 and 15 hours as a cashier at $14, 45 hours in total.

  1. Straight-time pay: 30 × $20 = $600.00 plus 15 × $14 = $210.00, for $810.00.
  2. Regular rate: $810 ÷ 45 = $18.00 an hour.
  3. Overtime premium: 5 × $9.00 = $45.00.
  4. Total pay: $810.00 + $45.00 = $855.00.

Federal rules also allow paying overtime at 1.5 times the rate of the job actually done during the overtime hours, but only if the employer and employee agree to it before the work is performed.

Example 4: commission. An employee earns $15 an hour, works 45 hours and earns $135 in commission for the week. Regular rate: ($675 + $135) ÷ 45 = $810 ÷ 45 = $18.00. Overtime premium: 5 × $9.00 = $45.00. Total: $855.00.

Overtime for salaried non-exempt employees

A salary does not cancel overtime, and knowing how to calculate overtime for salaried staff matters as much as for hourly crews. Under 29 CFR 778.113, the regular rate of a salaried non-exempt employee is the weekly salary divided by the number of hours the salary is meant to cover.

Example 5: salary for a 40-hour week. An office coordinator is paid $900 a week for a 40-hour schedule and works 46 hours. Regular rate: $900 ÷ 40 = $22.50. Overtime rate: $22.50 × 1.5 = $33.75. Overtime pay: 6 × $33.75 = $202.50. Total: $1,102.50. Because the salary only covers 40 hours, all six extra hours are paid at time and a half.

If the salary is intended to cover a 45-hour week, the first five hours over 40 are already paid at straight time, so only the half-time premium is owed for them. Fact Sheet #23 uses this setup: a $405 salary for 45 hours gives a $9.00 regular rate and $22.50 of extra overtime pay.

For other pay frequencies, convert the salary to a weekly amount first:

  • Biweekly: divide by 2. $1,800 every two weeks = $900 a week.
  • Semimonthly: multiply by 24 and divide by 52. $1,950 twice a month = $46,800 ÷ 52 = $900 a week.
  • Monthly: multiply by 12 and divide by 52. $3,900 a month = $46,800 ÷ 52 = $900 a week.

As of November 4, 2026, the federal salary level for the executive, administrative and professional exemptions is $684 a week ($35,568 a year), with $107,432 a year for highly compensated employees. The 2024 rule that would have raised those figures was vacated by a federal court, and the Department of Labor published a technical amendment on May 15, 2026, restoring the 2019 levels in the regulations. States such as California, New York, Washington and Colorado set higher thresholds.

The fluctuating workweek method

Some employers pay a fixed salary to non-exempt employees whose hours change every week and use the fluctuating workweek method in 29 CFR 778.114. It applies only if the hours really fluctuate, the salary stays the same whatever the hours, the salary covers at least minimum wage in the longest week, and both sides clearly understand the salary covers all hours worked.

Because the salary already pays straight time for every hour, the regular rate is the salary divided by the hours actually worked, and only a half-time premium is added. With a $900 weekly salary:

State daily overtime and other state rules

Federal law sets the floor, so how to calculate overtime in your business also depends on where your employees work. When a state rule is more generous, the employer follows whichever pays the employee more. A few states count overtime by the day as well as the week.

  • California: the California Department of Industrial Relations requires time and a half after 8 hours in a workday and for the first 8 hours on the seventh consecutive day of work in a workweek, and double time after 12 hours in a workday or after 8 hours on that seventh day. See our California overtime law guide.
  • Alaska and Nevada: both have daily overtime after 8 hours, with exceptions. In Nevada it applies only to employees paid less than 1.5 times the state minimum wage.
  • Colorado: overtime after 12 hours in a workday or 12 consecutive hours, as well as after 40 in a week.

The regular rate method in this guide still applies under state law, but each state can define included pay a little differently. Check your state labor department before you set up payroll rules.

Common overtime mistakes to avoid

  • Paying 1.5× the base rate only. If a bonus or differential was earned that week, the overtime rate is based on the higher regular rate, not the base wage.
  • Forgetting bonuses paid later. A quarterly production bonus must be allocated back to the weeks it covered, with extra premium for overtime weeks.
  • Counting PTO toward 40. It is not required federally. You can count it by policy, but then you pay more than the law requires, so decide on purpose. Our paid time off guide covers PTO policies.
  • Refusing to pay unapproved overtime. If the employee worked the hours and you knew or should have known, you owe the pay. Discipline is allowed; withholding wages is not.
  • Relying on memory for hours. A time clock app that records real punches is the simplest way to keep the records the FLSA requires.

Once you have the numbers, check that each pay stub shows regular and overtime hours separately. Our guide on how to read a pay stub shows employees what to look for.

Frequently asked questions

What is the regular rate of pay for overtime?

The regular rate of pay is everything an employee earned for the workweek, minus the payments federal law excludes, divided by all hours actually worked that week. It includes hourly wages, shift differentials, commissions and nondiscretionary bonuses, so it is often higher than the base hourly rate. Overtime is owed at 1.5 times this rate.

Can I average hours over two weeks to avoid overtime?

No. Under the FLSA each workweek stands alone, and the Department of Labor does not allow averaging hours across two or more weeks. An employee who works 50 hours one week and 30 the next is owed 10 hours of overtime for the first week, even if the biweekly pay period totals exactly 80 hours.

Does PTO count as hours worked for overtime?

Not under federal law. Paid vacation, holidays and sick leave are not hours worked, so they do not count toward the 40-hour threshold, and the pay for them is left out of the regular rate. An employer may choose to count PTO hours toward overtime by policy, because paying more than the law requires is always allowed.

How do I calculate overtime for an employee with two pay rates?

Add the straight-time pay from every job worked that week, then divide by the total hours to get a weighted average regular rate. Pay an extra half of that rate for each hour over 40. With an advance agreement, federal rules also allow paying overtime at 1.5 times the rate of the job done during the overtime hours.

Is a salaried employee owed overtime?

A salaried employee is owed overtime unless they meet an exemption. On November 4, 2026, the federal salary level for most white-collar exemptions is $684 a week, or $35,568 a year, and the employee must also pass a duties test. Several states set higher thresholds, so check your state labor department before treating anyone as exempt.
