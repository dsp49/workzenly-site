How to fill in the employee handbook template

Work through the template in this order. Most small businesses can finish a first draft in an afternoon.

  1. Fill in every bracket. Search for “[” and replace each one: company name, contacts, workweek, pay frequency, PTO numbers. Delete the bracket notes once you have acted on them.
  2. Delete policies you do not have. A handbook that promises a review process or a benefit you do not actually offer causes more trouble than a short handbook.
  3. Match what you really do. The workweek, the overtime approval rule and the meal break rule in section 4 should match how your time clock and payroll are set up. If your handbook says the workweek starts Sunday and payroll starts it Monday, overtime will be calculated on the wrong seven days. Our guide on how to calculate overtime explains why the fixed workweek matters.
  4. Check classifications. Section 3.3 tells employees whether they are exempt or non-exempt. Getting that wrong is an expensive wage claim, so read our exempt vs non-exempt guide before you finalize offer letters and the handbook.
  5. Write the holiday section carefully. Federal law does not require paid holidays for private employers, so your handbook is where the rules live: which days, who is eligible, and whether people who work the holiday get a premium. Our holiday pay guide covers the common choices.
  6. Add a state supplement for each state where you have employees (more on this below), then send the draft to counsel.

What to include in an employee handbook

An employee handbook does two jobs: it tells employees the rules in one place, and it gives you a written record that you told them. Here is what to include in an employee handbook, split into what the law makes you put in writing for covered employers and what is strongly recommended.

Federally required notices and the NLRB's handbook rules

FMLA notice in the handbook

Under the Department of Labor's FMLA regulations at 29 CFR 825.300, a covered employer must post a general notice of FMLA rights, and if it has any eligible employees it must also put that notice in its employee handbook or other written guidance about benefits or leave. If there is no handbook, the employer gives the notice to each new employee when hired. Both can be done electronically. According to the Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division, private employers are covered if they have 50 or more employees in 20 or more workweeks in the current or previous calendar year, and eligible employees can take up to 12 workweeks of job-protected leave in a 12-month period. Our FMLA leave guide covers eligibility in detail.

EEO laws and posters

No federal law makes you print an EEO policy in a handbook, but the laws behind it apply early. The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) says Title VII and the ADA cover employers with 15 or more employees, the Age Discrimination in Employment Act covers those with 20 or more, and the Equal Pay Act covers virtually all employers. Covered employers must also display the EEOC's “Know Your Rights” poster. State and local laws often cover smaller employers and more protected characteristics, which is why Template 2 ends with “any other characteristic protected by federal, state or local law.”

At-will language

Employment is at will in every state except Montana, where the Wrongful Discharge from Employment Act generally requires good cause for discharge once the probationary period ends. Even in at-will states, courts in some jurisdictions have enforced handbook promises as implied contracts, so keep the disclaimer and avoid promising a fixed discipline path you might not follow.

The NLRB and handbook rules

Section 7 of the National Labor Relations Act protects most private-sector, non-supervisory employees who act together about pay and working conditions, whether or not there is a union. In Stericycle, Inc. (2023), the National Labor Relations Board said a work rule is presumed unlawful if an employee could reasonably read it as discouraging that activity, unless the employer shows the rule advances a legitimate and substantial business interest that a narrower rule could not serve.

As of November 2026, our review found no Board decision overruling Stericycle. Law-firm summaries of NLRB General Counsel Memorandum GC 26-04 (August 26, 2026) report that the General Counsel has asked the Board to reconsider it, so the standard may change. Until it does, write rules narrowly. Rules that often draw scrutiny include:

  • Bans on discussing pay, hours or working conditions.
  • Blanket “all company information is confidential” clauses that sweep in wages and schedules.
  • Broad bans on negative comments, talking to the media or posting about work on social media.
  • Blanket no-recording rules without a stated, specific business reason.

The rights statement in section 8 of Template 1 does not cure an overbroad rule on its own, but it helps. Check nlrb.gov for current decisions before you finalize these sections.

State-specific handbook additions

State law is where most handbooks need work. Two common examples:

California

  • Harassment, discrimination and retaliation policy. The Civil Rights Council's regulation at 2 CCR 11023 requires a written prevention policy that lists the protected categories, offers a complaint route that does not require going to your direct supervisor, and promises no retaliation. It must be translated into every language spoken by at least 10% of the workforce at a facility, and distributed with a method such as a signed acknowledgment.
  • CFRA leave. Under 2 CCR 11095, if your handbook describes other personal or disability leave, it must describe California Family Rights Act leave. CFRA covers employers with 5 or more employees.
  • Pay, breaks and vacation. California has daily overtime, meal and rest break rules and requires payout of earned, unused vacation at separation. Our California overtime law guide covers the daily rules your timekeeping section should reflect.
  • Workplace violence prevention. Labor Code 6401.9, in effect since July 1, 2024, requires most employers to keep a written workplace violence prevention plan, according to the Department of Industrial Relations (Cal/OSHA). Reference it in the safety section.

New York

  • Sexual harassment prevention. Labor Law 201-g requires every employer to adopt a written sexual harassment prevention policy (the state model or one that meets its minimum standards) and provide interactive training every year.
  • Reproductive health decision making. Labor Law 203-e requires employers that provide a handbook to include a notice of employee rights and remedies under that section. A court injunction against the notice provision was vacated by the Second Circuit, so the requirement applies.
  • Electronic monitoring. Civil Rights Law 52-c requires private employers that monitor employee phone, email or internet use to give written notice at hiring, get an acknowledgment, and post the notice.
  • Paid sick leave. New York requires paid sick leave, with the amount tied to employer size. Put your terms in the PTO section.

Other states and many cities have their own sick leave, final pay and notice rules. Check your state labor department before you finalize the state supplement.

Employee handbook examples by type of business

The core template works for most small employers. These employee handbook examples show which sections to expand for common businesses.

  • Restaurant or cafe: tip policy and tip pooling, meal and rest breaks, food safety and hygiene, uniform rules, and how shift swaps work on busy weekends.
  • Retail store: cash handling and register shortages (check state limits on deductions), employee discounts, holiday season scheduling and blackout dates for PTO.
  • Healthcare or home care: licensing and certification renewal, patient privacy, travel time between client visits, and on-call pay.
  • Construction or field services: safety gear, injury reporting, vehicle use, travel time and clocking in at job sites with a GPS time clock.
  • Office team: hybrid and remote work, equipment, expense reimbursement and an exempt employee's schedule expectations.

How to roll out the handbook and collect acknowledgments

  1. Get counsel's review. Ask an employment attorney licensed in your states to review the final draft, especially the state supplement, the at-will language and any confidentiality or social media rules.
  2. Set an effective date at least two weeks out, so employees have time to read it and ask questions.
  3. Share it where people can find it later. A PDF in an email gets lost. Put it in the app or shared drive employees already use, and keep a printed copy available for anyone without a device.
  4. Walk through the changes. In a short team meeting, cover the rules people use every week: clocking in, requesting PTO, calling in sick, and who to talk to about problems.
  5. Collect a signed acknowledgment from every current employee and from each new hire during onboarding. E-signatures work. Keep each form in the personnel file.
  6. Track who has not signed and follow up within a week. If someone refuses, record the date you gave them the handbook and have a witness initial the note.

How often to update your employee handbook

Review the whole handbook at least once a year. Late fall works well, because many state minimum wage, sick leave and notice changes take effect on January 1. Update it sooner when:

  • You hire your first employee in a new state or city.
  • You cross a coverage threshold, such as 15 employees (Title VII and the ADA), 20 (the ADEA) or 50 (the FMLA).
  • You change pay frequency, the workweek, PTO accrual, holidays or the attendance policy.
  • A new law or agency decision affects a policy, such as an NLRB ruling on work rules.

Give each edition a version number and effective date, tell employees what changed, and collect a new acknowledgment for each major revision.

Frequently asked questions

Is an employee handbook required by law?

No federal law requires a private employer to have a handbook. But once you have one, some laws decide what goes in it. FMLA-covered employers must include the FMLA general notice in a handbook that covers benefits or leave, and California and New York add their own handbook content rules for covered employers.

Is an employee handbook a legal contract?

It should not be, but courts in some states have treated handbook promises as binding. A clear statement that the handbook is not a contract, an at-will statement, and the right to change policies with notice lower that risk. Avoid promising specific discipline steps or job security you do not mean to guarantee.

How often should I update my employee handbook?

Review it at least once a year, ideally in late fall, because many state minimum wage, sick leave and notice rules take effect on January 1. Also update it whenever you open in a new state, cross a headcount threshold such as 15 or 50 employees, or change a pay, PTO or attendance practice.

Do employees have to sign the handbook acknowledgment?

Federal law does not require a signature, but a signed or e-signed acknowledgment is your proof that each employee received the handbook and the at-will and policy terms. If someone refuses to sign, note the date you gave it to them, have a witness initial it, and keep that record in their file.

Can I use one employee handbook for employees in several states?

Yes. Most multi-state employers keep one core handbook with federal-level policies and add a state supplement for each state where they have employees. The supplement covers state sick leave, meal and rest breaks, final pay, vacation payout and required notices, and it controls whenever it gives employees more than the core policy.
