PTO payout laws by state: who must pay unused vacation in 2026

The 10 states that require paying out earned vacation when someone leaves, the states where your written policy decides, the deadline that applies, and how to calculate and tax the payout.

Quick answer

  • No federal law requires a PTO payout. Whether unused vacation must be paid when someone leaves depends on the state and, in most states, on the employer's written policy.
  • Ten jurisdictions require payout of earned vacation regardless of policy: California, Colorado, Illinois, Louisiana, Maine (11+ employees), Massachusetts, Montana, Nebraska, North Dakota and Rhode Island (after one year).
  • Payout = unused hours × final hourly rate. 16 hours at $17.75 is $284.00, paid on the state's final paycheck deadline and withheld as supplemental wages.

PTO payout laws by state come down to one question: does your state treat earned vacation as wages? Where it does, unused hours must be paid when employment ends, no matter what the handbook says. Where it does not, the employer's written policy or past practice decides, and a clear "unused PTO is not paid out" line is usually enforceable. This guide sorts all 50 states and Washington, D.C. into those groups as of November 12, 2026, then covers the rules around them: use-it-or-lose-it, sick time, the deadline, the math, the tax treatment and a sample policy. For the basics of accrual and caps, start with our paid time off guide.

No federal PTO payout law: the state and your policy decide

The U.S. Department of Labor says the Fair Labor Standards Act "does not require payment for time not worked, such as vacations, sick leave or federal or other holidays," and that these benefits "are matters of agreement between an employer and an employee." So there is no federal unused PTO payout rule, and no federal deadline for paying one.

Everything comes from state wage payment laws, which fall into three groups:

  1. Payout required regardless of policy. The state defines earned vacation as wages. A policy that forfeits it at separation is void. Ten jurisdictions are here, though several attach conditions (employer size in Maine, one year of service in Rhode Island).
  2. Payout required if the policy or practice promises it. Most states. The wage law covers vacation only when the employer agreed to pay it, so the handbook controls. A handful of these states (Maryland, New York, North Carolina, Wyoming, Indiana) default to payout when the policy is silent or was never put in writing.
  3. No statute on the question. A dozen states have no wage law that mentions vacation payout. A written promise is still enforceable as a contract, but the labor department usually will not take the claim.

Two things decide which group you are really in. First, what the state calls "vacation" often includes a combined PTO bank, because the hours can be used for any reason. Second, "earned" means accrued under your own policy, so an accrual cap or a waiting period that keeps hours from accruing in the first place is treated very differently from a rule that takes earned hours away.

Use-it-or-lose-it rules and accrual caps

Payout at separation and forfeiture during employment are the same legal question asked twice: can earned vacation be taken away? States that answer no at separation usually answer no at year end too.

  • California: the Labor Commissioner calls a policy that forfeits unused vacation at a set date "an illegal policy under California law." Caps that stop accrual at a set balance are allowed, as long as employees have a reasonable chance to take the time.
  • Colorado: after Nieto v. Clark's Market, any policy term that forfeits earned vacation is void. You may cap accrual, but you cannot cap how much earned vacation carries over.
  • Montana: "So called 'use it or lose it' policies are not permitted in Montana," says the Department of Labor and Industry, but "caps, or maximum accumulation amounts can be instituted."
  • Nebraska: courts have held that earned vacation, and PTO usable for any purpose, is wages, and that a policy conflicting with that is void.
  • Illinois: use-it-or-lose-it is allowed during employment if employees had notice of the rule and a reasonable opportunity to take the time. Earned time can never be forfeited at separation.
  • Massachusetts: the Attorney General has warned that caps and use-it-or-lose-it rules can become an illegal forfeiture of wages, so get advice before using one there.
  • Most other states: use-it-or-lose-it is allowed if the policy is written and employees know about it.

The difference between a cap and a forfeiture is the whole game in payout states. A cap says "once you reach 120 hours you stop earning until you use some." Nothing earned is lost, so California, Colorado and Montana all allow it. A forfeiture says "any hours over 40 disappear on December 31." Hours that were earned are taken away, which is exactly what those states prohibit. If you want to limit your payout exposure in a payout state, use a cap at 1 to 1.5 times the annual accrual and encourage people to take their time. Is unused sick time paid out?

Usually not. State paid sick leave laws require employers to provide the leave, not to cash it out. California's Labor Commissioner answers the payout question with "No, not unless your employer's policy provides for a payout," and points employers to Labor Code 227.3 for vacation instead. Washington L&I treats paid sick leave balances under "separate requirements" from vacation. Nebraska's statute excludes paid leave other than earned vacation from final wages "unless the employer and the employee ... have specifically agreed otherwise."

The exception is the combined PTO bank. If employees can use any hour in the bank for a beach day, the payout states treat every hour as vacation. California says a PTO plan that mixes vacation and sick leave "is subject to the same rules as other vacation policies," and Nebraska's Supreme Court reached the same result in Fisher v. PayFlex Systems (2013). In those states, a split plan (separate sick and vacation buckets) can cost less at separation than one bank, because the sick bucket is not paid out.

Two more wrinkles. California requires an employer to reinstate unused sick leave if it rehires the employee within 12 months, unless the hours were in a PTO bank and were paid out at separation. And Chicago's Paid Leave and Paid Sick and Safe Leave Ordinance† requires employers with 51 or more covered employees to pay out unused "paid leave" (the any-reason bucket, not paid sick leave) at separation; confirm the current thresholds with the Chicago Office of Labor Standards.

When the payout is due: the final paycheck deadline applies

A PTO payout is part of final wages, so it is due on the same day as the last paycheck. That deadline varies more than the payout rule itself, and in several states it depends on whether the employee quit or was let go. How to calculate a PTO payout (worked example)

The formula is short: payout = unused hours × final hourly rate. Use the balance on the last day of work, after the final pay period's accrual, and the rate in effect that day, which is what California requires and what most policies say.

Take Chris Walker, a barista at Northfield Coffee in Austin, Texas, who earns $17.75 an hour. Northfield's written policy says unused PTO is paid at separation, which makes it wages under the Texas Payday Law. He leaves on Friday, November 20, 2026 with 16 unused PTO hours. The figures are illustrative and use 2026 federal rates; Texas has no state income tax.

  1. Gross payout: 16 × $17.75 = $284.00.
  2. Federal income tax withholding, if paid as a separate check at the IRS supplemental flat rate: 22% × $284.00 = $62.48.
  3. Social Security: 6.2% × $284.00 = $17.61. Medicare: 1.45% × $284.00 = $4.12.
  4. Net payout: $284.00 − $62.48 − $17.61 − $4.12 = $199.79.

Because Chris was laid off, Texas gives Northfield six days to pay the $284.00 along with his final hours. If the shop also offers severance pay, that is a separate, voluntary payment; the PTO payout is owed first and on the deadline.

Salaried employees. Convert salary to an hourly rate first: annual salary ÷ 2,080 hours. A $52,000 manager has an hourly equivalent of $25.00; 24 unused hours is 24 × $25.00 = $600.00. If your policy tracks PTO in days, multiply days by the employee's scheduled daily hours before you apply the rate.

Taxes. IRS Publication 15 (2026) says vacation pay is withheld like regular wages when it covers a vacation period, but a lump-sum payment "for unused vacation leave" is treated as supplemental wages. If you withheld income tax from the employee's regular wages this year or last, you may either withhold a flat 22% on a separately paid or separately identified payout, or add it to the regular final wages and withhold on the total. Supplemental wages over $1 million in a year are withheld at 37%. Social Security (6.2% up to the 2026 wage base of $184,500) and Medicare (1.45%) apply either way, and the payout counts as taxable wages for state income tax and unemployment insurance. Report it on the employee's W-2 with their other wages. Our guide to FICA taxes covers the employer match.

Partial-period accrual. If PTO accrues per hour worked, run the final pay period's hours through accrual before you pay out. Emily Carter accrues 0.0385 hours per hour worked; 78 hours worked in her last period adds 78 × 0.0385 = 3.00 hours to her balance. Skipping this step is the most common underpayment we see, and in payout states it is a wage claim waiting to happen.

Writing a PTO payout policy (sample)

A payout policy has four jobs: say whether unused PTO is paid, say what "earned" means, say how the number is calculated, and fit the law of every state where you have employees. Copy the sample below into your handbook and edit the bracketed choices. In a payout state, pick the "is paid" option; the alternative is not enforceable there anyway.

Sample PTO payout policy
PAID TIME OFF AT SEPARATION 1. Payout of unused PTO. When your employment with [Company name] ends for any reason, [all earned and unused PTO is paid in your final paycheck / earned and unused PTO is not paid out, except where state law requires payment of earned vacation or PTO]. [Employees who resign must give at least [two weeks'] written notice to receive the payout, unless state law provides otherwise.] 2. What counts as earned. PTO is earned as it accrues under the Paid Time Off policy in section [X] of this handbook, through your last day of work. PTO that has not yet accrued is not earned. Hours advanced to you before they accrued [will be deducted from your final pay where state law allows / are not recovered]. 3. Rate of pay. The payout equals your earned, unused PTO hours multiplied by your base hourly rate on your last day of work. For salaried employees, the hourly rate is annual salary divided by 2,080. Shift differentials, tips, bonuses and overtime premiums are not included. 4. Timing. The payout is included in your final paycheck, which is issued on the date required by the law of the state where you work and no later than the next regular payday. 5. Sick leave. [Sick leave is part of the PTO bank and is paid out with it. / Sick leave is tracked separately from PTO and is not paid out at separation, unless state or local law requires it.] If you are rehired within [12] months, unused sick leave will be reinstated where the law requires. 6. Taxes. PTO payouts are wages. Federal, state and local taxes are withheld, and the payout is reported on your Form W-2. 7. State supplements. Employees in California, Colorado, Illinois, Louisiana, Maine, Massachusetts, Montana, Nebraska, North Dakota and Rhode Island: see the [state] supplement to this handbook. Where a state or local law gives you more than this policy, the law controls. Acknowledgment. I have read and understand the Paid Time Off at Separation policy. Employee name: ______________________ Signature: ______________________ Date: ____________

Three drafting notes. First, keep the acknowledgment: Wyoming excludes vacation from wages only if the employee acknowledged the forfeiture policy in writing, and Maryland and North Carolina expect notice of the rule, so a signed page is cheap insurance. Second, a notice condition ("give two weeks to get your payout") is common in policy-decides states, and we cover how employees should give it in our two weeks notice letter guide, but it is void in the ten payout states. Third, put the policy where people can find it: a handbook in the employee app beats a binder in the back office. What employees can do if PTO is not paid

If your final paycheck arrives without the PTO you expected, work through these steps in order.

  1. Check the policy. Find the handbook or offer letter language on payout, your last PTO balance and your state's rule in the PTO payout laws by state table above. In a policy-decides state, a clear written "no payout" rule is usually the end of the road unless the employer broke it for others.
  2. Ask in writing. Email the employer with the hours, the rate and the dollar amount, and cite the policy or statute. Many underpayments are a missed final-period accrual and get fixed in a day.
  3. File a wage claim. Every payout state and most policy-decides states take vacation claims through the labor department: California's Labor Commissioner, Colorado's Division of Labor Standards and Statistics, the Illinois Department of Labor, Montana's Wage and Hour unit, the Texas Workforce Commission and so on. Claims are free, and there is a filing deadline, often one to three years.
  4. Small claims court works in the no-statute states and for amounts under the court's limit. Bring the policy, pay stubs showing the balance and your written request.
  5. Penalties. Some states add them on top of the wages: California's waiting time penalty under Labor Code 203 can reach 30 days of pay, and several states allow double or treble damages and attorney's fees. An employment attorney will usually tell you in a short call whether a claim is worth pursuing.

For employers, the cheapest version of all this is a balance nobody disputes. Show PTO on every pay stub, run the final accrual before the last payroll, and pay on the state's deadline. Frequently asked questions

Which states require PTO payout when an employee leaves?

Ten jurisdictions require payout of earned vacation no matter what the policy says: California, Colorado, Illinois, Louisiana, Maine (employers with 11 or more employees), Massachusetts, Montana, Nebraska, North Dakota (with a narrow exception) and Rhode Island (after one year of service). In most other states, payout is required only when the employer's policy or practice promises it.

Can an employer refuse to pay out unused PTO?

In most states, yes, if a written policy says unused PTO is not paid at separation and employees were told about it. In California, Colorado, Illinois, Massachusetts, Montana, Nebraska and a few others, earned vacation is wages and cannot be forfeited at separation, so a no-payout policy is void. Sick time is a separate question.

How is a PTO payout calculated?

Multiply the unused hours by the employee's final hourly rate. A barista with 16 unused hours at $17.75 an hour is owed 16 × $17.75 = $284.00 in gross pay. For salaried employees, divide the annual salary by 2,080 to get the hourly rate. The payout is taxable and is usually treated as supplemental wages for federal withholding.

Is a PTO payout taxed differently from regular pay?

It is taxed as wages, but the withholding method can differ. The IRS treats a lump-sum payment for unused vacation as supplemental wages, so an employer may withhold a flat 22% for federal income tax if it is paid separately from regular wages, or combine it with regular wages and withhold normally. Social Security and Medicare taxes still apply.

Do employers have to pay out unused sick leave?

Usually not. State paid sick leave laws, including California's, generally do not require payout of unused sick time unless the employer's policy promises it. The exception is a combined PTO bank: where the whole bank can be used like vacation, payout states treat all of it as vacation, so the sick hours get paid out too.

When does the PTO payout have to be paid?

On the state's final paycheck deadline, because the payout is part of final wages. California requires it immediately when an employee is fired and within 72 hours when one quits without notice. Illinois and Maine require it by the next regular payday. Texas gives employers six days after a discharge. Check your state's final pay rule.
