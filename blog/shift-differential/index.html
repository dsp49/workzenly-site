Shift differential: what it means and how to calculate it

What a shift differential is, the common types, what federal law requires, and the exact math for night shifts, split windows and overtime.

Quick answer

  • A shift differential is extra pay for working less popular hours, such as nights, evenings, weekends or holidays, paid as a flat amount per hour or a percentage of base pay.
  • Federal law does not require it. The US Department of Labor says extra pay for night work is a matter of agreement between employer and employee.
  • If you pay one, it counts toward the regular rate (29 CFR 778.207), so overtime is 1.5 times the higher rate, not just the base wage.

A shift differential is an extra amount an employer pays on top of the base wage for hours worked on a less desirable shift. The most common is a night shift differential, but employers also pay premiums for evenings, weekends, holidays, on-call duty and hazardous work. Federal law does not require any of them. Once you offer one, though, federal overtime rules decide how it affects pay, and getting that part wrong is a common payroll error. This guide covers the meaning, the types, the law and the exact math.

Shift differential meaning

The plain shift differential meaning is “the difference in pay between one shift and another.” A worker who earns $18.00 an hour on the day shift and $19.50 an hour on the overnight shift has a $1.50 differential. The work is the same; the hours are harder to fill.

Employers use shift differential pay for three practical reasons:

  • Coverage. Nights, weekends and holidays are hard to staff. A premium gives people a reason to pick those shifts up instead of trading them away.
  • Fairness. Overnight and rotating schedules disrupt sleep, childcare and family time. A differential recognizes that cost.
  • Retention. On teams that run around the clock, a clear premium can keep experienced people on the shifts that most need them.

A differential is not the same as overtime. Overtime is triggered by the number of hours worked in a week. A differential is triggered by when the hours are worked, or the conditions of the work. An employee can earn both in the same week, and the two interact, as the examples below show.

Types of shift differential pay

Most policies use one or more of these six types. The names vary by employer and union contract, so define each one in writing.

  • Night shift differential. Paid for overnight hours, often a window such as 11 p.m. to 7 a.m. This is the most common type in hospitals, plants and distribution centers that run 24 hours a day.
  • Evening (second shift) differential. Paid for afternoon and evening hours, such as 3 p.m. to 11 p.m. It is often smaller than the night premium.
  • Weekend differential. Paid for hours on Saturday and Sunday, or from Friday evening to Sunday night. Some employers pay it only to staff whose normal schedule is weekdays.
  • Holiday premium. Paid for hours worked on listed holidays, often as a multiple of base pay such as 1.5 times. This is separate from paid holiday time off; our guide to holiday pay rules explains the difference.
  • On-call pay. Paid for being available to come in or take calls outside scheduled hours, usually as a flat amount per shift or per day, plus regular pay for any time actually worked.
  • Hazard pay. Paid for hours spent on dangerous, physically demanding or dirty work, such as certain cleanup, confined-space or high-heat tasks.

Flat-dollar vs percentage differentials

Every differential is set one of two ways.

Flat dollar per hour. Everyone on the night shift gets the same extra amount, for example $1.50 an hour. It is simple to explain and easy to budget. Because it is the same for everyone, it is worth proportionally more to lower-paid staff.

Percentage of base pay. The premium is a share of each person’s hourly rate, for example 10%. An employee at $20.00 earns $2.00 extra per hour; one at $30.00 earns $3.00. It keeps pace with raises automatically and keeps the gap between pay grades the same.

Here is the same 8-hour night shift under each method:

  • Flat: $18.00 base + $1.50 = $19.50 an hour. 8 × $19.50 = $156.00.
  • Percentage: $20.00 base + 10% ($2.00) = $22.00 an hour. 8 × $22.00 = $176.00.

Neither is required or preferred by law. Pick the one that matches how your pay scale works, write it down and apply it the same way to everyone in the same role.

Is shift differential required by law?

No federal law requires private employers to pay a shift differential. The US Department of Labor’s Wage and Hour Division states that the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) does not require extra pay for night work, and that extra pay for night shifts is a matter of agreement between the employer and the employee or the employee’s representative. The Department of Labor also says the FLSA does not require pay for holidays or premium pay for weekend and holiday work. The DOL’s hazard pay page says the FLSA does not address hazard pay except to require that it be included in the regular rate.

Two things can still make a differential mandatory for a given employer:

  • A contract. A union collective bargaining agreement, an offer letter or a written policy that promises the differential.
  • Government contracts and public employers. Federal employees have their own night pay rules, covered below. Some prevailing-wage contracts set their own fringe and premium terms.

State and local laws change often, so check your state labor department before assuming nothing applies to you.

The overtime rule you cannot skip

Once you pay a differential, federal regulation 29 CFR 778.207(b) says the FLSA requires extra premiums such as nightshift differentials, and premiums for hazardous, arduous or dirty work, to be included in the employee’s regular rate. The regular rate is total pay for the workweek divided by total hours worked (29 CFR 778.109). Overtime for non-exempt employees is then owed at 1.5 times that regular rate for hours over 40 in the workweek.

On-call pay works the same way: 29 CFR 778.223 says on-call payments must be included in the regular rate. The main exception is a weekend or holiday premium of at least 1.5 times the normal rate for the same work. Under 29 CFR 778.203, that kind of premium can be treated as an overtime premium, left out of the regular rate and credited toward overtime owed. A smaller weekend premium, such as $1.00 an hour, must be included in the regular rate. For the full method, read our guide on how to calculate overtime.

Shift differential examples with the math

All figures are gross pay before taxes. The employees are non-exempt and the workweek is fixed.

Example 1: A shift that crosses into the differential window

A warehouse pays $18.00 an hour and a $1.25 night differential for hours worked between 6 p.m. and 6 a.m. Jordan works 3:00 p.m. to 11:30 p.m. with an unpaid 30-minute meal break from 7:00 to 7:30 p.m.

  1. Hours worked: 8.5 hours on site − 0.5 unpaid = 8 hours.
  2. Hours before 6 p.m.: 3:00 to 6:00 p.m. = 3 hours at base pay.
  3. Hours in the window: 6:00 to 7:00 p.m. (1 hour) + 7:30 to 11:30 p.m. (4 hours) = 5 hours.
  4. Base pay: 8 × $18.00 = $144.00.
  5. Differential: 5 × $1.25 = $6.25.
  6. Total for the shift: $144.00 + $6.25 = $150.25.

Some employers use a “majority of hours” rule instead: if most of the shift falls in the window, the whole shift earns the premium. Under that rule Jordan’s 5 of 8 hours is a majority, so the differential is 8 × $1.25 = $10.00 and the shift pays $154.00. Either rule is lawful if it is written down and applied consistently. The point is to decide in advance, because the time clock punches will be read one way or the other.

Example 2: Overtime with a night shift differential

Maria earns $20.00 an hour. This workweek she works 45 hours, and 20 of those hours are night hours with a $2.00 differential.

  1. Straight-time pay: 45 × $20.00 = $900.00.
  2. Differential: 20 × $2.00 = $40.00.
  3. Total straight-time earnings: $900.00 + $40.00 = $940.00.
  4. Regular rate: $940.00 ÷ 45 hours = $20.8889 an hour (about $20.89).
  5. Overtime premium: 5 overtime hours × $20.8889 × 0.5 = $52.22.
  6. Total pay for the week: $940.00 + $52.22 = $992.22.

If payroll calculated overtime on the base rate alone, the premium would be 5 × $20.00 × 0.5 = $50.00 and the week would total $990.00. That is $2.22 short. It looks small, but it repeats every week for every employee who works nights and overtime, and back wages can be claimed for prior years. Example 3: Percentage differential on a full night shift

A plant pays a 10% night differential on base pay for hours from 11 p.m. to 7 a.m. Sam earns $24.00 an hour and works 11 p.m. to 7:30 a.m. with an unpaid 30-minute break at 3 a.m.

  1. Hours worked: 8.5 hours on site − 0.5 unpaid = 8 hours.
  2. Hours in the window: 11 p.m. to 3 a.m. (4 hours) + 3:30 to 7:00 a.m. (3.5 hours) = 7.5 hours. The last half hour, 7:00 to 7:30 a.m., is after the window closes.
  3. Differential rate: $24.00 × 10% = $2.40 an hour.
  4. Base pay: 8 × $24.00 = $192.00.
  5. Differential: 7.5 × $2.40 = $18.00.
  6. Total for the shift: $192.00 + $18.00 = $210.00.

Paying the premium on all 8 hours would give 8 × $26.40 = $211.20. That overpays by $1.20. Paying from the schedule instead of the punches can also go the other way: an employee who stays an extra hour inside the window is owed the premium for that hour too. The policy has to say exactly when the window opens and closes, and payroll has to follow the punches.

Industries that commonly pay shift differentials

Any business that runs outside daytime hours may pay a differential. These four use them often, and the reasons are specific to each.

  • Healthcare. Hospitals, nursing homes and home care run all day and night. Nurses, aides and techs often earn separate evening, night, weekend and on-call premiums, usually set by policy or union contract. Our guide to healthcare scheduling software covers staffing those shifts.
  • Manufacturing. Plants that run two or three shifts often pay a second-shift and a larger third-shift premium to keep lines staffed.
  • Warehouses and distribution. Overnight receiving, picking and loading keep goods moving for next-day delivery, so night premiums are common.
  • Retail. Overnight stocking crews, 24-hour stores and holiday rush schedules may carry night, weekend or holiday premiums.

Security, call centers, utilities, transportation and hospitality also use them. Practices vary widely by employer, so look at what local employers in your field offer rather than relying on a national figure.

Night shift differential for federal employees

Federal workers have a written rule, which makes it a useful reference point even though it does not apply to private employers. According to the US Office of Personnel Management (OPM):

  • General Schedule (GS) employees receive night pay of 10% of their rate of basic pay for regularly scheduled work between 6 p.m. and 6 a.m. Basic pay here includes locality pay or special rate supplements. The rule is in 5 CFR 550.121.
  • OPM says GS night pay is paid in addition to overtime, Sunday or holiday premium pay, and is not included in the basic pay used to compute those premiums. These are Title 5 rules for federal agencies, not the FLSA regular-rate rule private employers follow.
  • An employee is generally not entitled to night pay for voluntarily working flexible hours between 6 p.m. and 6 a.m.
  • Federal Wage System (FWS) trade and craft employees use a different rule: 7.5% of basic pay when the majority of a regularly scheduled shift falls between 3 p.m. and midnight, and 10% when the majority falls between 11 p.m. and 8 a.m. The differential is paid for the whole shift.

A worked example for a GS employee: a basic rate of $30.00 an hour (including locality) on a regularly scheduled 10 p.m. to 6 a.m. shift earns 10% × $30.00 = $3.00 an hour in night pay, or 8 × $3.00 = $24.00 for the shift. The $30.00 rate is illustrative; actual GS rates come from OPM’s published pay tables.

How to write a shift differential policy

A short, specific policy prevents most disputes. Put it in your handbook (our employee handbook template has a compensation section to build on) and cover these points:

  1. Who is eligible. Name the roles or departments, and say whether part-time, temporary and salaried employees qualify.
  2. The exact windows. Give clock times and days, for example “hours actually worked between 11:00 p.m. and 7:00 a.m.”
  3. The amount and method. State a flat dollar amount or a percentage of base pay, and what “base pay” includes.
  4. How partial shifts are counted. Only hours inside the window, or the whole shift if the majority falls inside it.
  5. Stacking. Whether night, weekend and holiday premiums combine, or only the highest applies.
  6. Paid time off. Whether PTO and holiday pay include the differential for employees who normally work nights. Federal law does not require it; decide and write it down.
  7. Overtime. A plain statement that differentials are included in the regular rate when overtime is calculated.
  8. How it shows on the pay stub. A separate earnings line, so employees can check it. Our guide on how to read a pay stub shows what employees look for.
  9. Changes. How much notice you will give before changing or ending a differential, in line with your state’s notice rules.

What employers should do

  • Pay from actual punches, not the schedule. As Example 3 shows, a half hour past the window changes the total. Use a time clock app that records real start, break and end times.
  • Put the differential in the regular rate. Check that your payroll system includes differentials before multiplying by 1.5. This is the most common shift differential pay mistake.
  • Apply it consistently. Pay the same premium to everyone in the same role and window. Inconsistent premiums invite complaints and can raise discrimination questions.
  • Watch state overtime rules. Daily overtime states multiply the effect. In California, a night shift over 8 hours is owed daily overtime at a regular rate that includes the differential; see our California overtime law guide.
  • Keep records. The FLSA requires employers to keep payroll records for at least three years and time cards for at least two.
  • Review it once a year. If a shift is still hard to fill, the premium may be too small. If a percentage differential has grown with raises, check it still fits the budget.

When the schedule, the time clock and payroll share the same hours, premium hours and the overtime regular rate are calculated the same way every week.

Frequently asked questions

Is shift differential required by law?

Not under federal law. The US Department of Labor says the Fair Labor Standards Act does not require extra pay for night work, and the same is true for weekends and holidays. It is a matter of agreement between employer and employee, or a union contract. Check your state labor department for any state rule.

Does shift differential count toward overtime?

Yes. Under 29 CFR 778.207(b), night shift differentials and premiums for hazardous or dirty work must be included in the regular rate. Overtime is then paid at 1.5 times that higher regular rate, not 1.5 times the base wage alone, for non-exempt employees who work over 40 hours in a workweek.

What is a typical night shift differential?

There is no legal standard for private employers, and we do not publish market averages we cannot verify. Employers set either a flat amount per hour, such as $1.50, or a percentage of base pay, such as 10%. Federal General Schedule employees get 10% for regularly scheduled work between 6 p.m. and 6 a.m.

Do salaried employees get shift differential?

They can if the employer offers it. Exempt salaried employees are not owed overtime, so a differential is simply extra pay. For salaried non-exempt employees, any differential becomes part of total pay for the week and raises the regular rate used to calculate overtime, the same as for hourly staff.

Can an employer stop paying shift differential?

Generally yes, for future work, because federal law does not require it. The employer must still pay any differential already earned, give the notice your state requires before changing pay, and follow any union contract or written agreement. Announce the change in writing before the pay period it affects.
