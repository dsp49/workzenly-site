Quick answer

  • Time and attendance tracking records when people start, break and stop work, then checks it against the schedule and leave calendar to show lateness, absences and overtime.
  • The FLSA does not require a particular method, but it does require complete records of hours worked each day and week, kept for at least two years (time cards) and three years (payroll).
  • Choose by how your staff actually clock in (phone, shared tablet or wall clock), whether hours flow into payroll without retyping, and whether the data it collects is legal in your state.

Time and attendance tracking is how an employer knows who worked, for how long, and who did not show up when they were scheduled. For a small business with hourly staff, it is also the source of every number on a paycheck. Pick the wrong setup and you pay for it twice: once in the hours a manager spends fixing time cards every Friday, and again if a wage claim shows your records cannot back up what you paid. This guide compares the main methods, lists what any time and attendance system has to do under federal rules, and gives you a checklist and the privacy questions to ask before you sign with any vendor.

What time and attendance tracking covers

"Time" and "attendance" sound like the same thing, but they answer different questions. Time tracking answers how many hours do we owe this person? Attendance tracking answers did this person work the shift they were scheduled for? A useful system answers both from one punch.

  • Clocking in and out. The start and end of each shift, with the exact time and, for mobile clocks, where the punch happened.
  • Breaks. Unpaid meal breaks taken out of the day, short paid breaks left in, and a record that state-required breaks were offered.
  • Overtime. Hours over 40 in the workweek, plus daily overtime in states that have it, flagged before the week closes rather than discovered on payday.
  • PTO and leave. Vacation, sick time and holidays recorded as paid hours that are not hours worked, so they show on the pay stub without inflating overtime. Our guide to paid time off policies covers the rules behind the balances.
  • Absences and lateness. Late arrivals, early departures and no-shows measured against the schedule. This is where employee attendance tracking meets your written attendance policy.
  • The schedule link. Without the schedule, the clock only knows someone punched in at 9:14 a.m. With it, the system knows they were due at 9:00 and are 14 minutes late.

If a product only does the first item, it is a time clock. If it does all six, it is a time and attendance system. Most small teams need the second, even if they start by shopping for the first.

What a time and attendance system must do

The US Department of Labor says the FLSA does not require any specific timekeeping method. What it requires is the record. Judge every option, from a paper sheet to the most expensive time and attendance software, against these six jobs.

Capture the records the FLSA requires

The federal recordkeeping rule at 29 CFR 516.2 lists what employers must keep for each non-exempt employee. The items a time system touches directly are:

  • The day and time the workweek begins.
  • Hours worked each workday and total hours each workweek.
  • The regular hourly rate in overtime weeks, straight-time earnings and total overtime premium pay.
  • Total wages, additions and deductions, and the date of payment and the pay period it covers (usually held in payroll).

The same rule also covers identity details such as full name, address and occupation, which usually live in the employee record. Payroll records must be kept for at least three years and time cards, schedules and wage calculations for at least two. Ask any vendor how long they keep punch data and how you get it out if you leave.

Handle rounding honestly, or skip it

Under 29 CFR 785.48, rounding to the nearest 5 minutes, tenth of an hour or quarter hour is allowed only if it evens out over time and does not leave employees unpaid for time they worked. With quarter-hour rounding, a punch at 8:07 a.m. rounds to 8:00 and one at 8:08 a.m. rounds to 8:15. A setting that always rounds clock-ins later and clock-outs earlier fails the test. Some states and courts are stricter, so the safest setting is no rounding at all: if the system keeps the exact minute, pay the exact minute.

Calculate overtime the way your state does

Federal overtime is 1.5 times the regular rate for hours over 40 in a fixed workweek. Some states add daily rules; California pays time and a half after 8 hours in a workday and double time after 12, as our California overtime law guide explains. A system should also keep PTO and holiday hours separate from hours worked, because under federal law they do not count toward the 40.

Here is an illustrative week at Northfield Coffee in Austin. Jordan Lee, a barista at $18.25 an hour, works 42.5 hours. Texas follows the federal weekly rule, so the pay is 40 × $18.25 = $730.00, plus 2.5 overtime hours at $27.375 = $68.44, for $798.44 before taxes. If one Tuesday clock-out had been missed and entered from memory as an hour early, the overtime would vanish from the paycheck without anyone noticing. Know your state's break rules

Federal law does not require meal or rest breaks. When an employer offers short breaks of about 5 to 20 minutes, federal rules treat them as paid work time; a bona fide meal period, typically 30 minutes or more with the employee fully relieved of duty, can be unpaid. Many states go further. The DOL's table of state meal period laws lists requirements in states including California, Colorado, Illinois, Minnesota, New York, Oregon and Washington, and a smaller group that also require rest periods. A good system lets you set a break rule per location and flags a shift where a required break is missing.

Keep an audit trail

Punches get edited: someone forgets to clock out, a manager fixes it the next morning. That is normal. What matters is that the original punch, the new value, who changed it, when and why are all kept. In a dispute, that edit history is the evidence that your records are accurate. A spreadsheet cannot give you this; most time and attendance software can, so ask to see it in the demo.

Send hours to payroll without retyping

At the end of the period, the system should produce approved totals per person: regular hours, overtime hours, PTO and holiday hours by type, and any premium or differential hours. The weakest version is a CSV that someone uploads or retypes. The strongest is approved hours flowing straight into payroll, with each pay stub line tracing back to a punch.

Buying checklist for time and attendance software

Take these questions into every demo. Ask the vendor to show you each one live, with a realistic shift, not a slide.

  1. Can every worker clock in? Staff with smartphones, staff without them and staff who cannot carry phones on the floor. Look for both a personal phone app and a shared device option.
  2. Does location checking match your sites? A geofence for fixed stores, GPS punches for crews on the move, and a fallback for basements and steel buildings where signals drop.
  3. Are exact punch times kept, with edit history? Original punch, edited value, editor, timestamp and reason.
  4. Can you turn rounding off? And if you keep it, does it round both ways?
  5. Does it apply your overtime and break rules? Weekly, daily and seventh-day overtime where your state has them, and break rules set per location.
  6. Is it tied to the schedule? Late arrivals, early departures and no-shows only make sense against a shift. Ask how it handles a no call, no show and whether a manager is told before a shift goes uncovered.
  7. Does PTO live in the same system? Approved time off should block the schedule, update the balance and appear on the timesheet without a separate step.
  8. How do approvals work? A manager should review exceptions (missed punches, overtime, flagged locations) and approve the rest of the week quickly.
  9. Where do hours go next? Straight into payroll, or an export someone has to upload or retype?
  10. What does it collect, and who sees it? Location, photos, biometrics. Ask how long each is kept, who can view it, and how you delete it.
  11. Can employees see their own hours? Staff who can check their week in an app catch mistakes before payday instead of after.
  12. How do you get your data out? Ask for a full export of punches and edits, because you must keep time records for at least two years even if you switch vendors.

Biometric time clocks and privacy laws

Fingerprint and face-scan clocks are sold as the cure for buddy punching. They can work, but in some states the data they collect carries real legal risk. Read these summaries as a starting point and confirm the current text with your state before you buy.

  • Illinois (BIPA). The Biometric Information Privacy Act, 740 ILCS 14, requires a private entity to tell people in writing that it is collecting biometric data, state the purpose and how long it will be kept, and get a written release before collecting it. The entity must also publish a written retention schedule and destroy the data when the original purpose is satisfied or within 3 years of the person's last interaction, whichever comes first. In employment, the release can be signed as a condition of employment, and a 2024 amendment (Public Act 103-0769) allows electronic signatures. BIPA lets individuals sue for liquidated damages per violation; the 2024 amendment treats repeated scans of the same person by the same method as a single violation, but exposure is still significant.
  • Texas (CUBI). Section 503.001 of the Texas Business and Commerce Code says a person may not capture a biometric identifier for a commercial purpose without informing the individual and getting consent first. Identifiers must be protected with reasonable care and destroyed within a reasonable time, no later than the first anniversary of the date the purpose expires. For identifiers an employer collects for security purposes, the statute presumes the purpose expires when employment ends. Only the Texas Attorney General enforces it, with civil penalties of up to $25,000 per violation. The section was amended effective January 1, 2026, so check the current text.
  • Washington. RCW 19.375 bars enrolling a biometric identifier in a database for a commercial purpose without notice, consent or a way to prevent later commercial use, and limits selling or disclosing it. The chapter says collecting identifiers for a security purpose does not need notice or consent, and it is enforced by the Washington Attorney General under the state consumer protection act. Whether a time clock falls inside the law depends on how it is used, so get advice before rolling one out.
  • New York. Labor Law section 201-a bars employers from requiring fingerprinting as a condition of getting or keeping a job, with exceptions such as some public and hospital employers. Commentary on the law suggests voluntary systems, or scanners that do not store an actual fingerprint, may be treated differently, so check with the New York State Department of Labor or counsel.

Other states and some cities have their own biometric or consumer privacy rules, and the list grows most years. The practical choice for many small businesses is to skip biometrics: a shared tablet with PINs, or phone punches with a geofence check, solves most buddy punching without collecting a fingerprint.

Location data and employee notice laws

A GPS or geofenced clock collects less sensitive data than a fingerprint, but it still collects location, and several states require notice of electronic monitoring. New York Civil Rights Law section 52-c, Connecticut's section 31-48d and Delaware's Title 19, section 705 all set written notice rules for certain monitoring, and California's privacy law treats precise geolocation as sensitive personal information for businesses it covers. Our guide to what a geofence is and how it works walks through each one and how to set a radius that does not reject honest employees.

The habits that keep you on the right side of most of these rules are simple: read location only at clock-in and clock-out, tell employees in writing what is recorded and who sees it, put it in your employee handbook, and offer a shared tablet for anyone who cannot or will not use a personal phone. A flagged punch is a reason to review the time, not a reason to skip paying for hours someone worked.

Honest trade-offs

Every time and attendance system shifts work from one place to another. Know which trade you are making.

  • Accuracy versus friction. A tight geofence, photo capture or biometric scan makes punches harder to fake and also harder for honest staff to complete. Start generous, review flagged punches for a few weeks, then tighten.
  • Phones versus shared devices. Personal phones cost you nothing in hardware but depend on employees' devices, data plans and goodwill. A kiosk is under your control but is one more thing to charge, mount and replace.
  • Standalone clock versus connected system. A standalone time clock is quicker to set up. A system that also runs schedules, PTO and payroll takes longer to set up but removes the weekly export and retyping.
  • Strict lateness rules versus morale. Once lateness is measured to the minute, staff notice. Pair the system with a clear, consistent tardiness policy that defines grace periods before you switch it on.
  • More data versus more duty. Every field you collect (location trails, photos, scans) is a field you must protect, disclose and eventually delete. Collect what the timesheet needs.

Costs to plan for

Vendors price time and attendance software in different ways, and prices change often, so compare current quotes on each vendor's site. Beyond the subscription, budget for:

  • Pricing model. Per employee per month, per active user, per location, or a base fee plus users. Check whether seasonal or part-time staff count when they are not working.
  • Feature tiers. GPS, geofencing, scheduling, PTO and payroll connections are often in higher tiers than the basic clock.
  • Hardware. Tablets, wall mounts and chargers for kiosks; terminals and replacement parts for biometric clocks.
  • Payroll connection. A separate payroll service may charge for the integration, or leave you uploading files by hand.
  • Setup time. Entering employees, sites, break rules and PTO balances, and training managers to approve timesheets.
  • The hidden weekly cost. The manager hour spent reconciling time cards, schedules and payroll. This is the cost a connected system is meant to remove, so measure it before and after.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between time tracking and attendance tracking?

Time tracking records the hours someone worked so they can be paid. Attendance tracking compares those punches with the schedule and the leave calendar to show who was late, absent, on PTO or a no-show. A good time and attendance system does both from the same punch, so pay and attendance never disagree.

Does federal law require a time clock?

No. The FLSA does not require any particular timekeeping method. Paper, a punch clock or an app are all acceptable if the records are complete and accurate. For non-exempt staff you must record hours worked each workday and workweek, keep payroll records for three years and keep time cards for two.

Are fingerprint time clocks legal?

In most states, yes, but some states regulate them closely. Illinois requires written notice, a written release and a public retention policy before you collect a fingerprint or face scan. Texas and Washington have their own biometric rules, and New York limits required fingerprinting of employees. Check your state before you buy.

Can a time and attendance system round punches?

Federal rules allow rounding to the nearest 5 minutes, tenth of an hour or quarter hour if it evens out over time and does not underpay employees. Some states and courts are stricter. A system that keeps exact punch times lets you pay the real minute and avoid the question entirely.

What should a time and attendance system export to payroll?

At minimum, each employee's regular hours, overtime hours, paid leave hours by type and any premium hours, for a pay period that matches your payroll. Better systems send approved hours straight into payroll, so nobody retypes them, and keep the original punches and edits behind every total.
