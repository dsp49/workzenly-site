Quick answer

  • Military time counts the day from 0000 (midnight) to 2359. From 0100 to 1159 the hour is the same as AM time; from 1300 to 2359 subtract 12 to get PM time. 1800 is 6:00 PM.
  • Minutes never change: 1830 is 6:30 PM, 0745 is 7:45 AM. Say the hour then the minutes: eighteen thirty, zero seven forty-five.
  • Midnight is written 0000 at the start of a day. 2400 means the end of a day, and most time clocks and schedules never use it.

How to read military time

Military time is four digits. The first two are the hour on a 24-hour clock, from 00 to 23. The last two are the minutes, from 00 to 59. There is no colon and no AM or PM, because the hour already tells you which half of the day it is.

  1. Split the digits into hours and minutes. 1545 is 15 hours and 45 minutes.
  2. Check the hour. 00 is the midnight hour (12:xx AM). 01 to 11 are morning hours and read the same as AM time, without the leading zero. 12 is the noon hour (12:xx PM). 13 to 23 are afternoon and evening hours: subtract 12 and add PM.
  3. Keep the minutes. 1545 becomes 3:45 PM. 0920 becomes 9:20 AM. 0015 becomes 12:15 AM.

To go the other way, reverse it. AM hours from 1 to 9 take a leading zero (7:45 AM is 0745). 10 and 11 AM stay as they are (1120). Noon stays 12 (1230 is 12:30 PM). PM hours from 1 to 11 add 12 (6:30 PM is 1830). The only odd case is the midnight hour: 12:30 AM is 0030, not 1230.

Spoken military time reads the hour, then the minutes. On the hour, the hour is followed by “hundred”: 0600 is “zero six hundred”, 1400 is “fourteen hundred”. With minutes, say both pairs: 0745 is “zero seven forty-five”, 1830 is “eighteen thirty”. Minutes under 10 keep their zero: 2105 is “twenty-one zero five”. In the US military the word “hours” is often added at the end (“eighteen hundred hours”), and in civilian settings most people simply read the digits. Some people say “oh” instead of “zero” (“oh seven hundred”); the military prefers “zero” because it is harder to mishear on a radio.

Midnight: 0000 or 2400?

Both exist, and they mean different things. The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), in its times-of-day FAQ, describes the 24-hour convention as 0000 for midnight at the beginning of a day and 2400 for the end of a day. 2400 on Tuesday and 0000 on Wednesday are the same instant; the number you write says which day you are talking about.

In practice, almost every clock, time clock, scheduling tool and payroll system runs the day from 0000 to 2359 and never displays 2400. Even the Navy and Marine Corps correspondence manual (SECNAV M-5216.5), which sets the writing style for official Navy documents, is widely reported to have moved from a 0001 to 2400 day to a 0000 to 2359 day in its 2015 edition. The safe habit for a workplace is:

  • Write midnight as 0000 and attach the date (“Wed 0000”). Never type 2400 into a time clock; most will reject it.
  • If a paper schedule needs to show a shift ending at midnight, “2200 to 2400” is understandable, but the time record should show the clock-out at 0000 on the next day.
  • Avoid “12 AM” and “12 PM” altogether. NIST calls them ambiguous and recommends writing 11:59 PM or 12:01 AM when the day matters, or using the 24-hour clock. Noon is 1200 and midnight is 0000; that is simpler than remembering which 12 is which.

Minutes stay the same

The military time chart above lists whole hours only, because only the hour changes between the two systems. Whatever the minutes are in 12-hour time, they are the same in military time, and the other way round. That is why converting a time with minutes is no harder than converting an hour:

Time zones and Zulu time

Military time on its own says nothing about the time zone; 1800 in Austin is 1800 Central time. When a message needs to work across zones, the military adds a letter. Z stands for Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), the time at the prime meridian, and is spoken as “Zulu” from the phonetic alphabet, so 1800Z is “eighteen hundred Zulu”. The National Weather Service writes all of its forecast times this way, with a Z after the hour, and describes Zulu as the same thing as UTC for practical purposes.

To convert Zulu to US local time, subtract the zone’s offset. On standard time (early November to mid-March) Eastern is UTC−5, Central is UTC−6, Mountain is UTC−7 and Pacific is UTC−8; on daylight saving time each offset is one hour smaller. So 1800Z on November 12, 2026 is 1300 Eastern, 1200 Central, 1100 Mountain and 1000 Pacific. For most employers this only matters for teams split across states or for dispatch, aviation and logistics work that uses UTC in its paperwork. Time clock software records each punch in the location’s local zone, which is what payroll needs; pick one zone per worksite and keep schedules and timesheets in it.

Why timesheets and shift schedules use 24-hour time

The 24-hour clock became the default for hospitals, restaurants, warehouses and anyone who runs a night shift because it removes the single most common timesheet error: the wrong AM or PM. “10:00 to 6:30” could be a day shift of 8.5 hours or a night shift of 8.5 hours, and a missing or misread “PM” turns one into the other. “2200 to 0630” can only be one thing.

It also makes the arithmetic across midnight straightforward. Take a night shift that starts at 2200 and ends at 0630 with no unpaid break:

  1. From 2200 to midnight (2400) is 2 hours.
  2. From 0000 to 0630 is 6 hours 30 minutes.
  3. Total: 8 hours 30 minutes, or 8.5 hours.

A faster version is to add 24 to the end time whenever it is earlier than the start time: 0630 becomes 3030, and 3030 − 2200 = 830, which is 8 hours 30 minutes. Pay the shift from that total. If the worker earns a night premium for those hours, the shift differential guide shows how to add it to the regular rate before computing overtime.

A day shift converts the same way. At Northfield Coffee in Austin, Jordan Lee opens at 0530 and clocks out at 1345 with a 30-minute unpaid lunch. 1345 − 0530 = 8 hours 15 minutes; minus 30 minutes is 7 hours 45 minutes, or 7.75 hours. Written as 5:30 AM to 1:45 PM, the figures are identical; the 24-hour version is just harder to get wrong on a form that dozens of people fill in each week.

From 24-hour time to decimal hours

Payroll multiplies hours by a rate, so the hours and minutes from a time card have to become a decimal. The rule is decimal hours = hours + minutes ÷ 60. The 8 hours 30 minutes from the night shift above is 8 + 30 ÷ 60 = 8.5 hours. Jordan’s 7 hours 45 minutes is 7 + 45 ÷ 60 = 7.75 hours. A less tidy figure such as 8 hours 20 minutes is 8.33 hours, and that one is a repeating decimal, so convert the week’s total minutes once rather than rounding each day.

Common mistakes with military time

  • Adding 12 to a morning time. Only PM hours from 1 to 11 add 12. 9:00 AM is 0900, not 2100.
  • Treating 12 PM as 0000. Noon is 1200. Midnight is 0000. If a schedule says “12:00” with no AM or PM, ask.
  • Reading 00xx as 12:xx PM. 0030 is 12:30 AM, half an hour after midnight. The 00 hour is always the first hour of the day.
  • Writing 2400 in a time clock. Use 0000 on the next day. Most systems reject 2400, and a few will record it as 0000 on the same day, which moves the punch back 24 hours.
  • Dropping the leading zero. Say it however you like, but write 0745, not 745. Four digits are what make the format unambiguous.
  • Mixing formats on one record. A card with 0730 in one row and 4:15 in the next invites a 12-hour mistake. Pick one format for the whole card and the whole team.
  • Crediting an overnight shift to the wrong day. A shift from 2200 Saturday to 0630 Sunday straddles the workweek if your week starts Sunday at 0000. Your policy should say which workweek those hours count toward, and the time clock should apply it the same way every time.   • Confusing Zulu with local time. 1800Z is 1200 Central on standard time. If a document carries a Z, convert before putting it on a schedule.

Frequently asked questions

What time is 1800 in military time?

1800 is 6:00 PM. The first two digits are the hour on a 24-hour clock; 18 is more than 12, so subtract 12 to get 6, and any hour from 1200 to 2359 is PM. It is spoken as eighteen hundred. 1830 would be 6:30 PM, spoken eighteen thirty.

Is midnight 0000 or 2400 in military time?

Both are used, and they mean two different things. NIST describes 0000 as midnight at the beginning of a day and 2400 as the end of a day. Most time clocks, schedules and software run the day from 0000 to 2359 and never show 2400, so write midnight as 0000 with the new date.

How do you say military time out loud?

Read the hour, then the minutes. On the hour, say the hour followed by hundred: 0700 is zero seven hundred, 1300 is thirteen hundred. With minutes, say both pairs: 0745 is zero seven forty-five, 1830 is eighteen thirty, 2105 is twenty-one zero five. Adding the word hours at the end is optional.

How do I convert 12-hour time to military time?

For AM times from 1:00 to 11:59, keep the hour and add a leading zero if it has one digit: 7:45 AM is 0745. Midnight hours (12:xx AM) become 00xx, so 12:30 AM is 0030. Noon hours (12:xx PM) stay 12xx. For PM times from 1:00 to 11:59, add 12 to the hour: 6:30 PM is 1830. Minutes never change.

Is military time the same as the 24-hour clock?

They count hours the same way, from 0 to 23. The difference is style. Military time is written as four digits with no colon (1830) and often spoken with hundred or hours. The civilian 24-hour clock keeps a colon (18:30) and is the format most time clocks, hospitals and transit schedules use. Either converts to 6:30 PM.

Why do timesheets and schedules use military time?

Because it removes AM and PM errors. A shift written as 10:00 to 6:30 could be a day shift or a night shift; 2200 to 0630 can only be one thing, and the hours worked (8.5) are simple to add. The FLSA requires employers to record hours worked each day and each workweek, and unambiguous times make those records easier to defend.
