Quick answer

  • Hours worked = clock-out time − clock-in time − unpaid meal break. 8:00 a.m. to 4:30 p.m. with a 30-minute lunch is 8:00 hours, or 8.00 in decimal.
  • Under federal law, breaks of about 20 minutes or less are paid. Only bona fide meal periods, typically 30 minutes or more with no duties, can be unpaid.
  • Pay = decimal hours × rate, plus overtime at 1.5× for hours over 40 in the workweek. The default week below, 41:30 hours at $18, pays $760.50.

How to use the time card calculator

The calculator works like a paper time card, one row per day of a single workweek.

  1. Enter clock-in and clock-out times. Use the time each shift actually started and ended. Your browser shows a.m. and p.m., so 4:30 p.m. is entered as 4:30 PM, not 16:30.
  2. Enter the unpaid break in minutes. This is the meal break you clocked out for, usually 30 or 60 minutes. Do not enter short rest breaks. They are paid time under federal law (see below).
  3. Leave days off blank. A day with no clock-in or clock-out shows as Off and adds nothing.
  4. Enter the hourly rate and choose overtime. Keep 1.5× for most non-exempt hourly employees. Choose straight time if you only want to total hours, or if you are exempt.

The result updates as you type. Each day shows hours and minutes first, such as 8:45, then decimal hours in brackets, such as 8.75. Payroll systems multiply the decimal figure by the rate. If you only need to convert a single number, the hours to decimal calculator does that on its own.

Hours calculator with lunch: what to deduct

The most common time card mistake is deducting the wrong breaks. The U.S. Department of Labor’s Wage and Hour Division sets two different rules under the FLSA.

  • Short rest breaks are paid. Breaks of about 5 to 20 minutes, such as a coffee or restroom break, count as hours worked. They must be paid and they count toward the 40 hours for overtime. Do not subtract them on a time card.
  • Bona fide meal periods can be unpaid. A meal break of typically 30 minutes or more does not have to be paid, but only if the employee is completely relieved from duty. Someone who eats at the desk while answering phones, or watches the register during lunch, is working, and that time is paid.
  • Federal law does not require breaks. The FLSA does not make employers offer lunch or coffee breaks. Some states do, covered below.

So as an hours calculator with lunch, the rule is simple: subtract only the unpaid meal period you were fully off duty for. If you enter a break shorter than 20 minutes, the calculator still subtracts it but shows a reminder, because under federal rules that time is usually paid. The DOL also says employers do not have to pay for unauthorized extensions of a break, as long as the employer clearly told employees how long the break may last.

How the clock in calculator handles overnight shifts

Night shifts in healthcare, warehouses and restaurants often end the next morning. If the clock-out time is earlier than the clock-in time, this clock in calculator assumes the shift ended after midnight and adds 24 hours before subtracting the break.

  • 10:00 p.m. to 6:30 a.m. is 8 hours 30 minutes on the clock. Minus a 30-minute meal break, that is 8:00 hours worked (8.00).
  • 4:00 p.m. to 12:30 a.m. with a 30-minute break is also 8:00 hours.

The calculator credits the whole shift to the day it started, which is how most time cards are laid out. One case needs care: overtime is counted per fixed workweek, and each workweek stands alone. If a Saturday night shift runs past the moment your workweek starts again, the hours after that point belong to the new week. Split that shift into two rows across two weeks if it matters for overtime. Can employers round time card punches?

Yes, within limits. The federal regulation at 29 CFR 785.48 says the Department of Labor accepts the long-standing practice of recording start and stop times to the nearest 5 minutes, tenth of an hour or quarter of an hour. The condition is that rounding must not result, over a period of time, in failing to pay employees for all the time they actually worked.

In practice, a neutral rule rounds both ways. Under quarter-hour rounding, a punch at 8:07 a.m. rounds down to 8:00 and a punch at 8:08 a.m. rounds up to 8:15. A rule that always rounds clock-ins later and clock-outs earlier shortchanges employees and fails the test. The same regulation says employers need not pay for time when an employee clocks in early or stays late without doing any work, but large gaps between punches and real hours raise doubts about the records.

This calculator does not round. It uses the exact minutes you enter, which is the safest way to check a paycheck.

State meal and rest break rules

Federal law sets the floor, and states can require more. The Department of Labor’s state tables list about 20 states with meal period rules for adult workers and nine with rest period rules. A few examples:

  • California: the Division of Labor Standards Enforcement (DLSE) requires a 30-minute meal period when a work period is more than 5 hours (waivable by mutual consent if the day is 6 hours or less) and a second one after 10 hours. Rest breaks are a paid 10 minutes for every 4 hours or major fraction, not required under 3.5 hours a day. A missed meal or rest break owes one extra hour of pay at the regular rate. California also has daily overtime, explained in our California overtime law guide.
  • Washington: a 30-minute meal period for work periods over 5 hours and a paid 10-minute rest period for each 4 hours worked.
  • Oregon: a 30-minute meal period for work periods of 6 to 8 hours and a paid 10-minute rest period for each 4 hours, taken separately from the meal.
  • Colorado: a 30-minute meal period for shifts over 5 consecutive hours and a paid 10-minute rest period for each 4 hours.
  • New York and Illinois: meal periods with their own timing rules. Illinois requires at least 20 minutes for employees working 7.5 continuous hours or more.

Exemptions, industry rules and collective bargaining agreements change these results, so confirm the details with your state labor department before setting a break policy.

What employers need to get right

Time cards are legal records, not just a pay worksheet. The DOL’s recordkeeping rules under the FLSA set out what to keep.

  • Record hours daily and weekly. For each non-exempt employee, keep hours worked each day and total hours each workweek, the day and time the workweek begins, the regular hourly rate, straight-time and overtime earnings, and total wages paid.
  • Keep them long enough. Time cards, work schedules and other wage computation records must be kept for at least two years. Payroll records must be kept for at least three years.
  • Any method works if it is accurate. The FLSA requires no particular form. A time clock, a timekeeper or employee-written times are all acceptable as long as the records are complete and accurate.
  • Pay for real hours, not the schedule. For fixed schedules, you may record the schedule and note that it was followed, but you must record actual hours whenever they differ.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate hours worked with a lunch break?

Subtract the clock-in time from the clock-out time, then subtract the unpaid lunch. A shift from 8:00 a.m. to 4:30 p.m. is 8 hours 30 minutes on the clock. Minus a 30-minute lunch, it is 8 hours worked. Only deduct a meal break of about 30 minutes or more where the employee is fully relieved of duty.

Are 15-minute breaks paid?

Under federal law, yes. The Department of Labor treats short breaks of about 5 to 20 minutes as hours worked, so they must be paid and they count toward overtime. Federal law does not require employers to offer breaks at all, but several states, including California, Colorado, Oregon and Washington, require paid 10-minute rest periods.

How do I convert minutes to decimal hours for payroll?

Divide the minutes by 60. Fifteen minutes is 0.25 hours, 30 minutes is 0.50 and 45 minutes is 0.75, so 8 hours 45 minutes is 8.75 hours. Multiply decimal hours, not hours and minutes, by the hourly rate: 8.75 hours at $18 an hour is $157.50 before taxes.

How does the calculator handle overnight shifts?

If the clock-out time is earlier than the clock-in time, the calculator assumes the shift ended the next morning and adds 24 hours. A shift from 10:00 p.m. to 6:30 a.m. with a 30-minute break counts as 8 hours. The calculator credits the whole shift to the day it started.

Can my employer round my time card?

Yes, within limits. The federal rule at 29 CFR 785.48 accepts rounding to the nearest 5 minutes, tenth of an hour or quarter hour, as long as over time it does not leave employees unpaid for time they actually worked. Rounding that consistently cuts time in the employer’s favor fails that test.

How long do employers have to keep time cards?

Under the FLSA, employers must keep time cards and other wage computation records for at least two years and payroll records for at least three years. The records must show hours worked each day and the total for each workweek. Some states require longer retention, so check your state labor department.
