An attendance policy tells employees when they are expected at work, how to report an absence or late arrival, and what happens when attendance problems repeat. This page gives you the text first. Template 1 is a standard attendance and punctuality policy for hourly, shift-based workplaces. Template 2 is a no-fault attendance point system with a points table and discipline steps. Template 3 is a short version for office and hybrid teams, and Template 4 is an acknowledgment form. Replace the [bracketed] items, delete what does not apply, and read the legal cautions before you roll anything out. Attendance policy templates to copy

Each block copies as plain text, so it pastes cleanly into Google Docs, Word or your employee handbook template. Pick Template 1 or Template 2 for hourly staff, not both. Template 3 works alongside either one if you also have office staff.

Template 1: Attendance and punctuality policy for shift workplaces
[Company name] Attendance and Punctuality Policy Effective date: [Month day, year] Applies to: [all non-exempt hourly employees at all locations] 1. WHY THIS POLICY EXISTS Our customers and coworkers count on every shift being covered. This policy explains what we expect, how to tell us when you cannot make it, and how we handle repeated problems. It applies the same way to every employee it covers. 2. SCHEDULES Schedules are posted in [the employee app / location] at least [14] days in advance. Check your schedule before each shift. If you cannot work a scheduled shift, you may ask a qualified coworker to swap, but your manager must approve the swap in [the employee app] before the shift starts. Once approved, the shift belongs to the coworker who picked it up. 3. BEING ON TIME Be clocked in and ready to work at the start of your scheduled shift. You may clock in up to [7] minutes before your shift. Do not start working before you clock in or keep working after you clock out. If you work, you will be paid for that time. A late arrival is clocking in more than [5] minutes after your scheduled start time. Leaving early without your manager's approval is an early departure. Returning late from an unpaid meal break by more than [5] minutes counts as a late arrival. 4. REPORTING AN ABSENCE OR LATE ARRIVAL If you will be absent or late, notify your manager through [the employee app / call or text to phone number] at least [2] hours before your shift starts, or as soon as you reasonably can if that is not possible. Tell us that you will be out or late and when you expect to return. You do not have to tell us medical details. A message passed on by a coworker, or a social media post, is not notice. For an absence that lasts more than one day, report each day unless you have told us your expected return date or you are on approved leave. 5. NO CALL, NO SHOW Missing a scheduled shift without any notice is a serious matter and will be handled under section 7. [Two / three] consecutive scheduled shifts missed without notice will be treated as a voluntary resignation, unless you were unable to give notice for reasons outside your control. 6. EXCUSED AND PROTECTED ABSENCES These absences are never counted against you under this policy: - Approved paid time off or vacation requested in advance - Paid sick leave used as allowed by state or local law [insert your state or city law, if any] - Leave under the Family and Medical Leave Act or a state family or medical leave law - Time off approved as a reasonable accommodation for a disability, pregnancy or religious practice - Absences related to a work injury covered by workers' compensation - Jury duty, witness duty, voting time, military leave and any other leave protected by law - Shifts canceled or changed by [Company name], and closures for weather or emergencies - [Bereavement leave under our policy] If you are not sure whether an absence is protected, tell your manager or [HR contact] and we will look into it. 7. REPEATED ATTENDANCE PROBLEMS An occurrence is one unexcused absence, late arrival or early departure. We count occurrences in a rolling [12]-month period: - [3] occurrences: documented conversation with your manager - [5] occurrences: written warning - [6] occurrences: final written warning - [7] occurrences: review for possible termination A no call, no show counts as [2] occurrences. [Company name] may skip steps for serious issues, and will consider the circumstances of each case. 8. PAY Non-exempt employees are paid for all time worked. Late arrivals and absences are unpaid unless you use paid time off or paid leave. We never dock pay for more time than you missed. 9. QUESTIONS Ask your manager or [HR contact name] at [email or phone]. This policy does not change at-will employment and may be updated with notice.
Template 2: No-fault attendance point system
[Company name] Attendance Point Policy Effective date: [Month day, year] Applies to: [all non-exempt hourly employees] 1. HOW THE SYSTEM WORKS [Company name] uses a no-fault point system. Unexcused absences, late arrivals and early departures earn points, regardless of the reason, unless the absence is excluded in section 3. You do not need to explain why you were out for the system to work, and managers do not decide case by case which reasons are good enough. Points are recorded from your time clock punches and absence reports. 2. POINTS TABLE Late arrival (more than [5] minutes up to 2 hours after shift start) ... 0.5 point Early departure without approval (up to 2 hours before shift end) ... 0.5 point Late arrival or early departure of more than 2 hours ... 1 point Absence with at least [2] hours' notice before the shift ... 1 point Absence with less than [2] hours' notice ... 1.5 points No call, no show (no notice before the shift ends) ... 3 points Consecutive absences for the same reason, reported each day ... counted as one absence 3. ABSENCES THAT NEVER EARN POINTS No points are given, and no points are added later, for any absence protected by federal, state or local law. This includes: - Leave under the Family and Medical Leave Act or a state family or medical leave law, including intermittent leave - Paid sick leave, or sick and safe time, used as allowed by state or local law - Absences approved as a reasonable accommodation for a disability, pregnancy or a related condition, or a religious practice - Absences related to a work injury covered by workers' compensation - Jury duty, witness duty, voting time, military leave, and leave for victims of crime or domestic violence where the law provides it - Any other absence protected by law These are also excluded: - Approved paid time off and approved unpaid time off - Approved shift swaps - Shifts canceled, shortened or changed by [Company name] - [Shifts you decline that were added after the schedule was posted, where a scheduling law gives you that right] - [Bereavement leave under our policy] If you think a point was given for a protected or excluded absence, tell [HR contact] and we will review it and remove it if it qualifies. You will not be penalized for asking. 4. HOW POINTS EXPIRE Each point expires [12] months after the date it was given. [Optional: if you go [90] calendar days in a row without earning a new point, [1] point is removed from your total, but your total never drops below zero.] 5. DISCIPLINE STEPS Steps are based on your point total in the rolling [12]-month period: [3] points: documented coaching conversation [5] points: written warning [7] points: final written warning [9] points: review for possible termination If one occurrence takes you past more than one threshold, you receive the step that matches your new total. You may see your current total at any time in [the employee app / by asking your manager]. A manager or [HR contact] will review your record and any protected leave before any termination decision. 6. NOTICE Report an absence or late arrival through [the employee app / phone number] at least [2] hours before your shift, or as soon as you reasonably can. For protected leave, follow our usual call-in procedure unless unusual circumstances prevent it. 7. PAY You are paid for all time worked. Points do not reduce your pay. Time missed is unpaid unless you use paid time off or paid leave. 8. OTHER TERMS [Company name] may take action outside the point system for serious misconduct. This policy does not change at-will employment. Questions go to [HR contact name] at [email or phone].
Template 3: Short attendance policy for office and hybrid teams
[Company name] Office and Hybrid Attendance Policy Effective date: [Month day, year] Applies to: [office, hybrid and remote employees] Working hours. Our core hours are [10:00 a.m. to 3:00 p.m.] [time zone], Monday through Friday. Be available during core hours on every workday unless you have approved time off. Outside core hours, work the schedule you agree with your manager. In-office days. Hybrid employees work in the office on [Tuesdays and Thursdays / the days set by your team]. Remote employees work from the location on file with [HR contact]. Tell [HR contact] before you work from a different state, even for a short time, because it can change tax and labor law obligations. Being reachable. On remote days, be signed in to [chat / email / phone] and respond within [one hour] during core hours. Put meetings and appointments that take you offline on your calendar. Absences and late starts. If you are sick or cannot start on time, message your manager through [the employee app / team chat] before your start time, or as soon as you reasonably can. Request planned time off at least [10] business days in advance in [the employee app]. You do not have to share medical details. Protected absences. Absences protected by law, including FMLA or state family leave, paid sick leave under state or local law, approved accommodations, jury duty and military leave, are never counted against you. Patterns. If attendance or availability problems repeat, your manager will talk with you first. Continued problems may lead to a written warning and further discipline, up to termination. Pay. Non-exempt employees record all time worked in [the time clock app], including remote work and after-hours messages. Exempt employees are paid their full salary for any week in which they do any work, except as federal and state law allow; attendance concerns are handled through discussion and discipline, not salary deductions for partial days. Questions go to [HR contact name] at [email].

How to fill in the attendance policy template

  1. Choose one system for hourly staff. Template 1 counts occurrences, where every unexcused event weighs the same. Template 2 weights events by how much they disrupt the schedule, so a no call, no show costs more than a 10-minute late arrival. Point systems take more tracking but feel fairer to most crews.
  2. Set the numbers to match your operation. A cafe that opens at 6:00 a.m. may need 2 hours' notice; a warehouse with on-call coverage may accept 1 hour. Pick a grace period, a notice window and thresholds, then use them for every location.
  3. Match your time clock. The early clock-in window and the late threshold should match how your time clock and any rounding rule are set up. Federal rules allow rounding only if it averages out so employees are paid for all time worked, and you must pay for any work done before the shift. Our tardiness policy guide covers grace periods and late arrivals in more detail.
  4. List your state and city leave laws in section 6 of Template 1 or section 3 of Template 2. If you are in a state with paid sick leave, name the law so managers know which absences are off limits.
  5. Decide how you handle no call, no shows. Many employers treat two or three in a row as job abandonment. Our no call, no show guide covers how to handle a first one and how to word that section.
  6. Send the draft to counsel, especially if you operate in more than one state.

What to include in an employee attendance policy

Whatever format you choose, an employee attendance policy should answer these questions in writing:

SectionWhat it answers
ScopeWho the policy covers: hourly, salaried, part-time, seasonal, each location
Schedules and swapsWhere schedules are posted, how far in advance, and how a swap is approved
DefinitionsWhat counts as late, an early departure, an absence and a no call, no show
NoticeHow and how far ahead to report an absence, and who to tell
Excused and protected absencesWhich absences never count, including every leave protected by law
Counting methodOccurrences or points, the rolling period and when points expire
Discipline stepsWhat happens at each threshold, and that serious issues can skip steps
PayThat all time worked is paid and how unpaid time is handled
Review routeWho employees ask when they think a point is wrong

Your attendance policy and your time off policy should be written together. If PTO needs 14 days' notice, say whether a same-day PTO request turns an absence into an excused one. Our guide to paid time off policies covers accrual, caps and the state sick leave laws a PTO bank has to meet.

How an attendance point system works

An attendance point system assigns a value to each unexcused event and adds them up over a rolling period. Because points are given without judging the reason, managers do not have to decide whose excuse is good enough, which is the main reason employers like it. The catch is that "no-fault" can never mean "no exceptions": protected absences stay out entirely.

Here is an illustrative example using Template 2's default numbers. Ben Alvarez is a barista at Northfield Coffee in Austin, Texas. Dates are in his rolling 12-month window.

DateEventPointsRunning totalStep
January 10Clocked in 20 minutes late0.50.5None
February 3Absent, called 3 hours before the shift11.5None
April 7Jury service0 (protected)1.5None
May 20Left 3 hours early without approval12.5None
June 8No call, no show35.5Written warning
July 1Absent, called 1 hour before the shift1.57Final written warning

On June 8, Ben's total jumped from 2.5 past both the 3-point and 5-point thresholds, so under the template he receives the written warning, the step that matches his new total. When the January 10 half point expires the following January 10, his total drops to 6.5. If he earns 2.5 more points before older ones expire, he reaches 9 and his record goes to review. The jury service day never touches his total.

How to enforce an attendance policy consistently

A policy protects you only if it is applied the same way to everyone. Discrimination claims often turn on whether one employee got a warning for something a coworker did without consequence.

  • Count from the record, not from memory. Base points on time clock punches and logged absence reports. A manager's recollection of "always late" will not hold up.
  • Check protected leave before every point. Before you record a point, make sure the absence was not FMLA leave, paid sick leave or an accommodation. Train managers to flag any absence where an employee mentions a medical condition, pregnancy, a family member's illness or an injury.
  • Tell employees their total. Share each point in writing within a few days and let people see their running total. Surprise terminations cause disputes.
  • Document every step. Each conversation and warning should state the date, the events, the current total and the next threshold. Have the employee sign or acknowledge it.
  • Review before you terminate. Have someone other than the direct manager check the full record, including any leave requests, before a termination.
  • Do not make silent exceptions. If you waive a point, write down why, so you can apply the same reasoning next time.

Legal cautions for attendance policies

No federal law requires an attendance policy, and employers can discipline for poor attendance. The limits come from leave and anti-discrimination laws. The most important rule for any point system: an absence protected by law cannot earn a point, a demerit or an occurrence.

FMLA

The Department of Labor's FMLA regulation at 29 CFR 825.220(c) says employers cannot use FMLA leave as a negative factor in employment actions such as discipline, "nor can FMLA leave be counted under no fault attendance policies." That includes intermittent leave taken a few hours at a time. The FMLA covers private employers with 50 or more employees, and our FMLA leave guide explains who is eligible. Under 29 CFR 825.215(c)(2), a perfect attendance bonus may be withheld when FMLA leave caused the employee to miss the goal, unless employees on equivalent non-FMLA leave still get it. Employers may generally require employees on FMLA leave to follow the usual call-in procedure, absent unusual circumstances.

ADA and the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act

The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission's 2016 resource on employer-provided leave and the ADA says employees with disabilities are generally subject to attendance rules, but that the ADA "requires that employers make exceptions to their policies, including leave policies," as a reasonable accommodation unless doing so causes undue hardship. That can mean excusing disability-related absences or allowing extra unpaid leave. The Pregnant Workers Fairness Act, which also covers employers with 15 or more employees, requires reasonable accommodations for known limitations related to pregnancy and childbirth. The EEOC's final rule, effective June 18, 2024, lists time off, such as for medical appointments or recovery, as a possible accommodation.

State paid sick leave laws

Many states and cities with paid sick leave laws make it illegal to count protected sick time against employees. Four examples:

  • California. The Labor Commissioner's paid sick leave FAQ says that if an employee has accrued and available sick leave and uses it for a purpose the law covers, the employer may not give an "occurrence" for the absence, because that is a form of discipline. Labor Code 234 makes an absence control policy that counts kin care sick leave toward discipline a per se violation. The FAQ also says an occurrence can still apply to any part of an unscheduled absence not covered by available sick leave.
  • New York. Labor Law 215, as amended effective in February 2023, treats "assessing any demerit, occurrence, any other point, or deductions from an allotted bank of time" for a legally protected absence as retaliation. Law-firm summaries note that giving the point is itself the violation, even if no discipline follows.
  • Colorado. The Healthy Families and Workplaces Act makes it unlawful to count paid sick leave taken under the law as an absence that may lead to discipline, discharge, demotion, suspension or other adverse action. The Colorado Department of Labor and Employment's required poster states the same rule.
  • Minnesota. Minnesota Statutes 181.9447, subdivision 6, makes it unlawful for an absence control policy or attendance point system "to count earned sick and safe time as an absence that may lead to or result in retaliation or any other adverse action."

Other states and cities have similar rules, and many also limit how much notice and documentation you can require for sick time. Check your state labor department before you set notice windows or doctor's note rules.

NLRA concerted activity

Section 7 of the National Labor Relations Act protects most private-sector, non-supervisory employees who act together about pay and working conditions, union or not. A group of employees who walk off a shift together to protest unsafe conditions or pay may be engaged in protected concerted activity, so do not treat that as a routine group of no call, no shows without talking to counsel. Also avoid rules that forbid employees from discussing their attendance discipline or schedules with each other.

Predictive scheduling laws

Oregon and several cities, including New York City, San Francisco, Seattle, Chicago, Philadelphia, Los Angeles, Berkeley, Emeryville and Evanston, have fair workweek or predictive scheduling laws for certain industries and employer sizes. These laws typically require advance notice of schedules and premium pay for changes, and many let employees decline hours added after the schedule is posted. If one applies to you, a declined added shift should not earn a point. Check the city or state labor office for the current rules and coverage.

Exempt employees

Under the federal salary basis rule, exempt employees generally cannot have their salary docked for partial-day absences, so handle their attendance through discipline rather than pay. Our exempt vs non-exempt guide explains the rules.

Attendance records to keep

Your attendance records are your evidence if a termination is challenged. Keep these, and check whether your state requires longer:

  • Time cards and punch records: at least 2 years under the Fair Labor Standards Act, according to the Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division. Payroll records: at least 3 years.
  • FMLA records: at least 3 years under 29 CFR 825.500. Medical certifications and histories must be kept as confidential medical records in files separate from the regular personnel file.
  • Discipline and termination records: the EEOC requires covered employers to keep personnel and employment records for at least 1 year, and for an involuntary termination, 1 year from the termination date. Keep them longer if a charge or lawsuit is filed.
  • Sick leave records: California requires employers to keep records of paid sick leave earned and used for 3 years, according to the Labor Commissioner. Other sick leave states set their own periods.
  • The point log itself: each point with its date, event and source punch, every removal and the reason, and the signed acknowledgment form.

How to roll out a new attendance policy

  1. Start clean. Begin counting points on the effective date. Do not apply a new point system to absences that happened before employees knew the rules.
  2. Give notice. Announce the policy at least two weeks before the effective date, and explain why you are making the change.
  3. Train managers first. Walk through the points table, the protected leave list and how to log an absence. Give them one place to send questions about protected leave.
  4. Hold a short team meeting. Cover the notice method, the late threshold, how to see a running total and how to ask for a review.
  5. Collect Template 4 from every current employee and every new hire during onboarding. E-signatures work. Track who has not signed and follow up within a week.
  6. Review after 90 days. Look at how many points were given, removed and disputed. If one location issues far more points than others, find out why before it becomes a consistency problem.

Frequently asked questions

Is a no-fault attendance policy legal?

Yes, in general. A no-fault or point-based policy that counts unplanned absences without asking the reason is lawful, as long as it never counts absences protected by law. FMLA regulations say FMLA leave cannot be counted under no-fault attendance policies, and states such as California, New York, Colorado and Minnesota bar counting protected sick leave.

Can an employer give attendance points for sick days?

Only for sick days that no law protects. If the employee uses paid sick leave under a state or city law, takes FMLA leave, or is absent because of a disability that needs accommodation, the day cannot become a point. Where no law applies, a sick day can count, though many employers still excuse it to encourage people to stay home.

How many attendance points before termination?

No law sets a number. Each employer picks its own thresholds; Template 2 starts coaching at 3 points and reviews termination at 9 points in a rolling 12 months. Choose numbers that fit how often you schedule people, put them in writing, apply them the same way to everyone, and review each termination before you act.

Can an employer require a doctor's note for an absence?

Often, but state sick leave laws limit it. Colorado, for example, lets employers ask for documentation only after four or more consecutive workdays of paid sick leave, and other states set their own limits. Under the FMLA you can require medical certification on the regulation's terms. Keep any medical notes in a confidential file, separate from the personnel file.

Do attendance policies apply to exempt employees?

Yes, but discipline works differently. You can coach, warn or terminate an exempt employee for poor attendance. Under federal salary rules you generally cannot dock their salary for partial-day absences, and full-day deductions are allowed only in limited cases, such as full days off for personal reasons. Handle exempt attendance through conversations and warnings, not pay.
