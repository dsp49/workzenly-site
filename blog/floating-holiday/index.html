Quick answer

  • A floating holiday is a paid day off the employee chooses, usually for a religious or cultural holiday or a birthday. One or two a year is common, and no federal law requires any.
  • It is separate from PTO and from the fixed holidays on your calendar. Write down whether it is granted or accrued, whether it carries over, and whether it is paid out.
  • Payout depends on the state. In California, an unconditional floating holiday is treated as vested vacation and paid at separation. Elsewhere the written policy usually decides.

A floating holiday is a paid day off that the employee picks, instead of the employer picking the date for everyone. Employers usually offer one so people can observe a religious or cultural occasion that is not on the company calendar, or take a birthday. Because the date moves from person to person, this small benefit raises big questions: how many to give, whether the days expire, and what happens to unused ones when someone leaves. This guide answers each of them, with a copyable policy and a worked example.

What is a floating holiday

The floating holidays meaning is simple: the holiday "floats" because the date is not fixed in advance. Fixed holidays such as New Year's Day, Thanksgiving and Christmas Day fall on the same date for the whole team. It is a set number of paid days, usually one or two, that each employee can place on whichever date matters to them.

Typical uses are Diwali, Eid, Yom Kippur, Lunar New Year, Juneteenth when it is not already a company holiday, a family observance, or a birthday. Some employers require a reason tied to a holiday. Many do not ask at all, which is the safer choice: a policy that makes employees explain their religion invites problems you do not need.

The U.S. Department of Labor states that the Fair Labor Standards Act "does not require payment for time not worked, such as vacations, sick leave or holidays (Federal or otherwise)." So whether you offer one, how many, and on what terms is a matter of your own written policy and your state's rules. How many floating holidays to give

No law sets a number. One or two days a year is the common range, and a team of 20 hourly staff with tight shift coverage can usually absorb that. Work backward from your schedule: how many people can be off on a Friday before the shift runs short, and how far ahead you need a request to find cover.

Three practical rules keep the benefit from becoming a scheduling problem. First, ask for notice, such as two weeks, but accept shorter notice for a religious observance that comes up unexpectedly. Second, limit how many people can take the same day only as a staffing tool, never as a way to rank whose holiday matters more. Third, apply the same rules to every employee in the same job group. Granted or accrued, part-time and new hires

You decide whether employees receive the days all at once or earn them. Both are lawful, and the choice changes how you handle new hires and anyone who leaves mid-year.

  • Granted. The full amount lands on a set date, usually January 1 or the hire date. It is simple to explain. The cost is that a person who leaves in February has already used a full benefit.
  • Accrued. The days build up across the year, for example one-twelfth of the annual amount each month. This matches the benefit to time worked, but it needs tracking every pay period.

Proration is how you treat people who do not start on January 1 or who do not work full-time. Write the formula into the policy so it is applied the same way each time.

  • New hires: annual days x months remaining in the year / 12. With 2 days a year and an employee starting November 2, that is 2 x 2 / 12 = 0.33 day, or 2.67 hours of an 8-hour day. Decide in advance whether to round up to a half day.
  • Part-time employees: either give the same number of days, each worth the employee's regular scheduled hours that day, or scale the hours by the share of a full-time week. A person scheduled 20 hours a week gets 20 / 40 x 16 = 8 hours under the scaling method. See how many hours is part-time for the usual lines.

Prorating by a written formula is generally acceptable. Treating part-timers or new hires differently with no written rule is the version that creates complaints, so write it down before the first request arrives.

Use it or lose it and carryover

Most policies expire the days on December 31, which is the use it or lose it model, because the benefit is meant to be a day taken in the year. Others allow one unused day to carry into the next year. Either is fine in most states if the policy says so.

The exception is a state that treats earned vacation as wages. California, for example, does not allow vested vacation to be forfeited, so a day that counts as vacation cannot simply expire. The next section explains when that applies. If you operate in several states, the simplest approach is to use the carryover or payout rule of your strictest state for everyone, or to publish a state supplement. The general rules for caps and forfeiture are in our PTO caps and carryover section.

Payout when employment ends

The federal government does not require payout of any vacation or holiday time, because the FLSA does not require pay for time not worked. State law and your own written policy fill the gap.

California. Labor Code section 227.3 requires that vested vacation be paid as wages at the final rate when employment ends. The Division of Labor Standards Enforcement (DLSE), part of the California Department of Industrial Relations at dir.ca.gov, presumes that leave given without conditions is vacation no matter what the employer calls it. As California employer groups describe the DLSE position, one that can be used at any time and is not tied to a specific event is vested vacation and must be paid out. One tied to a specific event, such as an employee's birthday or a choice among named holidays, is generally treated like a fixed holiday and is not paid out. We could not confirm the exact DLSE manual wording for this post, so California employers should check the DLSE Enforcement Policies and Interpretations Manual or ask counsel before finalizing a policy.

Other states. Most follow the written policy: if your policy promises payout, you owe it, and if it says unused days are forfeited, many states allow that. A few states beyond California treat earned vacation as wages. Our PTO payout laws by state post lists which states require payout and which follow the policy. In Texas, where Northfield Coffee operates, check the Texas Workforce Commission for how it treats payout under a written policy.

Whatever you choose, state it in the handbook. Silence is what turns a one-day dispute into a wage claim. Our employee handbook template has a general PTO policy that pairs with the policy text below.

Religious accommodation under Title VII

These days are often adopted for religious reasons, so it helps to know where the legal duty sits. Title VII of the Civil Rights Act requires employers with 15 or more employees to reasonably accommodate an employee's sincerely held religious belief or practice unless doing so would cause undue hardship. The U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) lists floating or optional holidays, flexible scheduling and voluntary shift swaps among common accommodations in its religious discrimination guidance on eeoc.gov.

The bar for undue hardship is higher than many employers think. In Groff v. DeJoy (2023), the Supreme Court rejected the old "more than de minimis cost" test. The EEOC now states that undue hardship is shown when a burden is "substantial in the overall context of an employer's business," which the Court framed as substantial increased costs in relation to the conduct of that particular business. Coworker inconvenience or a general preference for a uniform schedule is not enough on its own.

What this means for a floating holiday policy:

  • It can be part of an accommodation, but it does not replace the duty. An employee who needs several days, or a standing schedule change, still has to be considered individually.
  • Do not require employees to prove their religion to use one. If someone asks for a religious accommodation, route it to one named person and respond promptly.
  • Do not shrink an accommodation just because the person has floating holidays left. Offer shift swaps or unpaid time where a paid day does not solve the conflict.
  • State and local laws can add more protection, so check your state fair employment agency.

How to track floating holidays

Track it as a separate PTO type with its own balance, not as extra PTO hours. For each employee you need five things: the days granted this year, the hours used, the date each was used, any proration applied, and the carryover or expiry date. Pay is the employee's regular rate for the hours they were scheduled that day. Those hours are paid time off, not hours worked, so they do not count toward weekly overtime. Floating holiday policy template

Copy the text below into your handbook, replace each [bracketed] item, and delete the options that do not apply. This is general information, not legal advice. Have an employment attorney review it, especially if you have employees in California.

Floating holiday policy
FLOATING HOLIDAY POLICY [Company name] Effective [Month day, year] Purpose. A floating holiday is a paid day off that you choose. It lets you observe a religious, cultural or personal occasion that is not on our company holiday calendar, such as [a religious holiday, a cultural celebration or your birthday]. Eligibility. [Full-time and part-time] employees are eligible [on their first day / after [90] days] of employment. [Temporary and seasonal employees are not eligible.] Amount. Eligible employees receive [2] floating holidays each calendar year, granted on [January 1]. Employees hired after January 1 receive [2 days x months remaining in the year / 12], rounded [up] to the nearest [half day]. Part-time employees receive the same number of days, with each day equal to their regular scheduled hours for that day. How to request. Request a floating holiday through [the employee app / your manager] at least [14] days ahead. You do not need to name the holiday or your reason, and we will not ask about your religion. We approve requests based on staffing. Requests tied to a religious observance are also handled under our accommodation policy, so tell [HR contact] if you need a different date or more time. Pay. A floating holiday is paid at your regular base hourly rate for the hours you were scheduled to work that day. Those hours are paid time off, not hours worked, so they do not count toward weekly overtime. Use it or lose it. [Option A: Unused floating holidays expire on [December 31] and are not paid out, except where state law requires otherwise.] [Option B: Up to [1] unused floating holiday carries over to the next year.] [California employers: use Option B or pay out unused days; unconditional floating holidays are vested vacation.] Separation. Unused floating holidays [are not / are] paid out when employment ends[, except where state law requires payout]. Changes. [Company name] may change this policy at any time and will tell employees in advance. This policy is not a contract of employment.

Worked example at Northfield Coffee

Northfield Coffee, a café in Austin, Texas, adopts a policy of 2 floating holidays a year, granted January 1, with a day equal to 8 hours for full-time staff. It pays biweekly with a Sunday to Saturday workweek. The dates and hours below are illustrative.

  • Dana Brooks, a barista hired Monday, November 2, 2026, at $18.25 an hour, is a new hire. Her 2026 grant is 2 x 2 / 12 = 0.33 day, which is 2.67 hours. The policy rounds up to the nearest half day, so Dana receives 4 hours for 2026. On January 1, 2027, she receives the full 2 days, or 16 hours.
  • Jordan Lee, a barista at $18.25 an hour, asks for Friday, November 27, 2026, the day after Thanksgiving, for a family observance. Jordan is paid 8 hours x $18.25 = $146.00 for the day. Those 8 hours are paid time off, so they do not count toward overtime in that workweek.
  • Maya Torres, front counter at $17.50 an hour, has one unused day on December 31. The policy says unused days expire, and in Texas the written policy controls, so no payout is owed. A California employee under the same policy would need the day carried over or paid out at 8 hours x the final rate.
  • A part-time employee scheduled 20 hours a week receives 8 hours a year under the scaling formula in the policy.

Jordan's request is approved in the employee app, the 8 hours come out of the floating holiday balance, and the paid hours flow into the November 15 to 28 pay period, paid Friday, December 4. Nothing in the vacation or sick balance changes.

Frequently asked questions

What is a floating holiday?

A floating holiday is a paid day off that the employee picks, instead of the employer choosing the date. Employers usually offer it so staff can observe a religious or cultural holiday that is not on the company calendar, or take a birthday. It is paid at the regular rate, and no federal law requires it.

Is a floating holiday the same as PTO?

No. PTO is a bank of hours employees can use for almost any reason, and it often accrues over time. A floating holiday is a small, fixed number of paid days, usually one or two a year, granted as a holiday benefit. Many employers track it as its own leave type so it never mixes with PTO or sick balances.

Do you get paid for unused floating holidays when you quit?

It depends on the state and the written policy. In California, the Division of Labor Standards Enforcement treats a floating holiday that can be taken any time for any reason as vested vacation, which must be paid at separation. In most other states, the employer's written policy decides. Check your state labor department.

How many floating holidays should an employer give?

One or two a year is common, but no law sets a number. Choose a number you can staff for, and say whether the days are granted on January 1 or earned over the year. Add a day only if your team is diverse enough that the fixed holiday calendar leaves out observances people actually keep.

Does a floating holiday satisfy religious accommodation requirements?

Not automatically. Under Title VII, an employer must reasonably accommodate an employee's sincere religious practice unless it would cause undue hardship, which after Groff v. DeJoy means a substantial burden. A floating holiday can be part of the accommodation, but it may not be enough if the employee needs more days or a schedule change.

Can an employer prorate floating holidays for part-time or new employees?

Yes, in most states, as long as the policy says so in writing and applies it the same way to everyone in the same group. A common method is to scale the grant by months remaining in the year for new hires and by scheduled hours for part-time staff. Round the result in a way the policy states.
