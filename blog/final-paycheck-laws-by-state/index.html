Final paycheck laws by state: when the last paycheck is due

Federal law sets no deadline for a last paycheck. Your state does, and the clock often runs faster when someone is fired than when they quit. Here is every state and DC, plus what the check must include.

Quick answer

  • No federal deadline exists. The US Department of Labor says employers are not required by federal law to give former employees their final paycheck immediately; it is due by the next regular payday at the latest.
  • Final paycheck laws by state set the real deadline, and many make it faster for a firing than for a resignation. California pays a fired employee on the spot and someone who quits within 72 hours; Texas gives six calendar days after a discharge but waits for the next payday after a quit.
  • The check must cover every hour worked, overtime, earned commissions and, where state law or your policy requires it, unused PTO. Severance is separate.

Final paycheck laws by state answer one question that comes up at every separation: how many days does the employer have to hand over the last check? Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), the answer is simply "by the next regular payday," and the Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division says nothing in federal law requires an immediate final paycheck. Most states and the District of Columbia add their own deadline, and about a dozen of them run the clock within hours or days of a firing. This guide gives the rule for all 50 states and DC in plain words, with the fired and quit deadlines side by side, then covers what goes in the check, what can come out of it, and what happens when it is late.

The federal rule: no deadline, but the FLSA still applies

The FLSA requires employers to pay minimum wage and overtime for every hour worked, and it treats a final pay period like any other. The Department of Labor's last paycheck page says that if the regular payday for the final pay period has passed and you have not been paid, you can contact the Wage and Hour Division or your state labor department. That makes the next regular payday the outer limit everywhere in the country. Nothing in the FLSA lets an employer wait for a signed release, a returned laptop or an exit interview.

Four states have no separate final pay statute: Alabama, Florida, Georgia and Mississippi. There, the next regular payday is the rule, and an employer who misses it faces an FLSA claim rather than a state penalty. Everywhere else the state deadline controls, and when a state rule and the federal rule differ, the one that pays the employee sooner wins. That is why final paycheck laws by state, not the FLSA, decide whether a check is late.

A second federal point matters for anyone wondering whether a firing itself was legal. In every state except Montana, employment is at-will by default, so an employer can usually end the job without notice. At-will status changes nothing about final pay: all hours already worked are owed on the schedule the state sets. Our guide to at-will employment covers the exceptions.

Two patterns stand out. First, about a dozen states (California, Colorado, Connecticut, Hawaii, Massachusetts, Minnesota, Missouri, Montana, Nevada, Oregon, Utah, DC and, within a few days, Alaska, New Hampshire, New Mexico, Texas and Vermont) move fast after a firing and slow down after a resignation, because the employer chose the timing. Second, the "whichever is earlier" states (Louisiana, Nebraska, Nevada, Idaho, DC) and the "whichever is later" states (Kentucky, Tennessee) look alike at a glance and are not. A Kentucky employer on a biweekly schedule can lawfully wait 14 days; a Louisiana employer on the same schedule may have to cut a check off-cycle.

Here is how that plays out for Northfield Coffee in Austin, Texas, which pays biweekly on Fridays: November 15 to 28 is paid December 4. If a barista is let go on Tuesday, November 17, Texas gives Northfield until Monday, November 23, the sixth calendar day, so the regular December 4 payday is too late and an off-cycle check goes out. If the same barista hands in a two weeks' notice letter on November 17 and works through November 28, the final pay simply rides the December 4 payday. Across the border in New Mexico, a firing would mean five days; in California, the same day.

What the final paycheck must include

A final paycheck is ordinary wages with a deadline attached, so everything that would appear on a normal pay stub has to be there. The list below applies in every state; the PTO line depends on where you are.

  • Every hour worked through the last shift, at the regular rate, including the partial pay period after the last regular payday. Hours cannot be rounded away because the person is leaving. If you track with paper, the time card for the final week is the most likely place for a dispute, which is why a time clock app that stores punches matters most at separation.
  • Overtime at 1.5 times the regular rate for hours over 40 in the final workweek, and daily overtime in states such as California, Alaska, Colorado and Nevada. The overtime calculator shows the math when a bonus or differential changes the rate.
  • Earned commissions and non-discretionary bonuses. Several states let the employer wait until the amount can be calculated (North Carolina pays on the first regular payday after it is known; Iowa allows 30 days for a commission true-up), but the money is still owed.
  • Expense reimbursements that were submitted and approved. They are not wages, but states like California require employers to repay necessary business expenses, and it is simplest to settle them with the last check.
  • Unused PTO where state law or policy requires it. California (Labor Code 227.3), Illinois, Massachusetts and a number of other states treat earned vacation as wages that must be paid out at separation. Many more states enforce whatever the written policy promises, and some, like Maryland and Tennessee, let a written policy say that unused time is forfeited. Our guide to PTO payout laws by state lists every state, and the paid time off guide covers accrual caps and use-it-or-lose-it rules.

Put the pieces together for Emily Carter, a kitchen employee at Northfield earning $19.00 an hour. For November 1 to 14 she had 72 regular hours, 6 overtime hours, 8 PTO hours and an $86 approved expense, which the site's regular payroll shows as gross pay of $1,777.00 ($1,368.00 + $171.00 + $152.00 + $86.00). If November 14 were her last day and Northfield's written policy pays out unused PTO, an illustrative 20-hour balance would add 20 × $19.00 = $380.00, for a final gross of $2,157.00. The taxes and deductions on that stub follow the same lines as any other payday; how to read a pay stub walks through each one.

Deductions allowed from a final check

Employers often see the last check as the one chance to recover a laptop, a uniform, a cash drawer shortage or a pay advance. The law is stricter than that instinct.

The federal floor: wages must be paid "free and clear." Under 29 CFR 531.35, wages do not count as paid unless the employee receives them finally and unconditionally. The Department of Labor's Fact Sheet #16 applies this to uniforms, tools, cash shortages, breakage and damage to company property: if the item is primarily for the employer's benefit, its cost cannot push pay below the $7.25 federal minimum wage or cut into overtime for that workweek, even when the employee was negligent. Normal withholding (federal and state income tax, Social Security and Medicare) and court-ordered garnishments are always allowed.

State limits are usually tighter. Most states require written authorization for any deduction that is not tax or a benefit contribution, and several ban the common ones outright:

  • California allows deductions for a loss only if the employer proves it came from the employee's dishonesty, willfulness or gross negligence; a simple accident or shortage cannot be taken out. On a loan or advance, only one regular installment can come out of the final check, never the full balance, even with written consent.
  • Washington's Department of Labor and Industries says an employer cannot hold back a last check because an employee has not returned keys or tools.
  • Wyoming's statute expressly lets an employer offset sums the employee owes from the final wages, and South Dakota allows payment to wait until company property comes back. Those are the exceptions, not the rule.

Unreturned equipment. The safest path in most states is to pay the full final wages on time, send a written request for the equipment with a return date, and recover the cost through a small claims action if needed. If your state allows a deduction with a signed authorization, get that signature at hiring or when the equipment is issued, not on the way out, and keep the pay above minimum wage for the final week. Penalties for late final pay

A missed deadline is a wage claim in every state, and in many states it is also a penalty that grows daily.

  • California's waiting time penalty (Labor Code 203) is the one most employers have heard of. If an employer willfully fails to pay final wages on time, the employee's daily rate of pay continues for each day the wages are late, weekends included, up to a maximum of 30 days. The Labor Commissioner's example: an employee with a $56 daily rate paid 10 days late is owed $560. At Emily's rate, $19.00 × 8 hours = $152 a day, a 10-day delay would cost $1,520 and the 30-day cap $4,560, on top of the wages. A good-faith dispute over the amount can defeat the penalty; forgetting to run payroll cannot.
  • Oregon charges eight times the employee's regular hourly rate for each day the wages are late, up to 30 days, according to the Bureau of Labor and Industries; paying within 12 days of the employee's written notice can cap the penalty at the unpaid wages.
  • Arkansas doubles the wages owed if a discharged employee is not paid within seven days after the next regular payday, and Missouri keeps a discharged employee's wages running for up to 60 days after a written request goes unanswered for seven days.
  • Ohio adds liquidated damages of 6% of the unpaid amount or $200, whichever is greater, once wages are 30 days past the regular payday.
  • Everywhere, a wage claim can bring interest, attorney's fees and, under the FLSA, liquidated damages equal to the unpaid minimum wage or overtime.

The lesson for small employers is that a late check is rarely cheaper than an off-cycle payroll run. The lesson for employees is to document the last day, the hours and the date the check arrived, because the penalty is counted in days.

Direct deposit, mailed check or cash

The deadline is about when the employee can get the money, not when payroll was submitted. That makes the delivery method part of compliance.

  • Direct deposit works for a final check if the employee had already authorized it and the funds land by the deadline. Most payroll runs settle in one to two banking days, so a "within 24 hours" or "same day" state usually needs a paper check or a same-day transfer; see how long direct deposit takes before relying on it.
  • Paper check at the workplace is the default in states that say wages must be "made available" at the usual place of payment. Colorado requires the check at the work site, the employer's local office or the employee's last known address.
  • Mail is allowed in many states only when the employee asks for it, and the mailing date often counts as the payment date: California treats a mailed check as paid on the date of mailing when the employee requested it, West Virginia and Kansas count the postmark, and Utah counts a postmark within one day of the separation. Keep the receipt.
  • Pay cards are permitted in most states under the same conditions as regular paydays, with no fees that reduce the wages.

One thing a final paycheck is not: severance. Severance is extra pay that no federal law requires and most states do not require either; it is owed only if a policy, contract or agreement promises it, and it is usually paid separately, often after a release is signed. Our severance pay guide explains the formulas and the tax withholding. Final wages, by contrast, can never be conditioned on signing anything.

Employer checklist for final pay

  1. Find your deadline before the meeting. Look up the state where the employee works (not where the company is based) in the table, and note whether the separation counts as fired or quit. For a discharge in a same-day or 24-hour state, have the check ready in the room.
  2. Close the time card. Pull every punch through the last shift, including any work after the last scheduled shift, and get missing punches fixed the same day. Remove the employee from future shifts so the schedule does not show them.
  3. Calculate overtime for the final workweek, including daily overtime where it applies, and add earned commissions and bonuses that can be calculated now. Note anything that must wait until it can be calculated and the state's rule for it.
  4. Check the PTO balance against your written policy and state law. If payout is required, the balance on the last day is wages. A PTO tracker with a dated balance makes this a one-line check.
  5. Review deductions. Taxes and garnishments yes; equipment, shortages and advances only with a signed authorization your state accepts, and never below minimum wage for the week.
  6. Choose a delivery method that meets the deadline, and get a mailing address and personal email on file, since the work email will be shut off.
  7. Document the separation. Send the termination or resignation acceptance letter with the final pay date, delivery method and PTO treatment spelled out. Keep payroll records for at least three years and time cards for two under the FLSA, longer where the state says so.
  8. Handle the rest of the paperwork: benefits and COBRA notices, state separation notices where required (several states require a written notice or unemployment insurance pamphlet at separation), and a W-2 in January to the address on file.

What to do if you haven't been paid

If the deadline in the table has passed and your last paycheck has not arrived, work through these steps in order. Most late checks get fixed at step two.

  1. Confirm the deadline. Check your state's row and whether you quit or were let go. If you quit in a next-payday state, the check may not be late yet. Count the days from your last day worked.
  2. Ask in writing. Email or text the manager or payroll contact: your last day, the hours you are owed, the PTO balance if payout applies, and a mailing address. In Minnesota, Missouri and Idaho a written demand starts the employer's short clock, so put it in writing even if you have already asked in person. Keep a copy.
  3. Gather your records. Your schedule, time clock punches or photos of your time card, your last pay stub, the handbook pages on PTO, and any message about when the check would arrive. If you were terminated, keep the termination letter.
  4. File a wage claim with your state labor department. Every state agency in the table takes final pay complaints, usually through an online form, at no cost and without a lawyer. State penalties such as California's waiting time penalty are claimed here. The FLSA claim for unpaid minimum wage or overtime goes to the US Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division, which also lists every state labor office.
  5. Consider small claims court for a straightforward unpaid amount, or an employment attorney where the amount is large or your state adds attorney's fees. Retaliation for filing a wage claim is illegal under the FLSA and most state laws.

Two things you do not have to do: sign a release to get your earned wages, or return company property first (except in South Dakota, where payment can wait for the property). Returning equipment promptly is still the right move; it removes the one argument an employer might raise.

Frequently asked questions

When do you get your last paycheck if you quit?

In most states, on the next regular payday. A few states are faster: California pays within 72 hours of quitting (on the last day if you gave 72 hours' notice), Oregon pays on the last day if you gave 48 hours' notice, and Nevada and DC pay within 7 days. Check the table for your state.

When is the final paycheck due if you are fired?

It depends on the state. California, Massachusetts, Minnesota, Missouri, Montana and Nevada require pay on the day of discharge or on demand. Utah gives 24 hours, Oregon and Connecticut the next business day, Texas six calendar days, and most other states the next regular payday. Federal law sets no deadline.

Can an employer hold your last paycheck until you return equipment?

Generally no. The deadline in the table applies whether or not you have returned a laptop, keys or uniform, and Washington's Department of Labor and Industries says so directly. South Dakota is an exception that lets payment wait for returned property. Employers can ask for the items back or pursue the cost separately, within state deduction rules.

Does the final paycheck have to include unused PTO?

Only where state law or the employer's own policy requires it. California, Illinois, Massachusetts and several other states treat earned vacation as wages owed at separation. In most states the written PTO policy decides, so read the handbook. Accrued PTO that is owed must be paid on the same deadline as the rest of the final pay.

What happens if an employer pays the final paycheck late?

The employee can file a wage claim with the state labor department or the federal Wage and Hour Division. Many states add penalties: California's waiting time penalty is a day's pay for each day late, up to 30 days; Oregon charges eight times the hourly rate per day, up to 30 days; Arkansas doubles the wages after seven days.

Is severance part of the final paycheck?

No. The final paycheck covers wages already earned through the last day, and the state deadline applies to it in every case. Severance is extra pay that no federal law requires; it is owed only if a policy, contract or signed agreement promises it, and it is usually paid separately, often after a release is signed.
