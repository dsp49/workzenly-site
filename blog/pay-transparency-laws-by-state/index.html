Quick answer

  • Pay transparency laws by state: as of November 12, 2026, 14 states plus Washington, D.C. require pay information in job postings: California, Colorado, Connecticut, Hawaii, Illinois, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Minnesota, New Jersey, New York, Vermont, Virginia and Washington. Delaware is enacted but starts September 26, 2027.
  • Most laws require the minimum and maximum pay you honestly expect to pay. About half also require a general description of benefits and other compensation. Size thresholds run from none at all to 50 employees.
  • If you hire anywhere in these states, or post a remote job they can see, put a good-faith range and a benefits line on every posting. The copyable block below does it.

Pay transparency laws by state now cover most of the country’s largest labor markets, and the list grew again in 2026. Colorado started the wave in 2021. Since then California, Washington, New York, Hawaii, Maryland, Illinois, Minnesota, New Jersey, Vermont and Massachusetts have added posting rules, and Virginia, Maine and Connecticut joined in 2026. These salary transparency laws all push the same direction: tell applicants what the job pays before they apply. This guide gives every state’s effective date, employer-size threshold, what to disclose and the penalties, then covers cities, remote jobs, salary history bans and how to write a range you can defend. This is general information, not legal advice. Check the agency link in each row or ask an employment attorney before you rely on it.

How to write a good-faith pay range

Every one of the pay transparency laws by state above asks for the same thing in different words: the pay you honestly expect to offer. A range you could not defend to a labor investigator is a risk. Use these steps.

  1. Start from your real pay structure. If the role has a pay band, post the band’s minimum and maximum for the people you would hire into it. If you hire at a set rate, post the fixed rate; California, Washington and Minnesota allow that, but Minnesota bars open-ended ranges such as “$17.50 and up.”
  2. Match it to what current staff earn. If your baristas earn $17.50 to $19.00 an hour, a posting for a barista at $17.50 to $19.00 is consistent. A posting at $14 to $30 is not. Wide ranges are one of the first things regulators and applicants challenge, and some agencies warn against them.
  3. Post it as hourly or annual pay. Hourly roles use an hourly rate; salaried roles use annual pay. Say “per hour” or “per year.” If tips, commission, bonuses or overtime are part of pay, say so, as Vermont requires for tipped and commission roles.
  4. Add the benefits line if the state wants it. Colorado, Washington, Connecticut, Illinois, Maryland, Minnesota, New Jersey and Delaware require a general description of benefits or other compensation, such as paid time off, health coverage, retirement match, bonuses and tip pooling. General is enough: you do not have to list plan details.
  5. Review the range when you hire. If you pay outside the range you posted, document why, and check that your current staff in the same job are not paid below the minimum. Review the range again if you post the role months later. Our guide to minimum wage by state covers the floor your range must clear.

Remote jobs and multi-state hiring

The hardest question is whether a posting for a remote job triggers a state’s law. The answers differ, and several states have not said. What we found:

  • Colorado covers remote jobs that could be done from Colorado, and listing a job “open anywhere except Colorado” does not avoid it, according to the state’s guidance as summarized by law firms.
  • Delaware covers remote positions offered by Delaware-based employers when the law starts in 2027. Westchester County covers roles wholly or partly performed in the county, including remote ones.
  • Massachusetts ties coverage to jobs whose primary place of work is in the state; Vermont covers Vermont-based roles and remote roles with a Vermont link; Connecticut ties it to roles performed in the state or tied to a Connecticut worksite.
  • Virginia, Hawaii and others have not clearly said whether an out-of-state employer must post a range on a job that is not in the state, or whether out-of-state staff count toward the size threshold.

Across all pay transparency laws by state, the practical rule is this: if a remote role could be filled by someone living in a covered state, or you advertise it there, post the range. It costs nothing, and it keeps you out of the unclear cases. If your team hires across state lines, keep a single posting template with a pay range and a benefits line, and let your hiring tool carry it to every board.

Salary history bans

Pay transparency often comes with a ban on asking applicants what they earned before. California, Colorado, Illinois, New York, Massachusetts, Washington, Maryland, New Jersey and many other states restrict salary history questions, and Virginia added its ban on July 1, 2026, along with a rule against refusing to hire someone who will not share it. Texas, where Northfield Coffee operates, has no statewide ban. Where bans apply, you generally may not ask about pay history or use it to set pay; many allow you to use salary information an applicant volunteers. A posted range plus a standard “What are your pay expectations?” question is the simplest way to stay compliant while negotiating. Check each state’s rule before you add either question to an application.

What to do this week

  1. List the states where you hire, including remote hires and the states where your staff live. Match them to the table.
  2. Audit every open posting on job boards, your site and recruiter listings for a range and a benefits line.
  3. Fix your templates for every state on the list of pay transparency laws by state: use the posting block above, with a range that matches what current staff earn.
  4. Set a promotion process. Colorado, Illinois and a few others require you to tell current employees about openings and promotions. Post them internally, not just outside. Your onboarding flow can then collect the signed offer and forms once someone accepts.
  5. Drop salary history questions from applications and interview scripts.
  6. Keep records. Save the posting, the date and the range you published, and the pay you offered. Several states require pay and job description records for years.

Frequently asked questions

Which states have pay transparency laws in 2026?

States that require a pay range in job postings and are in effect as of November 12, 2026: California, Colorado, Connecticut, Hawaii, Illinois, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Minnesota, New Jersey, New York, Vermont, Virginia and Washington, plus Washington, D.C. Delaware is enacted but starts September 26, 2027. Rules differ on size thresholds, so check each state.

Do pay transparency laws apply to remote jobs?

Often yes. Colorado covers remote roles that could be performed from the state, Vermont and Massachusetts tie coverage to a Vermont or Massachusetts connection, and Delaware covers remote jobs offered by Delaware employers. Several states are unclear. The safe approach for a job open to applicants in covered states is to post a good-faith range on the listing.

What counts as a good-faith salary range?

A good-faith range is the minimum and maximum you genuinely expect to pay for the job when you post it. California says the range you reasonably expect to pay upon hire. Minnesota bars open-ended ranges, and Massachusetts defines it as the range you reasonably and in good faith expect to pay. Do not stretch it to attract applicants.

Can I ask applicants for their salary history?

In many states you cannot. California, Colorado, Illinois, New York, Massachusetts, Washington and others restrict salary history questions, and Virginia added its ban on July 1, 2026. Where it is allowed, such as Texas, a pay range on your posting still lets you base pay on the job and the person’s skills instead of past pay.

What happens if my job posting leaves out the pay range?

It depends on the state. Penalties run from a written warning in Maryland and Delaware to $100 to $10,000 per violation in California and $500 to $10,000 in Colorado. Washington and Virginia allow applicants to sue, and several states give a short cure window if you fix the posting after written notice.

Does the law apply to small businesses?

Many laws have size thresholds: 4 employees in New York, 5 in Vermont, 10 in New Jersey and Maine, 15 in California, Washington and Illinois, 25 in Massachusetts, 30 in Minnesota and 50 in Hawaii. Colorado, Maryland, Connecticut and Virginia have no minimum size. Count employees the way each statute does, and check whether out-of-state staff are included.
