How many hours is part time? Federal, ACA and employer answers

There is no single legal number. Here is what the Department of Labor, the IRS, the Bureau of Labor Statistics and most employers mean by part time, and what changes at 20, 25, 29, 30 and 32 hours a week.

Quick answer

  • How many hours is part time under federal wage law? There is no number. The Department of Labor says the FLSA leaves full-time and part-time status to the employer, and minimum wage and overtime rules apply either way.
  • For health coverage, the IRS counts 30 hours a week (130 a month) as full time under the Affordable Care Act, so under 30 is part time for that purpose. The Bureau of Labor Statistics uses under 35 hours in its data.
  • Most employers set part time at 20 to 29 hours a week in their handbook, which keeps hourly staff under the 30-hour ACA line while still giving them a predictable schedule.

How many hours is part time? Ask the Department of Labor and the answer is “whatever the employer says.” Ask the IRS and the answer is anything under 30 hours a week. Ask the Bureau of Labor Statistics and it is anything under 35. Ask a restaurant or retail manager and you will usually hear 20 to 29. All four are correct, because each one is answering a different question: pay, health coverage, statistics or scheduling. This guide walks through each definition, what changes at every hour mark, and how to write a part-time policy that holds up.

How many hours is part time a week? The three official answers

Start with the law that governs pay. The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) does not define full-time or part-time employment. The Department of Labor’s Wage and Hour Division states that this is “a matter generally to be determined by the employer,” and that the label does not change how the FLSA applies. A part-timer gets the same minimum wage ($7.25 federally, higher in most states) and the same overtime protection as anyone else.

Next, health coverage. The Affordable Care Act’s employer shared responsibility rules, administered by the IRS, define a full-time employee as one with at least 30 hours of service a week on average, or 130 hours in a calendar month. That line only matters to applicable large employers (ALEs), meaning employers with 50 or more full-time employees including full-time equivalents in the prior year. For them, anyone under 30 hours is part time and does not have to be offered coverage.

Third, statistics. The Bureau of Labor Statistics counts people who usually work 35 or more hours a week as full-time and fewer than 35 as part-time in the Current Population Survey. BLS says plainly that these categories are for statistical purposes only, so they do not bind any employer.

The sibling question, how many hours is full time, has the same shape: 30 for the ACA, 35 for BLS, and 32 to 40 by company policy.

Two numbers on this table surprise most managers. First, the 401(k) long-term part-time rule kicks in at 500 hours a year, which is under 10 hours a week, so nearly every part-timer qualifies after two years. Second, the FMLA line of 1,250 hours is only 24 hours a week, so a 25-hour employee can qualify for job-protected leave. Our FMLA leave guide covers the other tests.

What changes at 30 hours: the ACA employer mandate

The 30-hour line matters only if you are an applicable large employer. Here is how the IRS has you count. For each month of the prior year, add your full-time employees (30+ hours a week or 130+ hours that month) to your full-time equivalents. FTEs are the total hours of all part-time employees that month, counting no more than 120 hours per person, divided by 120. Average the 12 monthly totals. If the result is 50 or more, you are an ALE for the current year.

An illustrative example: a restaurant group has 38 full-time employees and 30 part-timers who each work 60 hours a month. The part-timers contribute 30 × 60 = 1,800 hours ÷ 120 = 15 FTEs. The group’s count is 38 + 15 = 53, so it is an ALE even though only 38 people work full time. The IRS has a seasonal worker exception: if your count exceeds 50 for 120 days or fewer in the year and the people pushing you over are seasonal workers, you are not an ALE.

Once you are an ALE, each full-time employee must be offered affordable minimum essential coverage, and you must offer it to at least 95% of full-time employees to avoid the broader penalty. The IRS indexes the payments every year. Under Rev. Proc. 2025-26, the 2026 amounts are $3,340 per full-time employee (minus the first 30) if you fail the 95% offer test and at least one full-time employee receives a premium tax credit, and $5,010 for each full-time employee who receives a credit when you did offer coverage but it was unaffordable or inadequate. Check irs.gov for the 2027 figures before budgeting.

Two methods decide who is full time. The monthly measurement method checks each calendar month for 130 hours. The look-back measurement method averages hours over a measurement period of 3 to 12 months and locks the status in for a following stability period, which is what most employers with variable-hour staff use so that one busy month does not trigger an offer. Either way, the count is hours of service, which includes paid time off and paid holidays, not just hours worked. Jordan Lee at Northfield Coffee, scheduled 28 hours a week, averages 121 hours a month. Two 5-hour pickup shifts in the same month put him at 131, a full-time month under the monthly method.

If you have fewer than 50 FTEs, none of this applies. You choose your own benefits line, subject to your insurance carrier’s eligibility rules, which often require 30 hours but sometimes allow 20 or 25.

Overtime still starts at 40, whatever the label

Part-time status never reduces overtime rights. Under the FLSA, a non-exempt employee earns 1.5 times their regular rate for every hour worked over 40 in a fixed seven-day workweek. Almost all hourly part-timers are non-exempt, because the exemptions require a salary of at least $684 a week under federal rules plus a duties test, and part-time pay rarely reaches that. Our exempt vs non-exempt guide explains the tests.

Here is how it plays out. Chris Walker is a barista at Northfield Coffee in Austin, Texas, earning $17.75 an hour and scheduled for 24 hours in the Sunday to Saturday workweek of November 1 to 7, 2026. Two coworkers call out and he picks up 19 more hours, finishing at 43 hours.

  • Regular pay: 40 × $17.75 = $710.00
  • Overtime rate: $17.75 × 1.5 = $26.625
  • Overtime pay: 3 × $26.625 = $79.88
  • Week total: $789.88 before taxes

The fact that Chris is “part time” in the handbook changes nothing. Three points follow from this:

  • No federal daily overtime. A part-timer who works one 12-hour shift and nothing else that week earns straight time federally, though California, Alaska, Colorado and Nevada add daily rules. See how to calculate overtime for state detail.
  • No averaging across weeks. 22 hours one week and 44 the next is 4 hours of overtime, even though the biweekly total is 66.
  • Records are required for everyone. The FLSA’s recordkeeping rules do not have a part-time exception: keep payroll records for 3 years and time cards for 2. A time clock app that records actual punches is the simplest way to do that for a team whose hours change every week.

Benefits, PTO and sick leave for part-time employees

Federal law does not require any private employer to offer vacation, PTO, paid holidays or health coverage to part-time employees (or full-time ones, apart from the ACA rules above). So most benefit questions come down to three things: your handbook, your insurance plan documents, and state or local leave laws.

PTO and holidays. You can exclude part-timers, include them at a lower rate, or pro-rate. The cleanest approach is an accrual rate per hour worked, because it scales automatically. A rate that gives a 2,080-hour full-timer 80 hours a year is 80 ÷ 2,080 = 0.0385 hours per hour worked. Maya Torres, who works 25 hours a week at the front counter, earns 1,300 × 80 ÷ 2,080 = 50 hours a year on the same rate. Our PTO guide covers accrual methods and state payout rules, and holiday pay rules covers pro-rated holidays, such as 4 hours for a 20-hour employee. Whatever you choose, apply it consistently; a benefit given to some part-timers and not others invites a discrimination claim.

State and city sick leave laws usually cover part-timers. This is where “employer choice” ends. California’s paid sick leave law covers anyone who works 30 or more days for the same employer in a year, including part-time and temporary staff, with accrual of at least 1 hour per 30 hours worked and a minimum of 40 hours or 5 days a year, per the Labor Commissioner’s Office. New York State’s law covers all private-sector employees regardless of part-time status, also at 1 hour per 30 hours worked, with 40 hours a year for employers of 5 to 99 and 56 hours for 100 or more. More than a dozen other states and many cities have similar laws, and almost all of them accrue by hours worked so part-timers earn in proportion. Check your state labor department before writing the sick leave section of your policy.

Health insurance. Below 50 FTEs you decide, within your carrier’s rules. At 50 or more, the 30-hour ACA line decides for you. Many employers that are not ALEs still offer coverage at 30 hours because that is where carriers commonly set eligibility.

FMLA. Job-protected unpaid leave does not depend on full-time status. The Department of Labor’s eligibility tests are 12 months of employment, 1,250 hours worked in the 12 months before leave, and a worksite with 50 or more employees within 75 miles. A 25-hour-a-week employee at a qualifying employer meets the hours test.

401(k) plans: the long-term part-time rule

If you sponsor a 401(k), you can no longer keep part-timers out indefinitely with a 1,000-hour eligibility rule. The SECURE Act created a “long-term, part-time employee” category, and the SECURE 2.0 Act shortened it. For plan years beginning on or after January 1, 2025, an employee who is at least 21 and has worked at least 500 hours in each of two consecutive 12-month periods must be allowed to make salary deferrals. Before 2025 the test was three consecutive years.

What the rule does and does not require:

  • Deferrals only. You must let the employee contribute from their own pay. You are not required to give them the employer match or non-elective contributions, though you may.
  • Low bar. 500 hours a year is about 9.6 hours a week. A 20-hour part-timer hits it in six months of each year.
  • Hours must be tracked. You need hours of service by 12-month period for every part-timer, including people you previously treated as ineligible. The IRS notes the rule does not apply to plans that use the elapsed-time method for eligibility.
  • Guidance is still settling. The IRS issued Notice 2024-73 for 403(b) plans and said the final 401(k) regulation will apply no earlier than plan years beginning on or after January 1, 2026. As of this writing we could not confirm that the final rule has been published, so ask your plan administrator what your plan document says.

Unemployment benefits and part-time work

Unemployment insurance is a state program, and the Department of Labor notes that each state sets its own eligibility rules within federal guidelines. Three questions come up with part-timers.

Can a laid-off part-timer collect? Usually yes, if their wages during the state’s base period (in most states the first four of the last five completed calendar quarters) meet the state minimum and they lost the job through no fault of their own. The weekly benefit is based on past earnings, so a part-timer’s benefit is smaller than a full-timer’s.

Can someone collect while working part time? Most states pay partial benefits when hours are cut or a claimant finds part-time work, reducing the weekly benefit by some portion of the earnings. The formulas differ a lot by state.

Can a claimant limit themselves to part-time work? This is where states split. Some require claimants to be available for full-time work; others let people with a history of part-time work seek part-time jobs. Point employees to your state workforce agency rather than guessing.

For employers, the practical point is that cutting a part-timer’s hours sharply, or ending a seasonal role, can generate a claim that counts against your experience rating. Document the business reason for schedule reductions.

Scheduling part-timers fairly and within the law

Federal law sets no minimum notice for schedules, no minimum shift length and no limit on how often hours change. A growing list of places does. Oregon’s predictive scheduling law, enforced by the Bureau of Labor and Industries, covers retail, hospitality and food service employers with 500 or more employees worldwide and requires a written schedule 14 days in advance, a good-faith estimate of hours at hire, predictability pay for late changes (one hour of pay for added time, half pay for hours cut), and 10 hours of rest between shifts unless the employee agrees otherwise. New York City’s Fair Workweek Law, enforced by the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection, requires fast food employers to post schedules 14 days ahead and pay premiums for changes, and retail employers to give 72 hours’ notice with no on-call shifts. Seattle, San Francisco, Chicago, Philadelphia and Los Angeles have their own versions. Check your city before assuming you are clear.

Even where no law applies, part-time schedules go wrong in predictable ways. A few rules keep them fair and keep you on the right side of the 30-hour line:

  • Give every part-timer a floor and a ceiling. “Regularly scheduled 16 to 28 hours a week” is a promise an employee can plan a life around. It also tells managers how much pickup work is safe.
  • Watch monthly hours, not just weekly. The ACA test is 130 hours in a calendar month. A 28-hour employee who grabs two extra shifts crosses it. If you are an ALE using the monthly method, that is an offer of coverage or a potential payment.
  • Publish early and change rarely. Two weeks of notice is the legal standard in the strictest cities and good practice everywhere. When you must change a shift, ask for volunteers before reassigning.
  • Count hours of service, not just punches. Paid sick leave and PTO count toward the ACA’s 130 hours but not toward FLSA overtime. Your system should total each separately.
  • Review status on a schedule. Once a quarter, compare each part-timer’s actual average with their policy range. Someone averaging 33 hours for six months is full time in practice, and courts and agencies look at practice.

Sample part-time policy wording

Put the definition in writing so managers, payroll and employees use the same one. Replace the bracketed fields, delete the ACA paragraph if you have fewer than 50 full-time equivalent employees, and have an employment attorney review it against your state and city rules.

Part-time employment policy
EMPLOYMENT STATUS: PART-TIME EMPLOYEES [Company name] Effective [Month day, year] 1. DEFINITION A part-time employee is an employee who is regularly scheduled to work fewer than [30] hours per workweek. Part-time employees at [Company name] are normally scheduled between [16] and [28] hours per week. A full-time employee is regularly scheduled to work [30] or more hours per workweek. Your status is stated in your offer letter and in [the employee app / your personnel file]. 2. PAY, OVERTIME AND TIMEKEEPING Part-time employees are paid [hourly] at the rate stated in their offer letter. Non-exempt part-time employees receive overtime pay of 1.5 times their regular rate for all hours worked over 40 in the workweek, which runs from [Sunday 12:00 a.m.] to [Saturday 11:59 p.m.], [and daily overtime where state law requires it]. All employees must record their actual start, break and end times in [the time clock app]. Work performed off the clock is not permitted; if it happens, report it so it can be paid. 3. SCHEDULES Schedules are published at least [14] days before the first shift they cover in [the employee app]. Managers may offer additional shifts; accepting them is voluntary unless stated otherwise at the time of the offer. Part-time employees should not accept additional hours that would bring them to [30] or more hours in a week without manager approval, because sustained hours at that level may change your status. [Where a state or local fair workweek law applies, its notice and premium pay rules control.] 4. BENEFITS Part-time employees are eligible for the following benefits on a pro-rated basis: [PTO at the same per-hour accrual rate as full-time employees / paid holidays at X hours per holiday / 401(k) participation as described in the plan document]. Part-time employees [are / are not] eligible for group health, dental and vision coverage[, except as required by the Affordable Care Act as described below]. Benefits required by law, including paid sick leave under [state / city] law, are provided to all eligible employees regardless of status. 5. HEALTH COVERAGE AND HOURS OF SERVICE [ALEs only] [Company name] measures hours of service for all variable-hour and part-time employees using the [monthly / look-back] measurement method. Any employee who averages 30 or more hours of service per week (130 hours per month) during the applicable [month / measurement period] will be offered group health coverage for the following [month / stability period] as required by the Affordable Care Act. Hours of service include paid time off, paid holidays and paid sick leave. 6. STATUS REVIEW AND CHANGES [Company name] reviews each part-time employee’s average scheduled and worked hours at least [quarterly]. An employee who has averaged [30] or more hours per week for [six] consecutive months will be reclassified as full-time effective [the first of the following month], with benefits eligibility changing at that time. [Company name] may change an employee’s status or scheduled hours for business reasons with [two weeks’] written notice, except where law requires more. 7. QUESTIONS Contact [HR contact name] at [email or phone] with questions about your status, hours or benefits eligibility. This policy is general information for [Company name] employees and does not create a contract of employment. Employment remains at will. Where this policy conflicts with federal, state or local law, the law controls.

Pair this with your attendance policy so the rules on call-outs and pickup shifts match, and store the signed acknowledgment with the employee’s other HR documents.

Frequently asked questions

Is 30 hours a week part time or full time?

It depends on who is asking. For the Affordable Care Act employer mandate, 30 hours a week (130 hours a month) is full time, so an employer with 50 or more full-time equivalent employees must offer that person health coverage. The Bureau of Labor Statistics still counts 30 hours as part time, because its full-time line is 35. Many employers call 30 hours full time for benefits.

Is 32 hours a week considered part time?

Under the ACA, no: 32 hours a week is above the 30-hour full-time line, so a large employer must treat that employee as full time for health coverage. In BLS statistics, 32 hours is still part time because full time starts at 35. Your employer’s handbook decides the label for PTO and other benefits, and many treat 32 hours as full time.

Do part-time employees get overtime?

Yes, if they are non-exempt, which almost all hourly part-timers are. The Fair Labor Standards Act requires time and a half for hours worked over 40 in a workweek regardless of whether the employee is labeled part time. A part-timer scheduled for 24 hours who picks up extra shifts and works 43 hours is owed 3 hours of overtime.

Can an employer change you from full time to part time?

In most states, yes. Employment is at will unless a contract or union agreement says otherwise, so an employer can reduce hours for business reasons. The change cannot be discrimination or retaliation, and some cities and Oregon require predictability pay or notice for schedule changes. A large cut in hours may also let the employee claim partial unemployment benefits.

How many hours is part time for health insurance?

For the ACA employer mandate, anyone averaging under 30 hours a week, or under 130 hours in a calendar month, is part time, and an applicable large employer does not have to offer them coverage. Employers with fewer than 50 full-time equivalent employees are not subject to the mandate at all, so they decide their own eligibility line, subject to their insurance carrier’s rules.

Do part-time employees have to be offered a 401(k)?

If the employer has a 401(k) plan, generally yes after two years. Under the SECURE 2.0 Act, for plan years beginning in 2025 or later, an employee aged 21 or older who works at least 500 hours in each of two consecutive 12-month periods must be allowed to make salary deferrals. Employer matching contributions are not required for these long-term part-time employees.
