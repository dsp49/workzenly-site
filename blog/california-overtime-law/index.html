California overtime law: daily overtime, double time, 7th day

California pays overtime by the day as well as by the week. Here are the rules for 2026, how they stack, and the exact math for common schedules.

Quick answer

  • Time and a half after 8 hours in a workday or 40 in a workweek, and for the first 8 hours on the 7th consecutive day worked.
  • Double time after 12 hours in a workday and after 8 hours on the 7th consecutive day.
  • Each hour gets one premium. Hours already paid as daily overtime do not count again toward the weekly 40.

California overtime law is stricter than the federal rule. The federal Fair Labor Standards Act only looks at hours over 40 in a workweek. California also counts every workday on its own, so a non-exempt employee who works a 10-hour shift earns 2 hours of overtime that day, even if the week ends at 30 hours. The rules come from California Labor Code section 510 and the Industrial Welfare Commission (IWC) wage orders, and they are enforced by the Labor Commissioner’s Office, also called the Division of Labor Standards Enforcement (DLSE). This guide reflects those rules as of November 4, 2026.

California overtime rules at a glance

For most non-exempt employees aged 18 and over, the Labor Commissioner’s Office lists four triggers. When more than one applies to the same hour, the employee gets the higher rate, not both.

Up to 8 in a workday and up to 40 in a workweekRegular rate
Over 8 and up to 12 in a workday1.5× regular rate
Over 40 in a workweek (straight-time hours only)1.5× regular rate
Over 12 in a workday2× regular rate
First 8 on the 7th consecutive day of work in a workweek1.5× regular rate

A few definitions decide most questions:

  • Workday is any consecutive 24-hour period that starts at the same time each day. The employer sets it, for example midnight to midnight. A shift that crosses the boundary is split between two workdays.
  • Workweek is a fixed, regularly recurring period of seven consecutive workdays, such as Sunday through Saturday. Each week stands alone.
  • The seventh-day rule applies only when the employee works all seven days of the same workweek. Working Thursday through the following Wednesday crosses two workweeks and does not trigger it. The California Supreme Court confirmed this reading in Mendoza v. Nordstrom (2017). Labor Code section 556 also exempts weeks where total hours are 30 or fewer and no day is over 6 hours.
  • Hours worked means time actually worked. Paid holidays, vacation and sick time do not count toward daily or weekly overtime. Our guide to holiday pay rules explains how employers treat those hours.

How California daily overtime and weekly overtime work together

The most common payroll mistake in California is double counting. Under a January 19, 2000 DLSE opinion letter on overtime pyramiding, hours already paid at an overtime rate because of the daily rule are not counted again when you check the 40-hour weekly limit. The wage orders say the same thing for alternative workweeks: no hours paid at 1.5× or 2× are included in determining when 40 hours have been worked.

The practical method is a four-step pass through the week:

  1. For each workday, split the hours into regular (the first 8), time and a half (over 8 up to 12) and double time (over 12).
  2. Apply the seventh-day rule if the employee worked all seven days: the first 8 hours that day are time and a half and the rest are double time.
  3. Add up only the regular hours, in date order. Once that running total passes 40, every further regular hour becomes time and a half.
  4. Pay each hour once, at the single highest rate it earned.

If you skip step 3 and simply subtract 40 from total hours, then add daily overtime on top, you pay some hours twice. That overpays the employee, which is not illegal, but it inflates labor costs and makes pay stubs hard to explain. Paying less than the correct amount is the expensive error, so most employers calculate it hour by hour.

California overtime examples with exact math

Each example uses a workweek of Monday to Sunday and gross pay before taxes. The last column shows what federal law alone would require, so you can see where California adds pay.

Example 1: three 12-hour shifts. An employee earns $20 an hour and works 12 hours on Monday, Wednesday and Friday, 36 hours in total. Each day has 8 regular hours and 4 at time and a half. Regular: 24 × $20 = $480. Overtime: 12 × $30 = $360. Total: $840.00. Federal law owes no overtime here: 36 × $20 = $720.

Example 2: one long day. At $20 an hour, the employee works 14 hours on Monday and 8 hours Tuesday through Friday, 46 hours in total. Monday splits into 8 regular, 4 at time and a half and 2 at double time. Regular hours for the week are 8 + 32 = 40, so there is no extra weekly overtime. Pay: 40 × $20 = $800, plus 4 × $30 = $120, plus 2 × $40 = $80. Total: $1,000.00.

Example 3: the seventh consecutive day. At $20 an hour, the employee works 8 hours Monday through Saturday and 10 hours on Sunday, 58 hours in total. Monday to Friday is 40 regular hours. Saturday has no daily overtime, but the regular-hour total is already 40, so all 8 Saturday hours are weekly overtime at $30. Sunday is the seventh consecutive day: 8 hours at $30 and 2 hours at $40. Pay: $800 + 16 × $30 ($480) + 2 × $40 ($80) = $1,360.00.

Example 4: daily and weekly overtime in the same week. At $22 an hour (overtime $33), the employee works 10 hours Monday through Thursday, 8 hours Friday and 6 hours Saturday, 54 hours in total. Daily overtime is 2 hours on each of the first four days, 8 hours. Regular hours are 32 Monday to Thursday plus 8 Friday, which reaches 40, so all 6 Saturday hours are weekly overtime. Overtime hours: 8 + 6 = 14. Pay: 40 × $22 = $880, plus 14 × $33 = $462. Total: $1,342.00. Counting 14 weekly hours (54 − 40) plus 8 daily hours would wrongly pay 22 overtime hours.

The regular rate: what overtime is multiplied by

Overtime is 1.5× or 2× the regular rate of pay, not just the base wage. The Labor Commissioner’s Office says the regular rate includes hourly pay, salary, piece rates, commissions, shift differentials and non-discretionary bonuses. It excludes gifts, expense reimbursements, discretionary bonuses and pay for time not worked, such as vacation, holidays and sick days. The regular rate can never be lower than the applicable minimum wage, which is $16.90 statewide in 2026; see our California minimum wage guide for city rates.

Shift differential example. A night worker earns $20 an hour plus a $2 differential for every hour and works five 9-hour shifts. The regular rate is $22, so the overtime rate is $33, not $30. Pay: 40 × $22 = $880, plus 5 × $33 = $165, for $1,045.00. Our guide to shift differential pay covers mixed shifts.

Two rates in one week. When an employee works at two hourly rates, the DLSE uses a weighted average. Its own example: 32 hours at $11 and 10 hours at $9 is $442 of straight-time pay, divided by 42 hours, for a regular rate of $10.52. Those rates are below today’s minimum wage, but the method is the same.

Salaried non-exempt employees. The wage orders set the hourly rate at 1/40 of the weekly salary. A $4,000 monthly salary is $48,000 a year, or $923.08 a week. Divide by 40 for $23.08 an hour, so overtime is $34.62 an hour.

Flat-sum bonuses. California handles a flat attendance or shift bonus differently from federal law. In Alvarado v. Dart Container (2018), the California Supreme Court divided the bonus by non-overtime hours only and applied a 1.5× multiplier. If you pay flat bonuses, check this math with a payroll professional.

Alternative workweek schedules

An alternative workweek schedule (AWS) is the only way to schedule regular days longer than 8 hours without daily overtime. Most wage orders allow a regularly scheduled AWS of up to 10 hours a day within a 40-hour week, such as four 10-hour days or a 9/80 work schedule. The rules are strict:

  • Secret ballot. At least two-thirds of the affected employees in a work unit must approve it in a secret ballot election, held during working hours at the work site, before any work under it begins.
  • Written disclosure. The employer must explain the effect on wages, hours and benefits in writing and hold a meeting at least 14 days before the vote, in another language too if at least 5% of affected employees primarily speak it.
  • Reporting. Election results must be reported to the state’s Division of Labor Statistics and Research.
  • Overtime still applies. Hours beyond the agreed schedule up to 12 are time and a half, hours over 12 are double time, and every shift must be at least 4 hours.
  • No pay cuts. An employer cannot reduce the hourly rate because an AWS is adopted or repealed. One-third of affected employees can petition for a new vote to repeal it.

A manager and an employee cannot agree to a four-tens schedule on their own. Without a valid election, the 2 extra hours each day are daily overtime.

Exemptions and the 2026 exempt salary threshold

California overtime law does not cover exempt employees, but exemption is harder to meet than under federal law. For the executive, administrative and professional exemptions, an employee must pass both tests:

  1. Salary test. A monthly salary of at least two times the state minimum wage for full-time work. With the 2026 minimum wage of $16.90, the California Department of Industrial Relations sets this at $16.90 × 2 × 40 × 52 = $70,304 a year, or $5,858.67 a month. On January 1, 2027, the minimum wage rises to $17.40, and the threshold rises to $72,384 a year ($6,032 a month).
  2. Duties test. The employee must be primarily engaged, meaning more than half of their work time, in exempt duties, and regularly use discretion and independent judgment.

Only the state minimum wage sets the salary floor, not city minimum wages. Separate thresholds apply to some groups, such as computer software professionals and licensed physicians, and the department publishes them each year. Job titles do not decide exemption. A salaried “assistant manager” who mostly runs a register is usually non-exempt and owed daily overtime. Our exempt vs non-exempt guide compares the federal and state tests.

Industry-specific California overtime rules

California has 17 IWC wage orders, and the one that covers your business can change how California overtime law applies to you. The most common differences:

  • Agriculture (Wage Order 14). AB 1066 phased in overtime for farmworkers. Employers with 26 or more employees reached the standard 8 hours a day and 40 a week on January 1, 2022. Employers with 25 or fewer reached it on January 1, 2025. Today all agricultural employers pay time and a half after 8 hours in a day or 40 in a week and double time after 12 hours in a day, plus the seventh-day rule.
  • Hospitals and residential care (Wage Order 5). Under the 8/80 system, a hospital or residential care facility may use a 14-day work period instead of a 7-day week, by agreement with the employee before the work. Overtime is then owed for hours over 8 in a day and over 80 in the 14-day period. The DLSE has said the arrangement must be voluntary and cannot be a condition of employment.
  • Healthcare 12-hour shifts. Healthcare employers may adopt an AWS with regular days of up to 12 hours. Hours over 12 in a day are double time and hours over 40 in a week are time and a half. Staffing these shifts is easier with healthcare scheduling software that flags overtime before it happens.
  • Residential childcare. Wage Order 5 sets weekly overtime after 40 hours, double time after 48 hours in a week and after 16 hours in a day for employees with direct responsibility for children in 24-hour residential care.

Other groups, including some truck drivers, live-in domestic workers and employees under qualifying union contracts, have their own rules. Check the wage order posted at your workplace.

Meal and rest breaks and premium pay

Breaks are not overtime, but they are paid out alongside it and appear on the same pay stub.

  • Meal periods. An unpaid, duty-free 30-minute meal period must start no later than the end of the fifth hour of work. It can be waived by mutual consent if the workday is 6 hours or less. A second 30-minute meal is required for workdays over 10 hours, and it can be waived only if the day is 12 hours or less and the first meal was not waived.
  • Rest periods. A paid, net 10-minute rest break for every 4 hours worked or major fraction of 4, which the Labor Commissioner’s Office treats as more than 2 hours. No rest break is required if the workday is under 3.5 hours.
  • Premium pay. For each workday a required meal period is not provided, the employer owes one extra hour of pay at the regular rate. The same applies for missed rest breaks, capped at one hour per day for rest breaks no matter how many were missed. Premium hours are not hours worked, so they do not count toward overtime.

Example: an employee with a $22 regular rate works a 12-hour shift with no second meal break, no signed waiver and only two rest breaks. They are owed 4 hours of daily overtime plus a $22 meal premium. The 12-hour shift needed three rest breaks, so they are also owed a $22 rest premium.

Penalties for California overtime violations

Underpaid overtime is a wage claim that the employee can bring to the Labor Commissioner’s Office or to court, for the unpaid amount plus interest. Several penalties can stack on top:

  • Waiting time penalties. Final wages are due immediately at a discharge, and within 72 hours when an employee quits without notice. If an employer willfully fails to pay all wages owed, including unpaid overtime, the penalty is the employee’s daily rate of pay for each day late, up to 30 days. Inability to pay is not a defense.
  • Wage statement penalties. Pay stubs must show the hours worked at each rate. Missing or wrong rate lines can lead to separate penalties.
  • Civil penalties and PAGA. The Labor Commissioner can assess civil penalties, and employees can bring claims on behalf of the state under the Private Attorneys General Act.

Because these add up across every employee and pay period, a small calculation error can turn into a large claim.

What California employers should do

  • Fix the workday and workweek in writing. Pick a start time for each and keep it consistent.
  • Record real punches. Use a time clock app that captures start, meal and end times, so daily overtime and meal premiums come from records, not estimates.
  • Calculate hour by hour. Split each day, apply the seventh-day rule, then count only regular hours toward 40.
  • Build the regular rate correctly. Include differentials, commissions and non-discretionary bonuses.
  • Watch long shifts in the schedule. A schedule that shows projected hours per day, like Workzenly’s shift scheduling, makes 12-hour days and seventh days visible before they are worked.
  • Pay unapproved overtime. Discipline for policy breaks if needed, but always pay for hours worked.
  • Review exempt salaries every January. The threshold rises with the state minimum wage, so plan for $72,384 on January 1, 2027.
  • Run any AWS by the book. Keep election records and the disclosure, and confirm the results were reported.

Frequently asked questions

Is California overtime daily or weekly?

Both. California requires time and a half for hours over 8 in a workday and over 40 in a workweek, and double time for hours over 12 in a workday. An hour already paid as daily overtime is not counted again toward the 40, so each hour gets one premium, the higher one that applies.

When do you get double time in California?

Non-exempt employees earn double time for every hour over 12 in a single workday, and for every hour over 8 on the seventh consecutive day worked in a workweek. Double time is two times the regular rate of pay, which includes shift differentials and non-discretionary bonuses, not just the base hourly wage.

Can a California employee waive overtime pay?

No. An individual agreement to work overtime at straight time is not valid, even if the employee signs it. The only legal way to schedule longer days without daily overtime is a properly adopted alternative workweek schedule, approved by a two-thirds secret ballot vote of the affected work unit.

What is the California exempt salary for 2026?

For 2026, most exempt employees in California must earn at least $70,304 a year, or $5,858.67 a month. That is two times the $16.90 state minimum wage for full-time work. On January 1, 2027, it rises to $72,384 a year as the minimum wage goes to $17.40.

Does overtime have to be approved in advance to be paid?

No. If a non-exempt employee works the hours and the employer knew or should have known, the overtime must be paid at the correct rate. An employer can set an approval policy and discipline employees who break it, but it cannot withhold overtime pay for hours actually worked.
