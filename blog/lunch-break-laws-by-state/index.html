Quick answer

  • Lunch break laws by state start from a federal floor of nothing: federal law does not require meal or rest breaks. When an employer offers short breaks of about 5 to 20 minutes, they are paid. A bona fide meal period of 30 minutes or more, with the employee fully relieved of duty, can be unpaid.
  • About 20 states require a meal break for adult private-sector workers, most often 30 minutes once a shift passes 5 or 6 hours. Seven states (California, Colorado, Kentucky, Minnesota, Nevada, Oregon and Washington) also require paid rest breaks, usually 10 minutes per 4 hours.
  • Missed breaks cost money: one extra hour of pay per day in California, the break time plus an equal amount in Minnesota, and back wages anywhere an unpaid meal was really worked.

Lunch break laws by state are a patchwork. The U.S. Department of Labor says federal law does not require lunch or coffee breaks, so in Texas, Florida, Georgia and about 30 other states an adult can legally work an 8-hour shift with no meal break. Cross into California, Washington or New York and the same shift must include a 30-minute meal at a set point, and in some states paid rest breaks too. This guide lists the meal break laws and rest break laws for every state that has them, explains what has to be paid, and shows how to record breaks on a time clock so the records hold up. The tables draw on the Department of Labor’s state tables (last revised January 1, 2023) and the state agency pages for the larger states; see the notes where a state has changed its law since.

What federal law says about breaks

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) has no break requirement for adults. What it does regulate is pay. The Department of Labor’s rules in 29 CFR Part 785 set two tests that apply in every state, including the states with their own break laws:

  • Short breaks are paid. Under 29 CFR 785.18, rest periods of about 5 to 20 minutes “must be counted as hours worked.” A 10-minute coffee break or a 15-minute smoke break stays on the clock and counts toward the 40 hours that trigger overtime. You cannot offset them against other paid time.
  • Bona fide meal periods are not work time. Under 29 CFR 785.19, a meal period of 30 minutes or more is normally long enough to be unpaid, and a shorter one “may be long enough under special conditions.” The condition is that the employee is “completely relieved from duty.” Someone who eats at the register and rings up a customer, or answers emails at their desk over lunch, is working and must be paid for the whole period. The employee does not have to be allowed to leave the premises.

So the federal question is never “did the employee get a break” but “was the break paid correctly.” If you deduct 30 minutes for lunch and the employee kept working, you owe back wages, and if that pushes the week past 40 hours, overtime as well. The same logic sits behind the overtime calculation in every state.

Exempt salaried employees are outside these pay rules because they are paid the same whether they take lunch or not. Our exempt vs non-exempt guide covers who qualifies. State break laws, however, often cover hourly and non-exempt staff only, so check each table’s notes.

Meal break laws by state

States not listed, such as Texas, Florida, Georgia, Arizona, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Michigan and New Jersey, have no meal break law for adults, though several have rules for minors (covered below). A few patterns help. Most thresholds sit at 5 or 6 hours, so a 4-hour shift rarely triggers a meal break anywhere. Most states want the meal somewhere in the middle, not tacked on to the start or end of the day. And several states (Washington, New Hampshire, Colorado, Minnesota) say the meal becomes paid time when the employee cannot leave their post, which mirrors the federal rule.

Rest break laws by state

Rest breaks are rarer. The Department of Labor’s rest period table (also last revised January 1, 2023) lists seven states with a paid rest break for most adult private-sector workers, plus two narrow rules. The state agencies in California, Oregon and Washington confirm the figures below; Minnesota’s comes from the current statute.

In California, Jordan is owed a 30-minute unpaid meal that starts before noon (the end of the fifth hour) and two paid 10-minute rest breaks, because 9 hours is two 4-hour blocks plus a 1-hour remainder that is not a major fraction. Paid vs unpaid breaks, on-duty meals and waivers

Four rules decide whether break time goes on the paycheck.

  1. Anything 20 minutes or shorter is paid. That is federal law, so it holds in all 50 states. Illinois’ 20-minute meal and West Virginia’s 20-minute meal are therefore paid time in practice, and the Minnesota statute says the same of any break under 20 minutes.
  2. A 30-minute meal is unpaid only if it is duty-free. The employee must be relieved of all work. A manager who tells a cook to “keep an eye on the line” during lunch has turned it into paid time. Massachusetts goes further and requires that the employee be free to leave the workplace.
  3. On-duty meal periods must be paid and, in some states, agreed in writing. California allows an on-duty meal only when the nature of the work prevents relief (its example is a lone worker at a coffee kiosk) and only under a written agreement the employee can revoke at any time. Oregon lets an employer that cannot provide a 30-minute meal give a paid eating period with notice to the state. Washington and New Hampshire simply make the on-duty meal paid.
  4. Waivers are a state-by-state matter. California permits a mutual waiver of the first meal when the day is 6 hours or less and of the second when the day is 12 hours or less. Oregon and Tennessee allow written waivers for certain tipped servers. Rest breaks generally cannot be waived. Some states let the employee choose to skip the meal but not the rest break; confirm your state’s waiver rules with its labor agency before you rely on one, and keep every waiver signed and dated.

Unpaid meal time also changes the overtime math. A 10-hour day with a 30-minute unpaid lunch is 9.5 hours worked. Five such days are 47.5 hours, not 50, so the week carries 7.5 overtime hours. If the lunches were actually worked, the week is 50 hours and 10 overtime hours. Our time card calculator subtracts unpaid lunch from each day and shows the difference.

Break laws for minors

Many states that require nothing for adults do require meal breaks for workers under 18. The Department of Labor’s note to its meal table says 35 jurisdictions have separate meal period rules for minors, and where both an adult and a minor rule apply, the stricter one wins. Examples we checked against the state statute:

  • Florida (Fla. Stat. 450.081): minors 15 and younger cannot work more than 4 hours continuously without a 30-minute meal period. For 16- and 17-year-olds the same 4-hour rule applies on days they work 8 or more hours. A break shorter than 30 minutes does not count. Florida has no break rule for adults.
  • New Jersey (N.J.S.A. 34:2-21.4): minors under 18 cannot work more than 6 hours continuously without a 30-minute lunch. New Jersey has no adult break rule.
  • Oregon: minors get 15-minute paid rest breaks instead of 10, and 14- and 15-year-olds always get the meal period; the adult exceptions apply only to 16- and 17-year-olds.
  • Michigan, Ohio, Pennsylvania and Louisiana each publish a 30-minute break rule for minors who work 5 or more consecutive hours, with no equivalent for adults. Confirm the exact ages and wording on each state’s labor department site before scheduling.

Minor break rules sit alongside hour limits for school days and nights, so if you hire teenagers for the summer, build the break into the schedule and record it. State inspectors usually ask for the time records first.

Lactation breaks under the PUMP Act

One break is required by federal law for most workers: time to express breast milk. The PUMP for Nursing Mothers Act, signed December 29, 2022, extended the FLSA’s existing protection to nearly all employees, including salaried exempt staff, teachers, nurses and farm workers. According to the Department of Labor’s Fact Sheet #73:

  • Employers must give reasonable break time each time the employee needs to pump, for one year after the child’s birth. There is no cap on the number of breaks.
  • The space must be somewhere other than a bathroom, shielded from view and free from intrusion by coworkers and the public. A temporary space qualifies if it meets those tests. Employees working from home may not be observed on a webcam while pumping.
  • Pump breaks can be unpaid if the employee is completely relieved of duty. If the employee pumps during a paid break, it is paid like any other break. If the employee works while pumping, the time is paid. Exempt salaried employees must receive their full salary either way.
  • Employers with fewer than 50 employees (counted company-wide) are exempt only if compliance would cause undue hardship, judged against the employer’s size, resources and structure. Airline crewmembers are exempt; some rail and motorcoach workers have special rules.
  • Since April 28, 2023, employees can recover lost wages, an equal amount in liquidated damages and other remedies for violations, through a Wage and Hour Division complaint or a lawsuit.

Many states add longer protections, such as paid pump breaks or coverage beyond one year. Our maternity leave guide covers the related leave rules.

How to track breaks on a time clock

Every break law ends at the same place: the time record. If a wage claim lands, the employer has to show when each meal started and ended and that rest breaks were paid. The FLSA requires time cards to be kept at least 2 years and payroll records at least 3. Here is what works, and what gets employers into trouble.

The auto-deduct problem

Many systems subtract 30 minutes a day automatically for lunch. That is legal only if the employee really took a duty-free 30 minutes every day. The moment a cook eats in six minutes and goes back on the line, the deduction has clipped 24 minutes of paid work. Multiply by 250 shifts a year and a dozen employees and you have the shape of a class action. If you keep an auto-deduction, pair it with a simple way for employees to cancel it for a day and a daily attestation (“I took my full meal break today: yes/no”). Better still, drop it. The same caution applies to rounding punches: the 7-minute rule lets you round to the nearest quarter hour only if the rounding is neutral over time, and rounding a 30-minute meal down to 25 is not.

Punch meals, keep rest breaks on the clock

  • Meal breaks are their own punch. The employee taps “Start meal” and “End meal.” The system deducts the actual minutes, so a 22-minute lunch is deducted as 22 minutes, and it is flagged as short in states where 30 minutes is required.
  • Rest breaks stay paid. Record them as a break type that does not deduct from hours worked. In California, Oregon and Washington the record also proves the break happened.
  • Flag timing, not just length. California’s meal must start before the end of the fifth hour; Washington’s between the second and fifth. A clock that warns the manager at hour 4.5 prevents the premium.
  • Use a shared kiosk where phones are banned. A tablet time clock kiosk by the kitchen door lets staff start a meal in two taps without a personal phone.
  • Review the week before payroll. Approve timesheets with missed-break flags visible, and pay any premiums in the same period. Late premiums are a common second violation.

If you are still on paper, the timesheet templates include a lunch column, and the time card calculator totals the week. A time and attendance system removes the arithmetic and keeps the records for you.

Penalties and premium pay

What a missed break costs depends on the state, but the floor is the same everywhere: if an unpaid meal was actually worked, the employer owes the wages, any overtime those minutes created, and under the FLSA potentially an equal amount in liquidated damages, going back 2 years (3 for willful violations). On top of that:

  • California: one additional hour of pay at the regular rate for each workday a meal period is not provided, and one more hour for each workday a rest period is not provided, so up to 2 premium hours a day. Premium hours are not hours worked for overtime. Claims reach back 3 years. For Jordan at $18.25, one missed meal a week for a year is about $949 in premiums, before the back wages for any minutes worked.
  • Minnesota: under the current statutes, an employer that fails to provide a required rest or meal break owes the employee the missed break time at the regular rate plus an equal additional amount.
  • Colorado: the COMPS Order treats a missed 10-minute rest break as 10 minutes of unpaid work, so the time must be paid; check the current COMPS Order on the Colorado Department of Labor and Employment site for the exact remedy language.
  • Oregon and Washington: workers can file wage claims with BOLI or L&I for unpaid break time, and the agencies can add penalty wages and civil penalties; Oregon hospital workers have a $200 statutory penalty per missed break under ORS 653.258.
  • Other states: most impose civil fines per violation enforced by the state labor department, plus any unpaid wages. Delaware, for example, lists a fine of up to $1,000 for retaliating against an employee who complains.

The practical defense is the same in every state: a written policy, a schedule that fits the breaks in, a time clock that records them, and a manager who reviews the exceptions before payroll closes.

Frequently asked questions

Are employers required to give breaks?

Not under federal law. The Fair Labor Standards Act has no meal or rest break requirement, but it does say that short breaks of about 5 to 20 minutes must be paid when offered. About 20 states require a meal break and 7 require paid rest breaks, so the answer depends on where the employee works.

Does federal law require a lunch break for an 8-hour shift?

No. The Department of Labor says plainly that federal law does not require lunch or coffee breaks. An 8-hour shift with no lunch is legal under federal law. State law may require one: in California the meal must start before the end of the fifth hour, in Washington between the second and fifth hour, in Nevada after 8 continuous hours.

Can my employer make me work through lunch without pay?

No. A meal period can be unpaid only when you are completely relieved of duty. If you answer phones, watch the counter or keep working at your desk while eating, the time is hours worked under 29 CFR 785.19 and must be paid. In states with meal break laws, a missed or interrupted meal can also trigger premium pay.

Which states require paid rest breaks?

Seven states require paid rest breaks for most adult private-sector employees: California, Colorado, Kentucky, Minnesota, Nevada, Oregon and Washington. Most require 10 paid minutes for every 4 hours worked; Minnesota requires at least 15 minutes. Illinois requires paid rest breaks only for hotel room attendants, and Vermont requires reasonable opportunities to use the restroom.

Can an employee waive a meal break?

Only where state law allows it. California lets employer and employee agree to waive the first meal when the day is 6 hours or less, and the second when the day is 12 hours or less. Some states allow waivers for tipped servers or by written agreement. Rest breaks generally cannot be waived. Put every waiver in writing and keep it.

Is a 15-minute break paid?

Yes, in every state. Under 29 CFR 785.18, rest periods of about 5 to 20 minutes count as hours worked and must be paid. Employees should stay clocked in for a 15-minute break, and it counts toward the 40 hours that trigger federal overtime. Only a bona fide meal period of 30 minutes or more with no duties can be unpaid.
