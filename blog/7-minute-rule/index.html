Quick answer

  • The 7-minute rule is quarter-hour rounding: punches 1 to 7 minutes past the quarter hour round back, 8 to 14 minutes round forward. 9:07 becomes 9:00; 9:08 becomes 9:15.
  • Federal rule 29 CFR 785.48(b) accepts rounding to the nearest 5 minutes, tenth of an hour or quarter hour only if, over time, employees are paid for all time worked. Rounding that always favors the employer is not allowed.
  • If your time clock records the exact minute, paying the exact minute is the simplest and safest choice. Rounding never adds pay you can count on, but it can add liability.

The 7 minute rule is the everyday name for quarter-hour time clock rounding. Under it, a clock-in or clock-out is moved to the nearest 15-minute mark: 1 to 7 minutes past the quarter hour round down to it, and 8 to 14 minutes round up to the next one. The rule comes from the way the US Department of Labor reads 29 CFR 785.48(b), which accepts rounding to the nearest 5 minutes, tenth of an hour or quarter hour as long as, over time, the practice does not fail to pay employees for all the time they actually worked. This guide shows exactly how each minute rounds, what rounding does to a real paycheck, where employers get it wrong, and why many now skip rounding and pay exact minutes instead.

What the 7-minute rule is

Time clocks used to print the time on a card, and someone in payroll added the week by hand. Adding 7:53, 8:11 and 7:59 is slow, so employers rounded each punch to a quarter hour and worked in .25, .50 and .75 of an hour. The Department of Labor accepted the habit decades ago, and the regulation that recognizes it is still on the books.

The regulation, 29 CFR 785.48(b), says that in some industries, particularly where time clocks are used, there has long been a practice of recording starting and stopping times to the “nearest 5 minutes, or to the nearest one-tenth or quarter of an hour.” It presumes the arrangement averages out so employees are fully paid, and says the Wage and Hour Division will accept it for enforcement purposes, provided that it will not result, over a period of time, in failing to pay employees properly for all the time they have actually worked.

The 7-minute split is simply arithmetic. A quarter hour is 15 minutes, so the halfway point is 7.5 minutes. Any punch up to 7 minutes past a quarter-hour mark is closer to that mark and rounds back; any punch 8 minutes or more past it is closer to the next mark and rounds forward. The Department of Labor’s Fact Sheet #53 states it the same way: 1 to 7 minutes may be rounded down and not counted, but 8 to 14 minutes must be rounded up and counted as a quarter hour of work time. The fact sheet adds the warning that matters most: an employer may violate the FLSA if it always rounds down.

Three things follow from the rule, and most mistakes come from forgetting one of them.

  • It rounds both ways. An employee who clocks in at 8:53 is credited from 9:00 and loses 7 minutes; an employee who clocks in at 9:07 is also credited from 9:00 and gains 7 minutes. Over many punches the gains and losses are supposed to cancel out.
  • It applies to the punch, not the total. You round each clock-in and clock-out, then total the rounded times. A few systems round the daily total instead; the federal rule talks about starting and stopping times, and that is the safer reading.
  • It is permission, not a requirement. Nothing obliges you to round. Paying the exact minute always satisfies the federal rule.

One more point of confusion: a 7-minute grace period in an attendance policy is a different thing. A grace period decides whether a late arrival counts as a tardy for discipline. Rounding decides what time payroll uses. Our tardiness policy guide keeps the two apart.

Worked examples with the 7-minute rule

These examples use staff at Northfield Coffee, a fictional coffee shop in Austin, Texas. All figures are illustrative gross pay before taxes.

Single punches

  • Sam Rivera clocks in at 9:14 a.m. 14 minutes past 9:00 is in the 8-to-14 range, so the punch rounds forward to 9:15. Sam is paid from 9:15 and loses 1 minute of worked time: $0.30 at $18.00 an hour.
  • Maya Torres clocks in at 8:58 a.m. 58 minutes past 8:00 is 13 minutes past the 8:45 mark, so it rounds forward to 9:00. Maya loses 2 minutes, $0.58 at $17.50 an hour. If she had been doing nothing but waiting for her 9:00 shift, those minutes were not working time anyway (29 CFR 785.48(a) says employees who clock in early and do no work need not be paid for that time). If she started steaming milk at 8:58, they were.
  • A clock-out at 5:07 p.m. 7 minutes past 5:00 rounds back to 5:00. The employee loses 7 minutes. Had they clocked out at 5:08, the punch would have rounded to 5:15 and they would have gained 7.

The Department of Labor’s health care example

Fact Sheet #53, written for hospitals and nursing homes, shows that rounding can also cost the employer. Its example is an employee scheduled 7:00 a.m. to 3:30 p.m. with a 30-minute unpaid lunch who clocks in 10 minutes early and clocks out 7 minutes late every day. Under the 7-minute rule the 6:50 a.m. punch rounds back to 6:45 and the 3:37 p.m. punch rounds back to 3:30. That is 8 hours 45 minutes less lunch, or 8.25 hours a day, 41.25 hours for the week, and the fact sheet says the employee is owed 1.25 hours of overtime. An employer who assumed the schedule was 40 hours and paid 40 would be short, and our overtime calculator shows what that 1.25 hours costs at time and a half.

When time clock rounding becomes a problem

The federal rule has one condition: over time, employees must be paid for all the time they actually worked. Every rounding dispute comes down to a practice that failed that test. These are the patterns that get employers sued or investigated.

Rounding that always goes the employer’s way

The clearest violation is a system that rounds late clock-ins forward but leaves early clock-ins alone, or rounds early clock-outs back but credits late clock-outs at the scheduled time. Each punch that moves only moves against the employee, so the losses never average out. Fact Sheet #53 says plainly that an employer may violate the FLSA if it always rounds down. The federal courts have measured neutrality in the aggregate: the Wage and Hour Division’s 2026 opinion letter summarizes a Ninth Circuit case where rounding was upheld because one employee came out a net 3 minutes and $15 behind over about a year, an Eighth Circuit case that went the other way where the practice cost roughly 13,000 employees about 74,000 hours over six years, and a Tenth Circuit case where rounding favored the employer 94 percent of the time.

Rounding early clock-ins to the scheduled start when people are working

Opinion letter FLSA2026-8, dated May 28, 2026, looked at a public hospital that let employees clock in up to 7 minutes early and rounded those punches forward to the scheduled start, so a 6:53 a.m. clock-in was recorded as 7:00. Employees said they started chart review and handoff reports as soon as they clocked in. The Wage and Hour Division said that if employees are in fact doing compensable work in that window, the practice is neither facially neutral nor neutrally applied, because it only ever benefits the employer. It added that if employees who clock in up to 7 minutes late were also credited from their scheduled start, and that averaged out over time, the policy would likely comply. The same letter warns employers to expect “exacting scrutiny” of claims that a few regular minutes of work are too small to pay, because modern timekeeping records the exact time.

Rounding meal breaks

Rounding a 28-minute lunch to 30 minutes looks harmless until a state law requires a full 30-minute break. In Donohue v. AMN Services (2021), the California Supreme Court held that employers may not round meal-period punches, and that time records showing a short or late meal period create a presumption that the break was not provided. Outside California, rounding breaks still risks turning a paid short break into an unpaid one. Record meal punches exactly and leave them out of any rounding, as the week above does.

Rounding when the system already records the exact minute

Federal rules do not forbid this, but it is the weakest position to defend. If your app records 9:14 and you pay from 9:15, you have to show the system averaged out, and the records that prove or disprove it are your own. Some California courts have gone further, which the next section covers. The Wage and Hour Division made the same point in its 2026 letter, quoting an appeals court: with electronic timekeeping, checking whether rounding underpaid people is easy, because the system records the exact time of every punch.

Rounding the grace period into pay

A policy that says “you may clock in up to 7 minutes late without a tardy” and then also rounds every late punch forward has combined an attendance rule with a pay rule, and the pay rule only runs one way. Keep them separate: discipline follows the grace period, pay follows the punch or a neutral rounding rule.

State rules on time clock rounding

Most states follow the federal standard: neutral rounding is accepted, rounding that shortchanges employees is not. Two states need their own notes.

California. The 2012 Court of Appeal decision in See’s Candy Shops v. Superior Court accepted neutral rounding, and California employers relied on it for a decade. Donohue (2021) then barred rounding of meal periods. In Camp v. Home Depot (2022), a Court of Appeal held that an employer that records exact minutes and can pay them must pay them, and in Woodworth v. Loma Linda University Medical Center (2023) another Court of Appeal followed Camp. The California Supreme Court granted review of Camp on February 1, 2023 (case S277518), which means the decision is not binding while the court considers it. As of November 2026 the court had not published its ruling; check its docket or ask an employment attorney before relying on rounding in California. Many California employers already pay exact minutes to avoid the question. Our California overtime law guide covers the state’s other wage rules.

Oregon. The Bureau of Labor and Industries (BOLI) says Oregon requires employees to be paid for all hours worked and notes a November 29, 2022 federal district court ruling that Oregon’s rule on tracking and rounding hours differs from federal law. BOLI’s guidance is that employers may apply a rounding system to clock-in and clock-out times but must take precautions so employees are paid for all work time each pay period, for example by adjusting hours at the end of each pay period or by rounding only in the employee’s favor. In practice that means neutral rounding alone is not enough in Oregon.

Other states have paid sick leave, meal break and recordkeeping rules that interact with rounding, and city ordinances add more. Before you turn rounding on anywhere, check your state labor department’s guidance and write down what you find.

Why many employers stop rounding and pay exact minutes

Rounding solved a problem that no longer exists. The arithmetic that was tedious with punch cards is instant when a time clock app records punches and a timesheet totals them. Once the math is free, rounding has no upside, only costs:

  • It does not save money. A neutral system averages out by definition. If it saves you money, it is not neutral, and that saving is back pay waiting to be claimed.
  • It creates a burden of proof. With exact minutes, the records speak for themselves. With rounding, you may have to analyze years of punches to show the practice averaged out.
  • It confuses employees. A barista who clocks out at 5:07 and sees 5:00 on the timesheet assumes she was shorted, even if last Tuesday went her way. Exact minutes end that conversation.
  • It collides with state law. Meal-period rules in California and the pay-period checks Oregon expects both get easier when nothing is rounded.
  • It muddles overtime. The Fact Sheet #53 example shows rounding pushing a 40-hour schedule to 41.25 hours. Exact minutes make the overtime line obvious.

If you decide to keep rounding, keep it defensible: round every punch, in both directions, with the same increment for clock-ins and clock-outs; never round meal punches; run a quarterly check comparing rounded hours to exact hours for the whole team; and write the rule into the handbook so employees can see it. For what a compliant record looks like, read what a timesheet is, and remember that the FLSA requires employers to keep payroll records for at least three years and time cards for at least two.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 7-minute rule for time clocks?

The 7-minute rule is the common name for quarter-hour time clock rounding. Each punch is rounded to the nearest 15 minutes: a punch 1 to 7 minutes past the quarter hour rounds back to it, and a punch 8 to 14 minutes past rounds forward to the next one. So 9:07 becomes 9:00 and 9:08 becomes 9:15.

Is the 7-minute rule a federal law?

Not exactly. No law requires rounding. The federal regulation 29 CFR 785.48(b) accepts rounding to the nearest 5 minutes, tenth of an hour or quarter hour, as long as over time employees are paid for all time worked. The 7-minute split is simply what nearest-quarter-hour rounding works out to, and the Department of Labor explains it in Fact Sheet #53.

Can my employer round my clock-in time up but never round it down?

No. Rounding is accepted under federal rules only if it is neutral: it must round in both directions and average out so you are paid for all the time you actually work. A system that rounds late arrivals forward but never rounds early starts back always favors the employer, and the Department of Labor says that practice can violate the FLSA.

Does the 7-minute rule apply to lunch breaks?

Be careful here. Federal rules do not single out meal punches, but rounding a 28-minute break to 30 minutes can turn a paid break into an unpaid one. In California, the state Supreme Court held in Donohue v. AMN Services (2021) that employers may not round meal-period punches at all. Most payroll teams record breaks to the exact minute.

Can an employer round time if the clock records the exact minute?

Under federal rules, yes, if the rounding is neutral and averages out. California is the open question: a 2022 Court of Appeal decision, Camp v. Home Depot, said an employer that records exact minutes must pay them, and the California Supreme Court agreed to review that decision in 2023. Until it rules, many California employers pay exact minutes.

Is it better to pay exact minutes or to round?

If your time clock records exact minutes, paying them is simpler and safer. It removes the need to prove your rounding averaged out, avoids state-law disputes and costs almost nothing in extra arithmetic because software does the math. Rounding made sense when hours were added by hand from punch cards; most apps now total exact minutes automatically.
