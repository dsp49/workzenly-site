Restaurant scheduling software replaces the whiteboard, the spreadsheet and the group text with one schedule your whole team sees on their phones. For a restaurant, that is harder than it sounds. A Friday dinner needs a different mix of servers, bartenders, hosts, line cooks and dishwashers than a Tuesday lunch, half the team asks to swap something every week, and a few wrong shifts can cost you a tip credit, a premium payment or an overtime hour you did not plan for. This guide covers what the software has to do, the rules your schedule runs into, a buying checklist, and how the main kinds of tools compare.

What restaurant schedule software has to handle

Generic shift tools assume one kind of worker and one shift a day. Restaurants rarely work that way. Before you look at any product, list the patterns your week actually contains.

  • Front and back of house roles. Servers, bartenders, hosts, bussers and runners on the floor; line cooks, prep cooks and dishwashers in the kitchen. Many people work more than one role, and each role may carry a different pay rate.
  • Stations and sections. Server scheduling is often by section or station, not just by hours. A good tool lets you name the position on the shift so the manager knows who has the patio.
  • Split shifts. Lunch, a few hours off, then dinner. Common and useful, but in some states a long split day changes the pay (see New York's spread of hours below).
  • Opening and closing. Openers who receive deliveries and set up, closers who break down the line and count the drawer. Closing one night and opening the next morning is a clopening, which some cities restrict.
  • Labor cost against sales. Restaurants judge a schedule by labor as a share of sales. You want to see the scheduled wage cost of each day before you publish, so you can trim a slow Monday rather than find out on payday.

Here is the arithmetic, using illustrative numbers. Northfield Coffee in Austin expects about $3,600 in sales on a Saturday. The draft schedule has Maya Torres for 8 hours at $17.50 ($140.00), Jordan Lee for 8 hours at $18.25 ($146.00), Sam Rivera for 8 hours at $18.00 ($144.00), Chris Walker for 6 hours at $17.75 ($106.50) and Emily Carter for 8 hours at $19.00 ($152.00). That is 38 hours and $688.50 in wages, or about 19.1% of expected sales, before payroll taxes. Software that shows this number while you drag shifts around saves the back-of-napkin math. Hours also feed overtime: one extra closing shift can push someone past 40 hours, so the tool should warn you before it happens.

Tipped employees, the tip credit and dual jobs

If you pay servers or bartenders a lower cash wage and count part of their tips toward minimum wage, your schedule affects that tip credit. Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), the Department of Labor says an employer can pay a tipped employee a cash wage of at least $2.13 an hour and claim a tip credit of up to $5.12, as long as tips bring every hour up to $7.25. That is the rule in Texas, which follows the federal tipped wage; our Texas minimum wage guide covers the tip notice, tip pools and a worked example. Many states set a higher cash wage or ban the tip credit, so check yours.

The scheduling question is what happens when a tipped worker does other work. The federal "dual jobs" regulation (29 CFR 531.56(e)) separates two situations:

  • Related duties inside the tipped job. The regulation's own examples include a server who spends part of the time cleaning and setting tables, toasting bread, making coffee and occasionally washing dishes or glasses. The tip credit can still apply.
  • A separate, non-tipped job. If the same person also works as, say, a dishwasher or maintenance worker, no tip credit can be taken for the hours in that job. Those hours need at least the full minimum wage.

The rules here have moved. In 2021 the Department of Labor issued a rule limiting related, non-tip-producing work to 20% of the workweek and 30 continuous minutes, often called the 80/20/30 rule. In 2024 the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals vacated that rule in Restaurant Law Center v. U.S. Department of Labor, and the department restored the original 1967 regulation. On October 19, 2026, the department's Wage and Hour Division issued Field Assistance Bulletin 2026-1, which says any guidance setting specific time limits on duties within a tipped occupation no longer reflects its policy. The bulletin also says the department is preparing a proposed rule on tipped employees, so expect more change.

For scheduling, the practical habits are the same. If someone works a server shift on Friday and a dishwashing shift on Monday, schedule them as two roles so the hours are paid at the right rate. State law can be stricter than federal law: New York, for example, denies the tip credit on any day a tipped worker spends more than two hours, or more than 20% of the shift, on non-tipped work, as our New York minimum wage guide explains.

Minors, fair workweek and break rules on the schedule

Restaurants employ many first-job workers and operate in cities with their own scheduling laws. These are the rules most likely to show up on a restaurant schedule.

Teen workers. The Department of Labor's child labor rules for restaurants limit 14- and 15-year-olds to 3 hours on a school day, 18 hours in a school week, 8 hours on a non-school day and 40 hours in a non-school week, between 7 a.m. and 7 p.m. (9 p.m. from June 1 through Labor Day). They may not run fryers, broilers or other high-heat cooking equipment, or operate or clean power-driven food slicers, and no one under 18 may operate a power-driven meat slicer. Federal law sets no hour limits for 16- and 17-year-olds, but many states do. Your software should store birth dates and flag a 15-year-old on a 9 p.m. close.

New York City fast food. Under the city's Fair Workweek Law, chain fast food restaurants that are part of 30 or more locations nationally, including franchises, must give workers a regular schedule and post each work schedule 14 days ahead. The city's Department of Consumer and Worker Protection lists premiums of $10 to $75 for each change made with less than 14 days' notice, depending on the notice and whether hours are cut. A clopening with less than 11 hours between shifts needs the worker's written consent and a $100 premium, and new shifts must be offered to current staff before hiring. Our scheduling page summarizes similar laws in Oregon, Seattle, Chicago, Philadelphia and other cities.

New York spread of hours and call-in pay. If a New York restaurant employee's workday runs more than 10 hours from start to finish, breaks included, they get one extra hour of pay at the basic minimum wage. A split shift from 11 a.m. to 2 p.m. and 5 p.m. to 10 p.m. has an 11-hour spread, so it triggers the extra hour even though only 8 hours were worked. Restaurants must also pay call-in pay when they send someone home early: at least 3 hours for one shift, or the scheduled shift if shorter.

California meal and rest breaks. A 30-minute meal period must start by the end of the fifth hour of work, a second one is due on days over 10 hours, and employees get a paid 10-minute rest break for every 4 hours worked or major fraction. A missed meal or rest break costs an extra hour of pay at the regular rate. A schedule that books a server from 11 a.m. to 5 p.m. with no planned meal is a problem before the shift starts. Our California overtime law guide covers the daily overtime that comes with long shifts, and the time card calculator helps you check break-adjusted hours.

Shift swaps and last-minute call-outs

The schedule you publish on Monday is rarely the one that runs on Saturday. A restaurant employee scheduling app earns its keep in the middle of the week:

  • Swaps with a record. Staff offer a shift, a teammate takes it, and a manager approves. You want to see who asked, who accepted and when. Where fair workweek laws apply, a change the employee asked for is often treated differently from a change you made, so the record matters.
  • Open shifts. Post an uncovered Friday double and let people who want more hours claim it, instead of calling down a list. In New York City fast food, offering hours to current staff first is required.
  • Call-outs. When a line cook texts at 3:40 p.m. for a 4:00 p.m. shift, the manager needs to see who is qualified, who is not already near 40 hours, and who is not on approved time off. A clear call-out rule helps too; our guide to no call, no show policies has wording you can adapt.
  • Sending people home. Cutting a server early on a slow night is normal, but in New York it triggers call-in pay. As the New York guide shows, a Long Island server sent home after 1 hour of a regular 5-hour shift gets $11.35 for the hour worked plus 2 × $17.00 = $34.00, or $45.35.

If you are not ready for software, a printable layout from our work schedule template page is a fair start for a small team. The weak point is everything after you print it: swaps, call-outs and hours still live in texts and memory.

Buying checklist for restaurant scheduling software

Use this list when you trial any tool, including ours. Ask each vendor to build one of your real weeks in the demo, with your actual roles and your busiest night.

  1. Roles, positions and pay rates. Can one person hold several roles at different rates, such as server and host, or prep cook and dishwasher?
  2. Split shifts and doubles. Can you book lunch and dinner on the same day without workarounds, and does the tool flag a long spread of hours?
  3. Labor cost before you publish. Does it show scheduled hours and wage cost by day, and can you compare it with your sales forecast?
  4. Overtime warnings. Does it warn you when a shift will push someone past 40 hours, or past 8 in a day in California?
  5. Minor rules. Does it hold birth dates and flag shifts that break teen hour limits?
  6. Swaps, open shifts and approvals. Do changes leave a record with names and times?
  7. Time off in view. Do approved PTO and availability show where you build the schedule?
  8. Connection to the clock. Do punches line up against scheduled shifts, so late arrivals and missed breaks show up?
  9. Hours into payroll. Do worked hours reach payroll without exporting and re-keying them? If you track tips, how do tips reach payroll?
  10. Phones first. Will a 19-year-old busser actually open it? Can a shared tablet handle clocking in where phones stay in lockers?
  11. Several locations. If you run more than one restaurant, can staff work shifts at each and can managers see only their own location?

Frequently asked questions

What is the best restaurant scheduling software?

There is no single best tool for every restaurant. A restaurant-focused app may suit a busy full-service room that wants sales-based labor planning, while a connected system suits owners who want the schedule, time clock, PTO and payroll in one record. Trial two or three tools with one of your real weeks, and check each vendor's site for current features and pricing.

Can servers do side work without losing the tip credit?

Under federal law, yes, if the side work is related to the tipped job, such as setting tables or making coffee. In October 2026 the Department of Labor said time limits like the old 80/20 rule no longer reflect its policy. Work in a separate non-tipped job still needs the full minimum wage, and states such as New York set their own limits.

How far in advance do restaurants have to post schedules?

Federal law sets no notice period. New York City fast food employers must post schedules 14 days ahead, and Oregon, Seattle, Chicago and Philadelphia cover some larger restaurant employers with their own 14-day rules. Most independent restaurants are not covered, but posting two weeks ahead is still a good habit that makes swaps and time off easier to plan.

Do I have to pay a server I send home early on a slow night?

Federal law only requires pay for hours worked. Some states add a minimum. In New York, a restaurant that sends a worker home early generally owes at least 3 hours of call-in pay for one shift, or the scheduled shift if shorter. California has its own reporting-time pay rule. Check your state labor department before cutting staff early.

How late can a 15-year-old work in a restaurant?

Under federal child labor rules, 14- and 15-year-olds may work only between 7 a.m. and 7 p.m., or until 9 p.m. from June 1 through Labor Day. They are limited to 3 hours on a school day and 18 hours in a school week. Many states add stricter rules, so check yours before you schedule a closing shift.
