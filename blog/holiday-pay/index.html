Holiday pay: what US law requires and how to pay it

Federal law does not require holiday pay, but the way you pay holidays still changes overtime, exempt salaries and state compliance. Here are the rules, the 2026 and 2027 dates and the math.

Quick answer

  • The FLSA does not require private employers to give paid holidays or to pay extra for working on a holiday. Holiday pay is a policy choice.
  • Paid holiday hours are not hours worked, so they do not count toward the 40-hour overtime threshold under federal law.
  • A holiday premium of at least 1.5× the regular rate can be left out of the regular rate and credited toward overtime owed (29 CFR 778.203 and 778.219).

Holiday pay is extra pay or paid time off that an employer gives for a holiday, either as pay for a day off (a paid holiday) or as a higher rate for working on the holiday (holiday premium pay). Under federal law, neither one is required for private employers. That surprises many employees and some owners, but the U.S. Department of Labor says plainly that the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) does not require payment for time not worked, such as vacations or holidays. What the law does control is how holiday pay interacts with overtime, salaried employees and a handful of state rules. This guide covers each one, with the math.

Is holiday pay required?

For most private employers in the United States, no. The Department of Labor describes paid holidays as a matter of agreement between the employer and the employee, or the employee’s representative such as a union. So the honest answer to “is holiday pay required” depends on three things:

  • Your own written policy or offer letter. If you promised eight hours of pay for Thanksgiving, you owe it. A handbook policy can become an enforceable promise under state wage laws.
  • A union contract or individual employment contract. Collective bargaining agreements often set holiday lists, eligibility and premium rates.
  • State law or a government contract. A few states regulate work on holidays, covered below. Federal service contractors covered by the McNamara-O’Hara Service Contract Act must provide the paid holidays listed in the applicable wage determination.

The same is true for working on a holiday. Federal law does not require time and a half on holidays. A non-exempt employee who works on July 4 is paid their normal rate for those hours, and overtime applies only if their hours worked in that workweek go over 40. If you want the full overtime rules first, read how to calculate overtime.

Private employers also choose which days count as holidays. The federal list below is a common starting point, but nothing requires you to close on Columbus Day or to observe a Saturday holiday on Friday.

Holiday premiums, the regular rate and overtime

The holiday pay rules that trip up payroll are about the regular rate. Overtime is owed at 1.5 times the regular rate, which is total pay for the workweek divided by total hours worked, minus payments the law lets you exclude. Holiday pay can be excluded in two situations.

Pay for a holiday not worked

When an employee gets paid for a holiday they take off, that payment is not compensation for hours worked. Under 29 CFR 778.218 and 778.219, it is left out of the regular rate, and the hours are not counted toward 40. The flip side is that this pay cannot be credited toward overtime the employee earns in the same week.

A premium for working on a holiday

29 CFR 778.203 covers premium pay for work on Saturdays, Sundays, holidays, regular days of rest or the sixth or seventh day of the workweek. If the premium rate is at least 1.5 times the employee’s bona fide rate for the same work on other days, the extra pay:

  • is excluded from the regular rate, so it does not raise the overtime rate for the rest of the week, and
  • can be credited toward the overtime the employer owes for that workweek.

If the holiday premium is less than 1.5 times the rate, such as a $2-an-hour holiday bonus or a 1.25× rate, the extra pay must be included in the regular rate and cannot be credited toward overtime. The regulation also says a “holiday” has its ordinary meaning, a day customarily observed for historical or religious reasons. A day off because work is slow does not qualify.

29 CFR 778.219 draws one more line. Some contracts pay a set amount of idle holiday pay whether or not the employee works, and then pay straight time on top for hours worked on the holiday. That idle pay is excluded from the regular rate but is not an overtime premium, so it cannot be credited against overtime. Premium pay tied directly to the hours worked on the holiday, at 1.5× or more, can be.

Holiday pay worked examples

All examples use an hourly employee earning $20.00 an hour, non-exempt, under federal rules only. Figures are gross pay before taxes.

Example 1: paid holiday off, short week

Thanksgiving is on Thursday, November 26, 2026. The employee takes it off with 8 hours of holiday pay and works 32 hours on the other days.

  1. Hours worked: 32 × $20.00 = $640.00.
  2. Holiday pay: 8 × $20.00 = $160.00.
  3. Total: $800.00. No overtime, because only 32 hours were worked.

If the same employee had instead worked 40 hours and also received 8 hours of holiday pay, they would be paid for 48 hours ($960.00), but still owe no federal overtime, since hours worked are exactly 40.

Example 2: time and a half on holidays, overtime week

The employer’s policy pays time and a half on holidays. The employee works 8 hours on Thanksgiving and 40 hours on other days, 48 hours in total.

  1. Holiday hours: 8 × $30.00 = $240.00.
  2. Other hours: 40 × $20.00 = $800.00.
  3. Policy pay: $1,040.00.
  4. FLSA minimum: 48 × $20.00 = $960.00, plus 8 overtime hours × $10.00 premium = $80.00, for $1,040.00.

Because the holiday premium is at least 1.5×, the $80.00 of extra holiday pay is credited toward the $80.00 of overtime owed. No more is due. If the premium could not be credited, the employer would owe the overtime on top, $1,120.00. Our overtime calculator handles the straight overtime part of this math.

Example 3: a holiday bonus below 1.5×

The policy pays a $2.00-an-hour holiday bonus. The employee works 8 hours on the holiday and 37 on other days, 45 hours in total.

  1. Straight-time pay: 45 × $20.00 = $900.00.
  2. Holiday bonus: 8 × $2.00 = $16.00. It is less than 1.5×, so it goes into the regular rate.
  3. Regular rate: ($900.00 + $16.00) ÷ 45 hours = $20.36 an hour (rounded).
  4. Overtime premium: 5 hours × $20.3556 × 0.5 = $50.89.
  5. Total: $900.00 + $16.00 + $50.89 = $966.89.

A small holiday bonus raises the overtime rate for the whole week. Night and weekend premiums work the same way, as our shift differential guide shows.

Federal holidays in 2026 and 2027

There are 11 federal holidays. The dates below are the observed dates for federal employees published by the U.S. Office of Personnel Management (OPM). When a holiday falls on a Saturday, federal employees observe it on the Friday before; when it falls on a Sunday, they observe it on the Monday after. Private employers can follow the same pattern or set their own.

Federal holidays set the schedule for federal employees. For a private business, they are only a reference list. You can pay all 11, pick the ones your team actually takes off, or pay a shorter list and add a floating holiday or two. Whatever you choose, publish it before the year starts so staff can plan around it.

Exempt employees and holiday weeks

Salaried exempt employees follow the salary basis rule in 29 CFR 541.602. An exempt employee must receive the full predetermined salary for any week in which they do any work, with a short list of permitted deductions. Two points matter during holiday weeks.

  • Closing for a holiday is not a permitted deduction. The regulation bars deductions for absences caused by the employer or its operating needs: if the employee is ready, willing and able to work, you cannot deduct for time when work is not available. Close the office on Friday, December 25, 2026, and the exempt employee still gets the full week’s salary.
  • Full days off for personal reasons can be deducted. If the employee takes an extra unpaid day next to the holiday for personal reasons, a full-day deduction is allowed. Partial days cannot be docked from salary.

Improper deductions can cost an employer the exemption, which turns a salaried employee into one owed overtime. Our exempt vs non-exempt guide explains the salary and duties tests. Requiring exempt staff to use accrued leave for a closure day is a separate question; the salary itself must stay whole, so check with counsel before you build that into a policy.

State holiday pay rules

No state requires private employers to give every employee paid holidays off. A few states regulate work on holidays. When state and federal law differ, follow the rule that is better for the employee.

  • Rhode Island. The Rhode Island Department of Labor and Training (DLT) says work performed on Sundays and state legal holidays must generally be paid at least 1.5 times the normal rate, and an employee cannot be fired or penalized for refusing to work on those days. The state’s holiday list includes New Year’s Day, Memorial Day, Juneteenth, Independence Day and Victory Day (the second Monday in August). There are exemptions, including certain health care, hospitality, agriculture and commercial fishing work and manufacturers operating seven days a week, and retail businesses follow separate rules on how premium hours count against overtime, clarified by DLT regulations in 2025. Check the DLT before scheduling holiday shifts.
  • Massachusetts. Retail premium pay for Sundays and holidays under the state’s “blue laws” was phased out and ended on January 1, 2023, according to the Massachusetts Attorney General’s Office. Retail employers must still pay 1.5 times the regular rate for hours over 40 in a week, including hours on a Sunday or holiday, and rules on voluntary Sunday and holiday work for retail staff remain. Confirm current details with the Attorney General’s Fair Labor Division.
  • Everywhere else. State labor departments generally say holiday pay is set by the employer’s policy. But once you promise it, many state wage payment laws let employees recover promised pay. Daily overtime states such as California still apply daily overtime to holiday hours, as covered in our California overtime law guide.

Common employer holiday policies

Because the law leaves most of this open, your holiday pay rules are what your policy says. These are the choices to make in writing:

  • Which holidays are paid. List them by name and say how a Saturday or Sunday holiday is observed.
  • Who is eligible. Full-time only, part-time pro rata (for example, 4 hours for a 20-hour employee), or everyone after a waiting period.
  • How much. Usually the employee’s scheduled hours for that day at their base rate.
  • Attendance conditions. Many policies require working the scheduled shift before and after the holiday to be paid for it, with exceptions for approved PTO.
  • Holiday during PTO. Say whether a holiday that falls during approved vacation is paid as holiday pay rather than drawn from the PTO balance. See our paid time off guide for policy wording.
  • Working the holiday. The premium rate (1.5× is common), or a substitute day off, and how holiday shifts are assigned or volunteered.
  • Floating holidays. One or more days employees choose, often used for religious or cultural observances. Decide whether they expire at year end and whether unused days are paid out, because some states treat promised paid time like earned wages.

Put all of this in your handbook; our employee handbook template has a section for it.

What employers should do

  1. Publish the 2027 holiday list now. Name each paid holiday and the observed date, especially July 5 and December 24, 2027.
  2. Set holiday premiums at 1.5× or more if you pay one. That keeps the premium out of the regular rate and creditable toward overtime.
  3. Keep holiday pay and hours worked separate in payroll, so paid holiday hours do not trigger overtime by mistake and worked holiday hours do.
  4. Pay exempt staff full salary in weeks you close for a holiday.
  5. Check state rules if you have employees in Rhode Island, Massachusetts retail or a daily overtime state.
  6. Plan holiday coverage early. Post holiday shifts and let staff pick them up with shift scheduling, and track floating holidays with PTO tracking so balances are right.

Frequently asked questions

Is holiday pay required by law?

Not under federal law. The Fair Labor Standards Act does not require private employers to pay for holidays off or to pay a premium for working on a holiday. Holiday pay is set by company policy, an employment contract or a union agreement. A few states, such as Rhode Island, add their own rules for work on certain holidays.

Do I get time and a half for working on a holiday?

Only if your employer's policy, your contract or a state law says so. Federal law treats a holiday like any other day: you get your regular rate, plus overtime at 1.5 times your regular rate if your hours worked that workweek go over 40. Many employers choose to pay time and a half on holidays anyway.

Do paid holidays count toward overtime?

No. Under federal law, overtime is based on hours actually worked. Eight hours of paid holiday time is not hours worked, so it does not push an employee past 40 in the workweek. An employer can choose to count holiday hours toward overtime by policy, which is allowed because it pays more than the law requires.

Can I deduct a holiday from an exempt employee's salary?

No, if you close for the holiday and the employee works any part of that week. Federal rules say an exempt employee who is ready, willing and able to work must get the full weekly salary when the employer closes or has no work. Docking the salary for a holiday closure can put the employee's exempt status at risk.

What is a floating holiday?

A floating holiday is a paid day off the employee picks, instead of a fixed date the whole company takes. Employers use floating holidays so staff can observe religious or cultural days, birthdays or state holidays the company does not close for. Your policy should say how many there are, whether they expire and whether unused days are paid out.
