Quick answer

  • How does maternity leave work? There is no federal paid maternity leave for private-sector workers. The FMLA gives eligible employees at employers with 50 or more staff up to 12 weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave with health coverage continued.
  • Thirteen states and Washington, DC pay family leave benefits in 2026, usually 8 to 12 weeks of bonding leave at 60% to 90% of wages, up to a weekly cap. Five states also pay short-term disability for the weeks around the birth.
  • Everything else comes from your employer: parental leave policy, short-term disability insurance, PTO and sick time. Ask early, in writing, and confirm how the pieces run together.

How does maternity leave work in the US? In most of the country it is a patchwork rather than a single benefit. One federal law protects your job but does not pay you. A growing list of states pays part of your wages for a set number of weeks. Your employer's own policy fills whatever is left. This guide walks through each layer using the U.S. Department of Labor, the EEOC and the state program sites, with figures current as of November 12, 2026, then shows how an employee and a small employer put the pieces together.

How does maternity leave work in the US? The short version

Four federal laws shape maternity leave in the US. None of them requires a private employer to pay you while you are out.

  • Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA). Up to 12 workweeks of unpaid, job-protected leave in a 12-month period for the birth of a child and bonding, for prenatal care, or when pregnancy makes you unable to work. Your group health insurance continues. It applies to private employers with 50 or more employees, and to public agencies and schools of any size. Details in the next section.
  • Pregnancy Discrimination Act (PDA). Part of Title VII, enforced by the EEOC, covering employers with 15 or more employees. An employer cannot refuse to hire, fire, cut hours or deny benefits because of pregnancy, childbirth or related conditions. If it gives leave or light duty to employees with other temporary conditions, it must do the same for pregnancy.
  • Pregnant Workers Fairness Act (PWFA). In effect since June 27, 2023, with EEOC regulations in force since June 18, 2024. Employers with 15 or more employees must give reasonable accommodations for known limitations related to pregnancy, childbirth or related medical conditions unless that causes undue hardship. The EEOC's examples include extra or longer breaks, time off for health care appointments and telework. An employer cannot force you onto leave if another accommodation would work.
  • PUMP for Nursing Mothers Act. Signed December 29, 2022, enforced by the DOL's Wage and Hour Division. For one year after the birth, you get reasonable break time to express milk each time you need to, and a private space that is not a bathroom. Pumping breaks do not have to be paid unless you are not fully relieved from duty, or the employer pays for other breaks. Employers with fewer than 50 employees are exempt only if they can show undue hardship.

Federal government employees are the exception on pay. Under the Federal Employee Paid Leave Act, most federal workers get up to 12 weeks of paid parental leave for births or placements on or after October 1, 2020, used within 12 months of the event, per the Office of Personnel Management.

For everyone else, pay during maternity leave in the US comes from a state program (12 states and DC in 2026, listed below), from a state or private short-term disability policy, or from company benefits such as parental leave, paid time off and sick time. Most people end up combining all three.

FMLA maternity leave: who qualifies and what you get

FMLA maternity leave is the backbone of most leave plans because it is the only federal guarantee that your job will be there when you return. The DOL's Wage and Hour Division sets one employer test and three employee tests, all measured on the day leave begins:

TestWhat it meansWatch out for
Covered employerPrivate employer with 50 or more employees in 20 or more workweeks this year or last, or any public agency or public or private schoolPart-timers and people on leave count toward the 50

If you pass all of them, you get up to 12 workweeks of unpaid leave in a 12-month period for the birth of your child and bonding, which must finish within 12 months of the birth. You can also use FMLA before the birth for prenatal appointments or when pregnancy leaves you unable to work, and your spouse can take FMLA leave to care for you if you are incapacitated. Under 29 CFR 825.120, intermittent bonding leave (a few days a week, for example) is available only if the employer agrees, while leave for your own pregnancy-related condition can be intermittent when medically necessary.

Two protections come with it. Your employer must keep your group health coverage on the same terms as if you were working, and you keep paying your usual share. And when you return, you go back to the same job or a virtually identical one, with the same pay, benefits and shift. The leave itself is unpaid, but you may use your accrued PTO or sick time at the same time, and the employer may require you to. One cap to know: spouses who work for the same employer can be limited to a combined 12 weeks for birth and bonding, though not for the mother's own recovery.

Paid maternity leave by state: programs paying benefits in 2026

Paid maternity leave by state is where the real money is. Twelve states and DC run paid family leave programs that pay benefits in 2026, funded by payroll contributions. Three of them are new this year: Delaware and Minnesota began paying claims on January 1, 2026, and Maine on May 1, 2026. Each pays a share of your wages for bonding with a new child; most also pay separate medical leave for the birth parent's recovery, which is covered in the disability section below. A few things the table cannot show. Benefit caps change every year (several on January 1, Colorado and Maine in July, DC in October), so confirm the current cap on the program site before you budget. Most of these programs pay benefits but rely on another law for job protection: California PFL, for example, says plainly that it "provides benefit payments but not job protection," which comes from FMLA or the California Family Rights Act instead. Oregon (90 days), Minnesota (90 days), Maine (120 days) and Colorado (180 days) build job protection into the program after a set period of employment, and Massachusetts protects the job of anyone taking PFML; Washington's job protection mirrors the FMLA tests. And several programs let employers pass part of the cost to employees, so the line on a pay stub can read differently from one state to the next; our guide to reading a pay stub explains the common codes.

Maryland passed a paid leave law but has delayed it twice. According to the Maryland Department of Labor, payroll contributions begin January 1, 2027 at a total rate of 0.9%, and benefits become available January 1, 2028. Nothing is payable in 2026.

Outside these states, there is no public maternity pay. Your options are an employer policy, a private short-term disability plan, or saved PTO.

Short-term disability pay for the birth parent

Recovery from childbirth is treated as a temporary disability, which is why the weeks right around the birth are often paid by a disability program rather than family leave. Five states require disability coverage, and each treats pregnancy as a covered condition. Doctors typically certify about four weeks before the due date and six weeks after a vaginal birth or eight after a cesarean; the New Jersey and New York programs state those periods directly, and all of them pay longer with medical complications.

The New York figure stands out. The $170 DBL cap has not changed in decades, so New York birth parents usually move onto Paid Family Leave (67% of wages) as soon as their doctor releases them from disability. In California and New Jersey the disability and family leave benefits pay at the same rate, so the switch is about which program is paying, not how much.

In the other 45 states, disability pay comes only from a private policy. Many employers offer group short-term disability that replaces a share of pay for a set number of weeks after a waiting period, and some individual policies cover pregnancy if bought well before conception. Read the policy: the waiting period, the percentage, the weekly cap and whether the plan offsets state benefits all change the result.

How FMLA, state paid leave, disability and PTO stack

Ask an HR lead how does maternity leave work in practice and the honest answer is: on three clocks at once. The layers answer different questions, and a simple way to keep them straight is this:

  • Job protection comes from FMLA and state leave laws. They count weeks away from work, paid or not.
  • Pay comes from disability (for the birth parent's recovery), state family leave (for bonding) and employer PTO or parental leave. They count weeks of benefits.
  • Concurrency is the default. FMLA runs at the same time as state leave and paid benefits whenever they cover the same reason. Employers normally designate the leave as FMLA from day one so the clocks start together.

PTO is the piece people most often get wrong. Under the FMLA, you may use your paid leave during FMLA leave and your employer may require you to, which turns an unpaid week into a paid one without extending the leave. State programs differ: some let you top up a state benefit with PTO, others reduce the benefit or bar employers from forcing PTO use during state paid leave. Check the program's rules before you spend saved hours. Employer-paid parental leave usually runs on top of state benefits, either as a top-up to 100% of pay or as extra weeks after the state benefit ends.

Nothing requires Northfield to do this. Plenty of small employers offer it anyway because losing a trained barista costs more than covering eight weeks of shifts.

Paternity and parental leave for the other parent

Nearly every rule above is written for parents, not mothers. FMLA bonding leave belongs to fathers, adoptive parents and foster parents on the same terms, with the same 12 weeks, as long as they meet the eligibility tests. The only cap is the one for spouses sharing an employer. The DOL also allows FMLA leave to care for a pregnant spouse who is incapacitated, and after the birth if she has a serious health condition, so a father can take time during a difficult pregnancy or recovery as well as for bonding.

Every state paid family leave program in the table pays bonding benefits to either parent, and both parents can usually claim for the same child. What the other parent does not get is disability pay, since there is no medical recovery, so in New York, for example, a father's paid time is 12 weeks of PFL while the birth mother may have DBL plus PFL. Several state unpaid laws (Massachusetts, Minnesota, Vermont, New Jersey) also cover both parents. Employer parental leave policies vary most of all: some give both parents the same weeks, some give the non-birth parent fewer, and some distinguish a primary from a secondary caregiver. The PDA does not require equal leave for recovery from childbirth, but bonding leave offered only to mothers can be sex discrimination, so employers should write bonding policies to apply to all parents.

How to request maternity leave (with a sample email)

Timing matters. The FMLA requires 30 days' notice when the need is foreseeable, which a due date is, and most state programs and employer policies have their own notice rules. The practical order is:

  1. Read the handbook for the parental leave, PTO and short-term disability sections, and look for the FMLA notice employers must include.
  2. Map your layers. Which of FMLA, a state program, disability and PTO applies to you, and when each starts and stops.
  3. Tell your manager and HR in writing at least 30 days out, ideally around the end of the second trimester. Give the expected start date and return date, say that you are requesting FMLA (or state) leave, and ask what forms they need.
  4. Return the medical certification within 15 calendar days if your employer asks for one.
  5. File state claims yourself. Disability and family leave benefits are usually claimed by the employee directly with the state (California through SDI Online, New York through the employer's insurer, Maine through its administrator, and so on). Your employer confirms your wages but does not file for you.
  6. Agree the hand-off: who covers your work, how you will be contacted for genuine emergencies only, and how your premiums are paid while you are not receiving a paycheck.

If your company uses a time off request form or an app, submit through it as well so the dates are on record. The email below works for most situations; delete the bracketed parts that do not apply.

Sample maternity leave request email
Subject: Maternity leave request, [expected start date] to [expected return date] Hi [Manager name] and [HR contact name], I am expecting a baby, with a due date of [due date], and I am writing to request maternity leave. I am giving notice now so that we have more than 30 days to plan. My expected leave dates are [start date] through [return date], which is [number] weeks. I plan to work until [last day], unless my doctor advises otherwise. If the baby arrives early, I will let you know as soon as I can and we can adjust the dates. I am requesting that this leave be designated as [FMLA leave / leave under the (state) family leave law / parental leave under our policy]. [I also intend to apply for (state disability / paid family leave) benefits and will ask you to confirm my wages when the state requests it.] [I would like to use (number) hours of my accrued PTO during the (unpaid / waiting) period from (dates).] Please let me know which forms you need from me, including any medical certification, and how my share of health insurance premiums will be handled while I am on leave. Before I go, I will prepare a hand-off document covering [key duties] and I am glad to help train whoever covers my shifts. During leave I will check [work email / phone] for genuine emergencies only. Thank you. I am happy to meet this week to talk it through. [Your name] [Role, location] [Phone]

What employers must do

For an employer, maternity leave is a compliance checklist plus a staffing problem. The checklist first.

  • Post and publish the FMLA notice. Covered employers (50 or more employees) must display the DOL's FMLA poster even if no one is eligible yet, and put the general notice in the handbook or give it at hire. Our employee handbook templates include a leave section to adapt. State paid leave programs have their own posters, and several also require a written notice to each employee, so check the program's employer page.
  • Respond on time. Under DOL Fact Sheet 28D, give the eligibility notice and the rights and responsibilities notice within five business days of the request, and a written designation notice within five business days of having enough information to decide. If you ask for medical certification, allow at least 15 calendar days for it.
  • Keep health insurance going on the same terms during FMLA leave. Decide in advance how the employee pays their share: payroll deduction before leave, monthly payments during leave, or catch-up on return.
  • Restore the job. Same position or a virtually identical one, including shift, pay, location and benefits. Filling the role permanently because the replacement is "working out" is the classic violation.
  • Accommodate, do not push out. At 15 or more employees, the PWFA requires accommodations such as extra breaks, seating, schedule changes or time off for appointments, and bars forcing an employee onto leave when another accommodation works. The PDA bars treating pregnancy worse than other temporary conditions.
  • Provide pumping breaks and a private space for a year after the birth under the PUMP Act, at any size unless you can show undue hardship with fewer than 50 employees.
  • Handle state contributions. If you have employees in any of the 12 states or DC above, register with the program, withhold the employee share where allowed, remit the employer share where required and respond to wage verification requests promptly. Payroll software with state leave deductions built in removes most of this work.
  • Keep the records. FMLA records (dates, hours, notices, certifications) must be kept for three years, and accurate time records are what prove or disprove the 1,250-hour test. A time and attendance system that stores punches makes that trivial.

Small employers not covered by FMLA are not off the hook. Check the state table above for lower thresholds, remember that state paid leave programs usually apply at any size, and write a parental leave policy anyway: how many weeks, whether PTO can be used, whether the job is guaranteed, and how both parents are treated. A written policy applied consistently is also the best protection against a discrimination claim. Store it where employees can find it, next to the bereavement leave and jury duty policies, in a shared HR documents folder or the employee app.

Then the staffing side: block the leave on the schedule as soon as it is requested, decide whether to cross-train, hire temporary cover or redistribute shifts, and track leave weeks in the same place as PTO so the balances, the schedule and payroll agree. Managers who can see who is out before they approve another request avoid the week where three people are off at once.

Frequently asked questions

How long is maternity leave in the US?

There is no single answer. Federal law (FMLA) protects up to 12 weeks of unpaid leave for eligible employees at employers with 50 or more employees. State paid family leave programs add 8 to 12 weeks of partly paid bonding leave in 12 states and DC, and several states pay disability benefits for the weeks around the birth. Most other leave depends on your employer's policy.

Is maternity leave paid in the United States?

Not by federal law for private-sector employees. FMLA leave is unpaid. Pay comes from a state paid family leave or disability program if you work in one of the states that has one, from your employer's own parental leave or short-term disability policy, or from PTO and sick time you have saved up. Federal employees get 12 weeks of paid parental leave.

Can I take maternity leave if my employer has fewer than 50 employees?

FMLA does not cover you, but other laws may. The Pregnancy Discrimination Act and the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act apply at 15 or more employees. Several states give job-protected pregnancy or parental leave at much lower thresholds, and most state paid leave programs cover every employer. Otherwise your leave comes from company policy, so ask early and get the agreement in writing.

Does maternity leave start before the baby is born?

It can. FMLA leave is available before the birth for prenatal care or when pregnancy makes you unable to work, and state disability programs in California, New Jersey and New York typically pay for about four weeks before the due date. Any leave taken before the birth counts against your 12 FMLA weeks, so many parents work as long as their doctor allows.

Can both parents take FMLA leave for the same baby?

Yes, each eligible parent has their own 12 weeks. The exception is spouses who work for the same employer: the employer may limit them to a combined 12 weeks for birth and bonding. Leave for the mother's own recovery is not part of that cap, and unmarried parents who share an employer each keep their full 12 weeks.

Does my employer have to keep my health insurance during maternity leave?

During FMLA leave, yes. The employer must continue group health coverage on the same terms as if you were working, and you keep paying your usual share of the premium. Several state leave laws require the same. Outside those laws, continuation depends on your plan and policy, so confirm how you will pay premiums while you are not receiving a paycheck.
