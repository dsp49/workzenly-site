Quick answer

  • Employee onboarding is everything you do to get a new hire legal, paid, trained and productive, from the signed offer through about the first 90 days.
  • Run it in four phases: preboarding (paperwork and setup), the first day, the first week (training and shadow shifts), and 30/60/90-day check-ins.
  • Required orientation, training and shadow shifts are paid hours worked for non-exempt employees under federal law, and they count toward overtime.

Employee onboarding is the process of turning a person who accepted your offer into a teammate who can work a shift or run a project on their own. It starts before the first day with paperwork and setup, and it runs through roughly the first 90 days with training, a buddy and regular check-ins. This guide walks through each phase for two common workplaces, a coffee shop or clinic with hourly shift staff and a small office, then covers the legal pieces employers get wrong most often: paying for training time and finishing federal forms on schedule.

What employee onboarding is, and how it differs from orientation

People use the words interchangeably, but they are different sizes.

  • Orientation is an event. It usually happens on day one and covers the basics: a tour, safety rules, where to park, how to clock in, and any forms still open. It might take two hours.
  • Onboarding is a process. It starts when the offer is signed and includes orientation, job training, a buddy, scheduled check-ins with the manager and a first review around day 90.

A business can run a good orientation and still have weak onboarding. The new barista knows where the fire extinguisher is, but nobody shows them how to close the register, and by week three they are guessing. Onboarding new employees well means planning the weeks after orientation as carefully as the first morning.

A good onboarding process has three jobs: get the person set up legally and in payroll, teach them the job to a clear standard, and connect them with the people they work with. Each phase below serves one or more of those jobs.

The onboarding process, phase by phase

The steps are the same for every business. What changes is the detail. Here is what each phase looks like in a shift-based workplace and in an office.

Phase 1: Preboarding (offer accepted to day one)

Preboarding is the time between the signed offer and the first shift. Use it to clear paperwork so day one is about the job.

  • Send a welcome message with the start date, start time, address, where to park or enter, what to wear and who to ask for.
  • Collect paperwork electronically: signed offer letter, W-4, Section 1 of Form I-9, direct deposit details, emergency contact and a signed handbook acknowledgment.
  • Hourly or shift workplace: put their first two weeks of shifts on the schedule, paired with their buddy’s shifts. Order uniforms or name badges. Add them to the time clock so they can punch in on day one.
  • Office: order the laptop, create email and system accounts, set up their desk or remote equipment, and book their first-week meetings.
  • Pick a buddy and tell the buddy what you expect from them (more on that below).

Phase 2: The first day

Keep it short, specific and human. A new hire should leave knowing exactly what they did right and when they work next.

  • Coffee shop or clinic: a shorter first shift, often four hours. Meet the manager and buddy, tour the front and back, review safety and hygiene rules for the role, clock in, then shadow the buddy on the floor. At a clinic, add privacy and infection-control training required for the role before any patient contact.
  • Office: a welcome meeting with the manager, laptop and account setup, team introductions, lunch with the buddy, and one small real task they can finish by the end of the day.
  • Both: the employer examines I-9 documents, and the day ends with a five-minute check-in: what went well, what was confusing, when is the next shift or meeting.

Phase 3: The first week

Week one is for structured training. Write down the tasks the person should be able to do by Friday and check them off together.

  • Shift workplace: two or three shadow shifts, then shifts where the new hire does the work while the buddy watches. Cover opening and closing duties, the register or check-in system, break rules, how to request a shift swap and how to see their pay.
  • Office: walk through the tools, the team’s current projects and how work gets assigned. Give a first assignment with a real deadline in week two.
  • Make sure they have read the handbook sections that matter most in the first week: attendance, breaks, time off and conduct.

Phase 4: The first 30, 60 and 90 days

After week one, onboarding shifts from training to independence. Schedule three manager check-ins now, before the calendar fills up: day 30, day 60 and day 90. Required new-hire paperwork, in summary

Every US employer has a short list of federal paperwork for each new employee, plus state forms. The main items are:

  • Form W-4 for federal income tax withholding. Our W-4 form guide explains each step.
  • Form I-9 to verify identity and work authorization. USCIS sets firm deadlines: the employee completes Section 1 no later than the first day of work, and the employer completes Section 2 within three business days of the start date. See the I-9 form guide for document lists and E-Verify.
  • New hire report to your state’s new hire directory. Federal law sets 20 days from hire as the outer limit, and some states require it sooner.
  • State withholding form, direct deposit authorization, any state-required notices, and your signed handbook acknowledgment.

We cover the full list, including state notices, in our new hire paperwork guide. If your handbook is out of date or missing, start with our employee handbook template.

Training, shadow shifts and sign-offs

Most onboarding problems in hourly workplaces come down to training that lives in one experienced person’s head. Writing it down once fixes most of it.

  1. List the tasks. For a barista: open the store, pull a shot to standard, run the register, handle a refund, clean the machine, close. For a medical assistant: room a patient, take vitals, chart, stock rooms, follow the clinic’s privacy rules.
  2. Teach in three steps: watch (the buddy does it and explains), try (the new hire does it with the buddy beside them), solo (the new hire does it and the buddy checks afterward).
  3. Sign off each task. The manager or buddy initials the task when the new hire meets the standard. This tells everyone when the person can work without a buddy on shift.
  4. Schedule overlap on purpose. Put the new hire and the buddy on the same shifts for the first one to two weeks. If the schedule does not show it, it will not happen.

For office roles, the same idea applies with different tasks: the systems they must use, the reports they produce, the people they need to know. A short written list with sign-offs works better than a long slide deck.

Paying for training time: the federal rules

This is where onboarding becomes a payroll question. Under the Fair Labor Standards Act, the US Department of Labor’s rules on training time are in 29 CFR 785.27 to 785.32. Time at lectures, meetings and training programs is not hours worked only if all four of these are true:

  1. It is outside the employee’s regular working hours.
  2. Attendance is in fact voluntary.
  3. It is not directly related to the employee’s job.
  4. The employee does no productive work while attending.

New-hire training almost never passes that test. The regulations say attendance is not voluntary if the employer requires it, or if the employee is led to believe their job or working conditions would suffer if they skipped it. Training is directly related to the job when it is designed to help the employee do their current job better, and time in that kind of course given by the employer is hours worked.

In practice, for non-exempt employees:

  • Orientation, required training, safety courses and online modules are paid, including modules done at home if you assign them.
  • Shadow shifts are paid. Watching a coworker work, at your direction, is training directly related to the job.
  • Unpaid “trial shifts” are risky. If a candidate makes drinks or serves customers, that is work, and an employee who works must be paid at least minimum wage for it.
  • Training hours count toward overtime. A new hire who works 36 hours on the floor and spends 6 hours in required training has worked 42 hours that week.
  • Pay at least minimum wage. You can set a separate training rate if the offer letter says so, but it cannot fall below the federal, state or local minimum wage, whichever is highest. Our minimum wage by state guide lists current rates.

The rules do carve out narrow exceptions: voluntary courses an employee takes on their own at an independent school after hours, certain voluntary employer programs that match courses at a real school, and some related instruction in written apprenticeship programs. These rarely apply to onboarding. State law can add requirements, so check your state labor department.

The overtime math matters here. At $18 an hour, the 42-hour week above is 40 × $18 + 2 × $27 = $774. If the training hours are left off the timesheet, the employer pays $648 for 36 hours and owes the missing $126, which is the 6 training hours including the overtime premium. Sample 30-60-90 day onboarding plan

Copy this plan, replace the [bracketed] items and adjust the goals to the role. It is written for a shift-based job, with notes for office roles. Share it with the new hire on day one so they know what “doing well” means.

30-60-90 day onboarding plan
30-60-90 DAY ONBOARDING PLAN Employee: [Name] Role: [Job title] Start date: [Month day, year] Manager: [Name] Buddy: [Name] HR contact: [Name, email or phone] BEFORE DAY ONE (manager and HR) [ ] Offer letter signed [ ] W-4, state withholding form and direct deposit completed [ ] Form I-9 Section 1 completed; documents to bring on day one explained [ ] Handbook read and acknowledgment signed [ ] First two weeks of shifts scheduled with buddy on the same shifts [ ] Time clock access, uniform or badge ready (office: laptop and accounts ready) FIRST WEEK Goals: - Complete orientation, safety training and any role-required training (paid time) - Shadow [number] shifts with [buddy name] - Learn to clock in and out, take breaks, view the schedule and request a swap or time off Check-in: end of day one and end of week one (10 minutes each) FIRST 30 DAYS: LEARN THE JOB Goals: - Signed off on these core tasks: [task 1], [task 2], [task 3], [task 4] - Works a full shift without a buddy by day [number] - Knows who to call for [problem 1] and [problem 2] Office role: complete first assignment, meet each key coworker one on one Day 30 check-in (manager, 20 minutes): - What is going well? What is still confusing? - Tasks signed off so far / tasks still in training - Schedule and hours working for you? DAYS 31 TO 60: WORK INDEPENDENTLY Goals: - Signed off on remaining tasks: [task 5], [task 6], [opening or closing duties] - Meets the standard for [speed, accuracy or quality measure the role uses] - Can show a newer coworker how to do [one task] Office role: owns [recurring task or project] with light review Day 60 check-in (manager, 20 minutes): - Progress against the goals above - One thing to improve in the next 30 days - Any training, equipment or schedule changes needed DAYS 61 TO 90: CONTRIBUTE FULLY Goals: - Works any shift on the schedule without extra support - [Role-specific goal, e.g. trained on inventory, cross-trained on a second station] - Suggests one improvement to how we train new hires Day 90 review (manager, 30 minutes): - Written review of the first 90 days - Goals for the next six months - Feedback from the employee on their onboarding Signatures Employee: ____________ Date: ______ Manager: _____________ Date: ______

Keep the goals measurable. “Learn the register” is hard to check. “Rings up, voids and refunds a sale without help” is easy to check. If you hire for the same role often, keep a finished version of this plan for each role so the next manager does not start from a blank page. Who does what: manager, buddy and HR

RoleMain job in onboardingTypical tasks
Hiring managerOwns the plan and the outcomeSets 30/60/90 goals, schedules training shifts, runs check-ins, signs off tasks, writes the day 90 review
BuddyDay-to-day guide and first person to askWorks the same shifts in the first weeks, demonstrates tasks, answers “where is” and “how do I” questions, reports progress to the manager
HR or ownerKeeps it legal and on timeSends paperwork, completes I-9 Section 2, files the new hire report, adds the person to payroll, keeps records

In a small business, one person may wear two of these hats. That is fine, but the buddy should be someone other than the manager. New hires ask a peer questions they would not ask their boss. Pick a buddy who does the job well, give them time on the schedule to train, and thank them for it.

How to measure your onboarding process

You do not need industry benchmarks. Your own numbers, tracked over a year, tell you whether onboarding is working. Start with these:

  • 90-day retention: of the people who started in a period, how many are still employed 90 days later. Track it by role and location.
  • Time to solo: the number of days until a new hire works a full shift without a buddy, or completes their first assignment unaided.
  • Paperwork on time: whether every I-9 Section 2 was completed within three business days and every new hire was on the first payroll with the right withholding.
  • Check-ins held: whether day 30, 60 and 90 conversations actually happened.
  • New-hire feedback: two questions at day 30 and day 90: “What helped most?” and “What should we change?”

If 90-day retention drops at one location, look at what that location does differently in the first two weeks before you look anywhere else.

Common onboarding mistakes and best practices

These employee onboarding best practices come from fixing the mistakes that show up most often in small businesses:

  • Paperwork on day one. A new hire spending their first two hours on forms learns that your business is about forms. Move everything you legally can to preboarding.
  • No schedule for the first two weeks. Hourly new hires who do not know when they work next often do not come back. Publish their shifts before day one.
  • Unpaid training or trial shifts. This creates back-pay liability and a bad first impression. Pay for every hour of required training.
  • Training by whoever is on shift. Different coworkers teach different ways. Use one buddy and one written task list.
  • Information overload in week one. Teach what they need for the next shift, not everything at once.
  • Check-ins that never happen. Put day 30, 60 and 90 on the calendar during preboarding.
  • Missing the I-9 deadline. The three-business-day clock for Section 2 starts on the first day of paid work, not when someone gets around to it.

Remote and hybrid onboarding

Remote and hybrid employees need the same phases, with more planning, because nobody bumps into them in the break room.

  • Ship equipment early so the laptop arrives before day one, and test the login together on a short call before the start date.
  • Plan the I-9 document review. Section 2 still needs a document examination. USCIS allows a remote examination process for employers enrolled in E-Verify in good standing; otherwise use an in-person review, which can be done by an authorized representative. Check the current USCIS rules before choosing.
  • Over-schedule the first week: daily 15-minute calls with the manager, a daily check-in with the buddy, and video introductions to each coworker they will work with.
  • Write down the unwritten rules: core hours, expected response times, which channel to use for what, and how to record time if they are non-exempt.
  • For hybrid roles, put the first in-office days on the calendar early and use them for introductions and hands-on training rather than solo work.

Frequently asked questions

How long should employee onboarding last?

Plan for about 90 days. Paperwork and setup happen before day one, the first week covers training and shadow shifts, and the rest of the first three months is for building independence with regular check-ins. Simple hourly roles may be fully up to speed sooner, but keep the check-ins going to day 90.

What is the difference between onboarding and orientation?

Orientation is a one-time event, usually on the first day, that covers the company, the building, safety and paperwork. Onboarding is the whole process around it, from the signed offer through the first 90 days, including training, a buddy, check-ins and a first review. Orientation is one step inside onboarding.

Do I have to pay new employees for training?

Almost always, for non-exempt employees. Under federal rules, training time is unpaid only if it is outside normal hours, truly voluntary, not directly related to the job and involves no productive work. Required new-hire training, orientation and shadow shifts fail that test, so they are paid hours worked and count toward overtime.

Can a new hire do paperwork before their first day?

Yes, once they have accepted the job offer. A new hire can sign the offer letter, complete a W-4, set up direct deposit, read the handbook and fill in Section 1 of Form I-9 before day one. The employer still has to examine I-9 identity documents and complete Section 2 within the USCIS deadline.

What should a new employee do on the first day?

Keep the first day short and concrete. Meet their manager and buddy, finish any remaining paperwork, take a tour, learn how to clock in and see the schedule, get safety training for the role, and do a short real task or shadow a shift. End with a five-minute check-in and confirm their next shift.
