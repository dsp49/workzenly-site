How we chose these tools

We started from the products that show up most often when US buyers search for the best time and attendance software, then cut the list to tools that sell to US small and mid-sized businesses and publish enough on their own sites to check. Our criteria

  • Clock-in options: phone app, web, and a shared tablet kiosk for crews without company phones.
  • Punch verification: GPS stamps, geofencing, photo or face checks, PIN or QR codes to stop buddy punching.
  • Pay rules: weekly overtime at minimum, plus daily overtime, break tracking and rounding for states that need them.
  • Path to payroll: built-in payroll, a direct sync, or a clean export to the payroll provider you already use.
  • Transparent pricing: a published price, a real free plan or trial, and clear limits on what each tier includes.
  • Fit for hourly work: scheduling, time off and manager approvals close to the clock, not in a separate tool.

We left out enterprise suites that only sell through a sales quote, and pure productivity monitors that screenshot desktops, because they answer a different question. Ranking reflects fit for a typical US small business with hourly staff, not the size of the vendor.

How to choose time and attendance software

The best time and attendance software for you depends on your crew, your state and your payroll setup. A time and attendance system is part of your payroll records, so pick it the way you would pick a payroll provider: on accuracy and compliance first, convenience second. Work through this checklist with your own numbers before you start any free trial.

1. Match the clock to how your people work

Count how many employees work at a fixed site, how many move between job sites, and how many share a single device. A store or kitchen usually wants a wall-mounted tablet kiosk with a PIN or photo. Crews on the road need a phone app with GPS stamps and geofencing around each job site. If you have both, make sure one plan covers both without a second fee. Our roundup of GPS time clock apps goes deeper on location tracking.

2. Check overtime and break rules for your state

Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, non-exempt employees earn 1.5 times their regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek. Several states go further. California, for example, also requires daily overtime after 8 hours and double time after 12 hours in a workday, plus meal and rest breaks; our guide to California overtime law covers the details. The software should calculate the rules where you operate, flag missed breaks and alert managers before a shift tips into overtime. Test it: enter a 46-hour week and a 10-hour day and check the result against our overtime calculation guide.

3. Look at predictive scheduling if you run retail or food service

Oregon and cities including New York City, Chicago, Seattle, San Francisco, Los Angeles and Philadelphia have fair workweek or predictive scheduling laws for some retail, food service and hospitality employers. These rules typically require advance notice of schedules and premium pay for last-minute changes. If they apply to you, choose a system that ties the time clock to the schedule and records changes, as Deputy and the scheduling-first apps do.

4. Keep the records the law requires

The US Department of Labor requires employers to record the hours each non-exempt employee works each day and each workweek, keep payroll records for at least three years, and keep time cards for two years. Confirm the system keeps an edit history on timesheets, lets you export several years of data, and does not delete records when an employee leaves. If you round punches, set the rule so it averages out neutrally and never rounds only in the employer’s favor.

5. Get consent before using biometrics or location

Face recognition and fingerprint clocks collect biometric data. Illinois’s Biometric Information Privacy Act requires written notice and a signed release before collection, and other states and cities have their own rules. Put location and biometric tracking in your written attendance policy, tell employees when the app records their location, and prefer tools that stop GPS when the employee clocks out.

6. Price the full bill, not the headline

Add the base fee, the per-user or per-location fee, any add-ons you need (payroll, scheduling, real-time GPS, biometric kiosk) and any monthly minimum. Then compare that total for your real headcount. A $2.50 per-user plan can cost more than a $30 per-location plan once you have 15 people at one store.

7. Trial it with one real pay period

Run the trial alongside your current method for a full pay period. Have managers fix a missed punch, approve timesheets and send them to payroll, and have two employees clock in from a phone and a kiosk. If you track hours on paper today, our free timesheet templates and time card calculator make a good benchmark to compare against.

Frequently asked questions

What is time and attendance software?

Time and attendance software records when employees start and stop work, usually through a phone app, a web clock or a shared tablet kiosk. It totals hours, tracks breaks and overtime, flags missed punches and sends approved timesheets to payroll. Most products also handle schedules and time off, so managers can compare planned shifts with the hours actually worked.

Is there free time and attendance software?

Yes. Jibble has a free plan for unlimited users, Homebase has a free Basic plan for one location with up to 10 employees, and Connecteam is free for up to 10 users. Clockify is free for a handful of users but leaves the kiosk and attendance report out of its free plan. Every free plan limits something, so check the exclusions.

How much does time and attendance software cost?

Published prices on November 7, 2026 ran from about $2.50 to $13 per user per month, and some vendors add a monthly base fee. OnTheClock charges $5 a month plus $4 per employee, while Homebase charges per location instead of per user. Payroll, real-time GPS and biometric kiosks are often paid add-ons or sit in higher tiers.

How long do employers have to keep time records?

Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, employers must keep payroll records for at least three years and time cards for two years, according to the US Department of Labor. Records for non-exempt employees must show the hours worked each day and the total hours in each workweek. Some states require more, so check your state labor department.

Can a time clock app track where employees are?

Most apps on this list can stamp a GPS location on each clock-in and clock-out, and many support geofences that block or flag punches made away from the job site. QuickBooks Time and OnTheClock both say they collect location only while the employee is on the clock. Whatever you choose, tell employees in writing what the app records.

Is facial recognition at a time clock legal?

It is allowed in most states, but some states regulate biometric data. Illinois’s Biometric Information Privacy Act, for example, requires written notice and a signed release before an employer collects face or fingerprint data. If you turn on a face-verification kiosk, get written consent, publish a retention policy and check your state’s rules first.
